TASK 1: Study Source A

Source A: Climate graphs for McMurdo Station and Vostok Station in Antarctica

Source A: Climate graphs for McMurdo and Vostok in Antarctica
TIP: When comparing two climate graphs, remember to read the scales carefully. The axes differ significantly between sites (e.g. precipitation scale for McMurdo reaches 30 mm, whereas Vostok's scale reaches only 0.8 mm).
  1. a. Identify the coldest temperature recorded in the two graphs.
  2. b. Which site has the lowest rate of precipitation?
  3. c. Calculate the difference between the highest and lowest temperature for McMurdo.
  4. d. Compare the climate graph for McMurdo Station with the one for Vostok. Use data to support your answer.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Coldest temperature recorded:

  • Vostok Station: Approximately -68°C to -70°C recorded during the winter month of August. (In comparison, McMurdo's lowest temperature in August is -26°C to -27°C).

b. Site with the lowest rate of precipitation:

  • Vostok Station has by far the lowest precipitation rate.
  • Evidence from Source A: Vostok receives between 0.1 mm and 0.7 mm per month, totaling less than 5 mm of precipitation for the entire year (qualifying it as an extreme hyper-arid polar desert). In contrast, McMurdo receives between 10 mm and 25 mm each month.

c. Temperature difference (annual range) for McMurdo:

  • Highest temperature (January): -3°C
  • Lowest temperature (August): -26°C (accept -26°C to -27°C)
  • Calculation: $-3^\circ ext{C} - (-26^\circ ext{C}) =$ 23°C (accept 23°C – 24°C).

d. Comparison between McMurdo and Vostok:

  • Temperature comparison: McMurdo is substantially warmer year-round than Vostok. McMurdo's summer peak reaches -3°C (near freezing) in January, whereas Vostok's summer peak is much colder at -32°C to -35°C in December. In winter, McMurdo drops to -26°C, whereas Vostok plummets to an extreme -68°C to -70°C. McMurdo has a smaller annual temperature range (23°C) compared to Vostok's larger range (~36°C).
  • Precipitation comparison: McMurdo receives vastly higher precipitation (monthly totals 10–25 mm; annual total ~200 mm) than Vostok (monthly totals 0.1–0.7 mm; annual total under 5 mm). Note that the precipitation scale on McMurdo's graph is 35–40 times larger than Vostok's scale.
  • Geographical explanation: McMurdo is a coastal station located on Ross Island at sea level, benefiting from the moderating maritime influence of the Southern Ocean. Vostok is situated in the high continental interior on the East Antarctic Ice Sheet at an altitude of approximately 3,488 metres, where extreme elevation, distance from oceanic moisture (continentality), and katabatic high pressure produce hyper-arid, hyper-cold conditions.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. How key geographical factors influence Antarctic climate:

  • i. Angle of the Earth towards the sun: Because Antarctica is situated around 90°S latitude, incoming solar insolation strikes the Earth's curved surface at a very low, acute/oblique angle. Low solar angles mean sunlight delivers much less concentrated energy per square metre compared to vertical rays at the equator.
  • ii. Distance through the atmosphere: At oblique angles, solar rays must travel through a much thicker layer of the atmosphere before reaching the surface. This longer atmospheric pathway causes substantial loss of solar energy due to scattering, reflection, and absorption by gases and aerosols.
  • iii. Area that rays must warm: Because rays arrive obliquely, a given beam of solar energy is dispersed over a far larger surface area than at low latitudes. Furthermore, the white snow and ice surface has a very high albedo (reflecting up to 80–90% of incoming radiation back into space), preventing ground heating.
  • iv. High-pressure atmospheric system: The extreme cold causes air over the interior ice sheet to contract, become dense, and sink, forming a permanent polar anticyclone (high-pressure system). Sinking air warms slightly, suppresses convection, and prevents cloud formation, causing hyper-arid desert conditions and allowing unrestricted radiational heat loss into space.
  • v. Long dark winters: Due to Earth's axial tilt of 23.5°, the South Pole tilts entirely away from the sun during winter (March to September). Antarctica experiences months of continuous 24-hour darkness (polar night), meaning zero incoming solar radiation while continuous terrestrial radiation cools the surface down to -70°C or colder.

b. Model Summary: Why Antarctica's climate is so extreme:

Antarctica is the coldest, driest, and windiest continent on Earth due to an extreme combination of astronomical, atmospheric, and surface factors. Astronomically, its high latitude (66.5°S to 90°S) and the Earth's 23.5° axial tilt mean solar rays strike at an oblique angle, traveling through a thick atmospheric column and spreading weak insolation over a vast area. For six months during polar winter, the region is plunged into complete darkness, resulting in unrelenting heat loss.

Atmospherically, dense, chilling air sinks over the interior plateau, maintaining a permanent high-pressure system that prevents cloud formation and limits precipitation to less than 5 mm/year in inland stations like Vostok (shown in Source A). This classifies Antarctica as a hyper-arid cold desert. When cold, heavy air cascades from the high central plateau (over 3,000 m elevation) down toward coastal low-pressure zones, ferocious katabatic winds develop. Finally, the massive ice sheet creates a powerful positive feedback loop through high albedo, reflecting up to 90% of summer solar radiation, while coastal stations like McMurdo experience slightly moderated temperatures (-3°C to -26°C) due to proximity to the Southern Ocean.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. How the Antarctic environment has protected native species:

  • Geographic Isolation: Antarctica is completely surrounded by the stormy, ice-choked Southern Ocean and the Antarctic Circumpolar Current. This formidable oceanic barrier historically prevented colonization by terrestrial mammalian predators (e.g. foxes, wolves, rodents) and invasive species, allowing ground-nesting seabirds and seals to thrive without fear of land-based predation.
  • Harsh Inhospitable Climate: The sub-zero temperatures, treacherous sea ice, and fierce katabatic blizzards made permanent human settlement impossible. As a result, Antarctica never experienced large-scale deforestation, agricultural clearing, industrial pollution, or urbanization.
  • Absence of Permanent Human Populations: Wildlife habitats remained virtually untouched until modern exploratory times. Furthermore, the 1959 Antarctic Treaty and its Environmental Protocol (Madrid Protocol) legally designate the entire continent as a natural reserve devoted to peace and science, banning military activity, mining, and nuclear testing.

b. Model Case Study & Spider Diagram: Adaptations of the Emperor Penguin (Aptenodytes forsteri):

Key Adaptations for Survival in Extreme Cold:
  • Dense Plumage & Waterproofing (Anatomical): Possesses the highest feather density of any bird (up to 100 feathers per square inch). Overlapping, scale-like feathers form a windproof, waterproof outer shell lubricated by gland oils, trapping a thick insulating boundary layer of warm air underneath.
  • Subcutaneous Blubber Layer (Physiological): A dense layer of fat up to 3–4 cm thick beneath the skin provides vital thermal insulation and acts as an energy reserve during months of winter fasting.
  • Counter-Current Heat Exchange (Physiological): Blood vessels in the extremities (flippers, nasal passages, and feet) run closely parallel. Warm arterial blood from the core warms cold venous blood returning from extremities, drastically reducing heat loss through feet resting on ice.
  • Social Huddling Behavior (Behavioral): Male penguins form tightly packed huddles of thousands of individuals during the freezing winter incubation period. Penguins continually rotate from the icy outer edge to the warm interior, where temperatures can reach 20°C to 37°C, reducing individual heat loss by up to 50%.
  • Low Surface Area to Volume Ratio (Morphological): Large body mass (up to 40 kg) with compact bill and small flippers minimizes exposed surface area relative to body volume, reducing conductive heat loss.
  • Winter Breeding Strategy (Phenological): Breeds on fast sea ice during mid-winter so that chicks fledge during mid-summer (December/January) when open ocean food (krill, fish, squid) is at maximum seasonal abundance.
