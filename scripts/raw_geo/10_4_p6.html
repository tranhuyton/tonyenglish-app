a) Comparison Table:

TimeMillions of years (typically 100–300+ million years ago).Around 300 million years (Carboniferous period).
Original organic materialMicroscopic marine organisms (plankton, microscopic plants and animals) living in oceans.Terrestrial land vegetation, giant ferns, and swamp trees.

How it formsDead sea organisms sank to the ocean floor, were covered by thick layers of silt/mud (sedimentary rock). Key Similarities & Differences:
• Similarities: Both are non-renewable fossil fuels formed over hundreds of millions of years from decomposed living organisms subjected to extreme heat and tectonic pressure under thick sediment layers.
• Differences: Oil and gas originate from marine plankton in oceans and form fluid hydrocarbons (liquids/gases), whereas coal originates from terrestrial vegetation in freshwater swamps and forms a solid carbon-rich sedimentary rock.

b) Why nuclear energy is considered more sustainable:
• Massive Energy Density: Nuclear fuel has an extraordinarily high energy density compared to fossil fuels. A tiny uranium fuel pellet (about the size of a fingertip) generates the same amount of electricity as burning roughly 1 tonne of coal or 150 gallons of oil.
• Zero Carbon Emissions During Operation: Unlike coal and gas, nuclear fission does not involve combustion, so it releases zero greenhouse gases (CO₂) and zero sulfur dioxide or nitrogen oxides that trigger acid rain and air pollution.
• Longevity of Fuel Reserves: The textbook notes that accessible uranium supplies will last an estimated 230 years at current consumption rates—substantially longer than readily accessible crude oil and gas reserves—and advanced fuel-recycling can extend this even further.
• Continuous Baseload Reliability: Nuclear plants operate continuously 24/7 with very high capacity factors (>90%), reliably powering grid networks without depleting precious organic hydrocarbons needed for petrochemicals and aviation.

  1. Describe the similarities between generating tidal and wind energy.
  2. Solar power does not require turbines to generate electricity. Discuss how you think this makes solar energy more suitable for remote locations.
  3. With reference to Source F, describe how tidal energy can generate electricity.
  4. Explain why we can consider fuelwood both a renewable and non-renewable energy source.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Suggested Answers:

  • a) Similarities between generating tidal and wind energy:
    • Kinetic Fluid Energy: Both technologies generate power by harnessing the natural kinetic energy of a moving fluid (air currents for wind energy, seawater currents for tidal energy).
    • Turbine Rotation: Both rely on large rotating blades (turbines) connected to a generator that converts mechanical rotation into electrical energy.
    • Renewable & Zero Emissions: Both are 100% renewable resources that will not run out, producing zero greenhouse gas emissions during electrical generation.
    • Location-Specific: Both require specific geographic conditions to be effective (exposed coastal, offshore, or high-altitude areas with high wind speeds for wind power; narrow estuaries, bays, or channels with substantial tidal ranges and strong currents for tidal power).
  • b) Why solar power (no turbines) is more suitable for remote locations:
    • No Moving Parts (Photovoltaic): Solar panels generate electricity directly via the photovoltaic effect. Because there are no mechanical moving parts (unlike spinning turbines with gears and bearings), there is virtually zero friction, minimal mechanical failure, and little need for regular maintenance, lubrication, or spare replacement parts in hard-to-reach locations.
    • Modularity & Transportability: Solar PV systems are modular and lightweight. Individual panels can be carried by light vehicles, pack animals, or small boats to remote mountain villages, islands, or desert outposts where transporting huge turbine blades, generators, and heavy cranes is impossible.
    • Decentralized Off-Grid Operation: Solar setups can easily power small standalone systems (micro-grids) paired with local batteries, providing clean power to isolated homes, health clinics, and schools without requiring expensive connection to national electricity grids.
  • c) How tidal energy generates electricity (with reference to Source F):
    • A permanent barrage (barrier structure) is constructed across an estuary or bay, creating an enclosed tidal basin separated from the open ocean.
    • When the tide rises (flood tide), sluice gates in the barrage open, allowing seawater to flow into and fill the tidal basin.
    • At high tide, the sluice gates close, trapping water in the basin. As the ocean tide recedes (ebb tide), a significant difference in water level (head of water) develops between the high water in the basin and the lower ocean outside.
    • The trapped water is then released through submerged tunnels, where the powerful outgoing current forces underwater turbines to spin rapidly.
    • The spinning turbines drive internal electrical generators, and the generated electricity is transmitted via power cables and pylons to the mainland electrical grid. (Modern reversible turbines can also generate power as water rushes in during the incoming flood tide).
  • d) Why fuelwood can be considered both a renewable and non-renewable energy source:
    • Why it is Renewable: Wood comes from living trees and plants that naturally reproduce, grow, and can be replanted. If forests are harvested sustainably—meaning the rate of cutting is equal to or less than the rate of tree replanting and regrowth (sustainable silviculture)—fuelwood will continue to be replenished indefinitely, making it a sustainable, renewable biomass resource.
    • Why it can become Non-Renewable: When harvesting occurs at an unsustainable rate (deforestation driven by extreme fuel poverty, population growth, or commercial overexploitation), trees are consumed faster than they can regenerate. Over-cutting leads to clear-felled landscapes, topsoil erosion, desertification, and loss of seed banks. In such degraded environments, the forest ecosystem collapses and cannot recover on a human timescale, rendering fuelwood functionally finite and non-renewable for that region.
