TASK 1
  1. a. Explain the difference between development and sustainable development.
  2. b. Suggest how improving gender equality (SDG 5) can help close the development gap.
  3. c. Explain the differences between top-down and bottom-up development strategies.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Difference between development and sustainable development:

  • Development: General progress and improvement in people's quality of life, living standards, and economic wealth (GDP/GNI growth).
  • Sustainable development: Meeting the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It requires harmonising three core pillars: Economic growth, Social inclusion/equity, and Environmental protection (preventing resource depletion and ecological damage).

b. How improving gender equality (SDG 5) helps close the development gap:

  • Productive workforce: Providing girls with equal access to education and formal employment effectively doubles the productive talent pool, expanding national economic output and tax revenues.
  • Healthier, smaller families: Educated women tend to marry later, have fewer children, and invest more in their children's nutrition and healthcare, lowering infant mortality and demographic dependency ratios.
  • Household investment: Studies show women reinvest up to 90% of their income back into family welfare, education, and health, creating a powerful multiplier effect that breaks generational cycles of poverty.

c. Differences between top-down and bottom-up development strategies:

  • Top-down strategies:
    • Large-scale, expensive, high-tech projects directed by national governments, international organisations (e.g. World Bank), or TNCs (e.g. Hydroelectric dams, international airports).
    • Advantages: Generate massive amounts of electricity or transport infrastructure vital for nationwide industrialisation.
    • Disadvantages: Local communities are rarely consulted; risks forced displacement, massive foreign debt, and environmental destruction; wealth often fails to trickle down to the poorest.
  • Bottom-up strategies:
    • Small-scale, community-led projects initiated by grassroots groups or NGOs (e.g. WaterAid, Fairtrade), utilizing appropriate, intermediate technology (e.g. Gravity-fed water schemes, rainwater harvesting, microloans).
    • Advantages: Directly targets basic local needs; highly sustainable and cheap to maintain; strong local ownership and empowerment.
    • Disadvantages: Small localized impact; unable to meet nationwide industrial energy demands or solve systemic macroeconomic challenges.
TASK 2

‘To close the development gap, aid is better than trade.’ In pairs, discuss this statement.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

Discussion points for both sides:

  • Arguments that Trade is better than Aid:
    • Trade promotes economic self-reliance, domestic entrepreneurship, and sustainable long-term jobs without creating foreign dependency.
    • Export revenue directly funds infrastructure and public services via national tax collection.
    • Aid can sometimes foster corruption, be tied to unfavorable donor political conditions, or distort local markets (e.g. Free food aid undercutting local farmers).
  • Arguments that Aid is better than Trade:
    • Emergency / Humanitarian aid is vital and irreplaceable in immediate crises (famines, floods, conflicts) where commercial trade cannot operate.
    • Targeted development aid funds essential public goods (primary schools, rural clinics, clean water infrastructure) that generate no immediate commercial profit for private traders.
    • Unfair global trade structures (HIC agricultural subsidies, tariffs against processed goods, volatile commodity prices) often keep LIC primary commodity producers trapped in poverty.
  • Conclusion: Trade is the ultimate driver of long-term economic prosperity, but fair trade terms combined with targeted, uncorrupted development aid are essential to build the initial human and physical infrastructure needed for LICs to trade effectively.
TASK 3: Study Source

Fairtrade accused of failing to deliver benefits to African farmworkers

Fairtrade is one of the world’s most trusted ethical schemes, with two million farmers and workers across 70 countries around the world. Fairtrade products contribute to the funding of schools, health clinics, sanitation, and other social projects in rural areas. Fairtrade sets social, economic, and environmental standards for the companies and farmers involved in the supply chain and helps farmers earn secure and sustainable livelihoods.

Sales of Fairtrade-certified products from Uganda and Ethiopia are not benefiting poor farmworkers as profits fail to trickle down to the workforce. A UK government-sponsored study, which investigated the production of flowers, coffee, and tea in Ethiopia and Uganda, found that ‘where Fairtrade flowers were grown, and where there were farmers' groups selling coffee and tea into Fairtrade certified markets, wages were very low.’ Researchers said they also found evidence of the widespread use of children being paid to work on farms growing produce for Britain’s leading ethical label. The research reported that many of the poorest farmers did not have access to facilities. In one Fairtrade tea co-operative, the modern toilets funded with the Fairtrade premium were exclusively for the use of senior managers. Fairtrade has not been an effective mechanism for improving the lives of the poorest rural people.

Fairtrade International said the report’s conclusions were unfair and generalised because it compares the wages, and working conditions of workers in areas where small-scale Fairtrade-certified tea and coffee farmers were present with those on large-scale plantations.

Source: Fairtrade International article. Source: The Guardian

  1. a. Outline the aims of Fairtrade.
  2. b. Explain three benefits to farmers of having a more reliable income through Fairtrade.
  3. c. Explain, according to the report, three criticisms of Fairtrade in Uganda and Ethiopia.
  4. d. Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of bottom-up development strategies provided by NGOs such as Fairtrade International.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Aims of Fairtrade:

  • Guarantee a stable minimum price to farmers covering sustainable cost of production, shielding them from global commodity price crashes.
  • Provide an additional social dividend (Fairtrade Premium) to invest in community facilities (schools, health clinics, clean water).
  • Enforce social, economic, and environmental standards (fair working conditions, ban on exploitative child labor, responsible chemical usage).

b. Three benefits to farmers of a more reliable income:

  • Financial stability & farm investment: Farmers can budget for the future and reinvest in higher-quality seeds, fertilizers, and equipment to boost yields.
  • Improved household wellbeing: Families can reliably afford school fees, books, medicine, and nutritious food throughout off-peak seasons.
  • Community development: The collective premium funds communal wells, maternity clinics, and local transport infrastructure.

c. Three criticisms of Fairtrade in Uganda and Ethiopia:

  • Failure to trickle down: Wages for casual hired farmworkers remained extremely low, with financial benefits captured by farm owners/cooperative leaders.
  • Child labor: Evidence of widespread paid child labor on certified farms supplying Western supermarket brands.
  • Unequal access to facilities: Premium-funded amenities (e.g. modern flush toilets) were restricted exclusively for senior cooperative managers rather than the poorest fieldworkers.

d. Advantages and disadvantages of bottom-up NGO strategies:

  • Advantages: Targets the poorest grassroots producers directly; empowers smallholders with democratic decision-making; uses appropriate local technology; raises ethical consumer awareness in HICs.
  • Disadvantages: Small in scale and unable to solve nationwide poverty or heavy infrastructural deficits; susceptible to local corruption or elite capture; dependent on voluntary consumer willingness to pay higher retail prices in HICs; certification fees can exclude the poorest farmers.
