TASK 1

Study Source A: Global net zero prediction graph showing projected emission pathways by sector and gas between 2020 and 2100.

Source A: Global net zero prediction graph
  1. Describe the graph.
  2. What year are we currently expected to reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions?
  3. In 2030, what sector is predicted to contribute the greatest amount of emissions? What is the contributing greenhouse gas?
  4. Explain why low-income countries may be reluctant to reduce their greenhouse emissions.
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) Description of the graph:

  • Overall Trend: Total global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions peak around 2020 at roughly 53–55 GtCO₂ equivalent per year, followed by a steep, continuous decline over the 21st century.
  • Timeline to Net Zero: Net zero carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions is achieved first, around 2050–2055. Net zero across all greenhouse gases combined is reached later, around 2070.
  • Sectoral Differences: Emissions from the energy sector (Energy CO₂) undergo the most rapid reduction, plummeting from ~35 Gt in 2020 to near zero by 2050. In contrast, agricultural and land-use emissions (Land CH₄ and Land N₂O) decline at a much slower rate and remain positive throughout the entire century.
  • Negative Emissions: Beyond 2050, the curve drops below the zero line into negative emissions (reaching approximately −5 to −10 GtCO₂/year by 2080–2100), as nature-based solutions (afforestation) and negative emission technologies (direct air capture / BECCS) actively remove atmospheric carbon to offset residual non-CO₂ gases.

b) Year expected to reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions:

  • According to the graph, net zero greenhouse gas emissions is projected to be reached around 2070 (acceptable range: 2065 – 2075).
  • Note: Net zero CO₂ emissions alone is reached earlier, at approximately 2050.

c) Sector and greenhouse gas contributing greatest emissions in 2030:

  • Contributing Sector: The Energy sector (comprising power generation, transport, heating, and industrial fuel combustion).
  • Contributing Greenhouse Gas: Carbon dioxide (CO₂) (specifically Energy CO₂), accounting for over 30–35 GtCO₂/year of global emissions in 2030.

d) Why low-income countries (LICs) may be reluctant to reduce greenhouse emissions:

  • Economic growth and poverty alleviation: LICs prioritize lifting millions of citizens out of extreme poverty. Rapid industrialization, expanding domestic transport, and rural electrification require cheap, readily accessible, energy-dense power—traditionally supplied by domestic fossil fuels (coal, oil). Restricting these sources could stifle industrial growth.
  • High capital costs of clean technology: Renewable energy infrastructure (solar farms, wind turbines, hydroelectric dams, and modernized electrical grids) involves massive upfront capital expenditure. Most LICs lack the national financial reserves or borrowing power to finance green energy transitions without international grants.
  • Climate equity and historical responsibility: High-income countries (HICs) generated over 70% of cumulative atmospheric emissions since the Industrial Revolution to build their wealth. LICs argue it is fundamentally unjust to impose equal reduction burdens on developing states whose current per-capita emissions remain tiny (e.g. under 1 tonne/person/year in Sub-Saharan Africa vs over 14 tonnes in the USA).
  • Immediate domestic crises: Governments in LICs face immediate existential pressures such as healthcare deficits, food insecurity, unemployment, and civil conflict; long-term decarbonization targets are often seen as secondary to basic survival priorities.
TASK 2

Source B: Gardi Sugdub community facing sea-level rise

Gardi Sugdub, or ‘Crab Island,’ is a tiny island in an archipelago off Panama’s northern Caribbean coast. It has been home to Guna Indigenous people for over 100 years. Originally the island provided the Guna with refuge from mainland mosquito-borne illnesses and colonial restrictions, but today, Gardi Sugdub faces new challenges.

With nearly 1,300 people on an island the size of about five soccer fields, there is no room to expand homes or for children to play. Floods and storms have made life even harder on the island, affecting housing, water, health, and education. Such extreme weather is only expected to become more common as the climate crisis accelerates. While urgent government action can still mitigate the worst climate change scenarios, scientists are also virtually certain of a rise in sea levels globally, undermining the habitability of the island these residents call home.

Facing the inevitability of rising seas and surging storms on an island with no space, the Gardi Sugdub community have been contending with existential questions: what is next for our people? Do we have to move? If so, when, where, and how?

Source: Human Rights Watch

  1. Describe the challenges faced by the Gardi Sugdub community.
  2. Propose potential government actions that could help mitigate the challenges faced by the inhabitants of Gardi Sugdub in the short-term and long-term.
  3. Evaluate the role of international co-operation and climate policies in addressing the challenges of small island communities like Gardi Sugdub. How might global initiatives impact the local resilience and adaptation strategies of such communities?
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) Challenges faced by the Gardi Sugdub community:

  • Severe overcrowding and land scarcity: Nearly 1,300 residents are confined to a tiny coral islet equivalent to just five soccer pitches (~0.04 km²), leaving virtually zero space for new home construction, public sanitation, or children's recreation.
  • Frequent coastal flooding and storm surges: Rising sea levels combined with seasonal Caribbean storms routinely submerge wooden and cane dwellings, soaking personal belongings, contaminating freshwater supplies, and disrupting school classes.
  • Health and sanitation risks: Inundation overflows latrines and primitive waste systems, elevating the incidence of gastrointestinal diseases, skin infections, and mosquito-borne illnesses in stagnant floodwaters.
  • Cultural and psychological existential threat: The community faces the profound emotional trauma of being forced to abandon their ancestral territory, risking the dissolution of centuries-old Guna indigenous traditions, communal governance, and maritime heritage.

b) Proposed government actions (Short-term vs. Long-term):

  • Short-term actions:
    • Construct temporary protective rock revetments and sandbag sea-walls around vulnerable island perimeters to buffer against storm surges.
    • Install mobile solar-powered water purification units and provide emergency medical supplies and temporary flood-shelter facilities.
    • Implement early warning weather alert networks and emergency boat evacuation protocols for severe Caribbean storms.
  • Long-term actions:
    • Plan, finance, and execute the complete, dignified relocation of the community to safer, elevated mainland sites (e.g. the Isberyala resettlement project).
    • Build climate-resilient infrastructure on the mainland: modern schools, sewage treatment plants, paved roads, healthcare centers, and reliable clean water/electricity grids.
    • Provide economic transition support: establish reliable transportation links so Guna fishermen can still access traditional marine waters, while providing agricultural training and land titles for mainland subsistence farming.

c) Evaluation of international cooperation and climate policies:

  • Essential global mitigation: Local island adaptation has absolute limits. Without aggressive global emissions cuts by major economies under the Paris Agreement, continued sea-level rise will eventually submerge all coastal atolls, rendering local seawalls futile.
  • Access to climate adaptation finance: Developing countries like Panama have limited budgets. Multilateral funds (e.g. UN Green Climate Fund, Loss and Damage Fund) are vital to subsidize the tens of millions of dollars required for resettlement infrastructure and housing.
  • Technical expertise & legal human rights standards: International agencies (UNHCR, Human Rights Watch) help formulate legal frameworks ensuring that indigenous climate-displaced persons retain land tenure, cultural autonomy, and free, prior, and informed consent during planned relocation.
TASK 3

Study Source D: A conventional family home that is poorly designed and has a significant environmental impact.

Source D: A conventional family home that is poorly designed and has a significant environmental impact
  1. What could be done to reduce the environmental impact of the family home in Source D?
  2. As a class, debate the success of individual actions, such as improving the environmental impact of family homes, versus implementing climate change policies.
  3. Evaluate how you contribute to climate change. Take a typical day, for example: how do you get around? How do you keep warm or cool? What do you eat? Are there any changes that you could make that could reduce your impact?
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) Measures to reduce the environmental impact of the home:

  • Thermal insulation & glazing: Install high-spec loft insulation, cavity wall insulation, and replace drafty single-glazed windows with triple-glazed energy-rated windows to prevent massive domestic heat loss.
  • Renewable energy generation: Install rooftop solar photovoltaic (PV) panels to generate clean electricity, and solar thermal collector tubes to provide hot water.
  • Low-carbon heating/cooling: Replace old, inefficient gas or oil-fired central heating boilers with electric air-source or ground-source heat pumps.
  • Energy efficiency & smart tech: Upgrade to A+++ rated electrical appliances, switch all domestic lighting to low-energy LEDs, and install smart thermostats to optimize zone heating.
  • Water conservation: Install rooftop rainwater harvesting tanks for toilet flushing and garden irrigation; fit low-flow aerated taps and water-efficient showerheads.

b) Debate: Individual actions vs. Government climate change policies:

  • Arguments for Individual Actions (Bottom-Up):
    • Can be enacted immediately without waiting for bureaucratic legislation or political consensus.
    • Cumulative effect: when millions of households reduce energy, meat consumption, and driving, aggregate emissions drop significantly.
    • Changes consumer demand patterns, forcing businesses and retail markets to offer greener, carbon-neutral products.
  • Arguments for Government Policies (Top-Down):
    • Individual actions cannot re-engineer national electrical power grids, build high-speed rail networks, or decarbonize heavy industry (cement, steel, maritime shipping).
    • Policies create mandatory compliance (e.g. banning petrol cars, enforcing strict building insulation codes, implementing carbon taxes).
    • Subsidies and grants make clean energy affordable for low-income households who cannot afford solar panels or heat pumps on their own.
  • Conclusion: Neither approach is sufficient in isolation. Top-down policies create the economic and legal infrastructure, while bottom-up individual choices drive societal adoption and cultural change.

c) Personal carbon footprint self-evaluation:

  • Transportation: Assess reliance on private petrol/diesel car trips vs. walking, cycling, or public mass transit (buses, metro). Change: Walk or cycle for short journeys under 2 km; carpool or use school buses.
  • Heating & Cooling: Evaluate setting air conditioning too cold in summer or heaters too high in winter. Change: Adjust thermostat by 1–2°C (saving up to 10% energy); turn off appliances at wall sockets instead of leaving on standby.
  • Dietary Choices: Examine intake of ruminant meat (beef, lamb) and dairy, which have very high methane footprints. Change: Adopt "Meatless Mondays" or increase proportion of plant-based meals; reduce household food waste.
  • Consumer Habits: Consider purchases of fast fashion and single-use plastics. Change: Buy secondhand or durable goods; carry reusable bottles and shopping bags.
TASK 4

'It is better to mitigate the effects of climate change rather than adapt to them'. To what extent do you agree with this statement? Support your ideas with examples.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Extended Essay Framework & Model Evaluation:

1. Arguments in favor of MITIGATION (Tackling Root Causes):

  • Physical limits of adaptation: Human engineering cannot adapt infinitely. If global temperatures rise by 3–4°C, oceanic ecosystems will suffer irreversible collapse from acidification, and wet-bulb temperatures in tropical regions will exceed the biological limits of human heat tolerance, making outdoor work lethal.
  • Long-term cost effectiveness: Transitioning to renewable energy and reforesting ecosystems is vastly cheaper over generations than perpetually repairing destroyed cities, constructing endlessly higher sea walls, and managing millions of displaced refugees.
  • Concrete Examples: Transitioning electricity grids to solar and offshore wind; Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS); international treaties like the Paris Agreement (capping warming at 1.5°C); large-scale afforestation (e.g. Africa's Great Green Wall).

2. Arguments in favor of ADAPTATION (Managing Unavoidable Impacts):

  • Locked-in warming and climate inertia: Even if all global greenhouse gas emissions miraculously halted today, existing emissions will continue driving sea-level rise and atmospheric warming for centuries due to thermal inertia in deep oceans and ice sheets. Frontline communities need immediate protection today.
  • Immediate life-saving protection: Vulnerable developing nations cannot afford to wait decades for global emissions to fall while their crops fail and storm surges flood their coastal towns. Adaptation delivers immediate, measurable resilience.
  • Concrete Examples: Hard engineering (e.g. Thames Barrier in London, Delta Works in the Netherlands); soft engineering (mangrove afforestation in Bangladesh to buffer storm surges); agricultural adaptation (drought-resistant genetically modified seeds, floating agriculture); planned resettlement of at-risk islanders (Gardi Sugdub, Panama).

3. Balanced Evaluative Conclusion:

I disagree that one strategy is "better" than the other in isolation; rather, mitigation and adaptation are complementary, interdependent necessities. Without aggressive mitigation, climate change will accelerate beyond human adaptive capacity. Conversely, without immediate adaptation, millions of people in vulnerable regions will suffer catastrophic harm from locked-in warming before global mitigation takes full effect. Therefore, the most effective global response is an integrated, dual-track strategy combining aggressive emissions reductions with robust local adaptation financing.
