TASK 1

Study Source A: The structure of the Earth

Source A: The structure of the Earth
  1. Complete a labelled diagram of the Earth's structure.
  2. State which layer of the Earth is the thinnest.
  3. In pairs, discuss how convection currents move the tectonic plates across the surface of the Earth.
  4. Draw a simple diagram to show the movement of magma and the convection currents. Include the direction that the plates are moving in your diagram.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Labelled diagram of the Earth's structure:

Your diagram should include four clearly labelled layers from the outside inward:

  • Crust – The outermost, thinnest solid layer (5–70 km thick). Divided into oceanic crust (denser, thinner, basaltic) and continental crust (less dense, thicker, granitic).
  • Mantle – The thickest layer (~2,900 km). Made of semi-molten rock (magma). The upper mantle (asthenosphere) is partially molten and allows tectonic plates to move.
  • Outer Core – Liquid layer made mainly of iron and nickel (~2,200 km thick). Generates Earth's magnetic field.
  • Inner Core – Solid ball of iron and nickel (~1,200 km radius). Extremely high pressure keeps it solid despite extreme temperatures.

Examiner Tip: Arrows should show the layers from surface to centre. Label the depth/thickness if you know it.

b. Thinnest layer of the Earth:

  • The crust is the thinnest layer. Oceanic crust can be as thin as 5–10 km, while continental crust is 25–70 km — still far thinner than the mantle or cores.

c. How convection currents move tectonic plates:

  • The intense heat from the Earth's core causes magma in the mantle to rise (as it becomes less dense when heated).
  • As the magma rises to the base of the crust, it spreads sideways, dragging tectonic plates with it.
  • When the magma cools, it becomes denser and sinks back towards the core, completing the convection cycle.
  • This continuous circular movement of magma acts like a conveyor belt, moving tectonic plates across the Earth's surface at rates of a few centimetres per year.

d. Diagram showing magma movement and convection currents:

  • Your diagram should show a cross-section of the mantle beneath two tectonic plates.
  • Draw circular arrows in the mantle showing hot magma rising at the centre (beneath a mid-ocean ridge) and cool magma sinking at the edges (beneath a subduction zone).
  • Show arrows on the plate surfaces pointing outward (diverging) at the ridge where magma rises.
  • Label: hot magma rising, cool magma sinking, direction of plate movement, convection cell, crust/lithosphere.
TASK 2

Study Source B: The plates that make up the Earth's crust

Source B: Tectonic plate boundaries
  1. Which plate do you live on? Consider your distance from the nearest plate boundary. Are you at risk from earthquakes or volcanoes?
  2. Describe the direction of plate movement between the Pacific plate and the Nazca plate.
  3. Name two plates that are moving towards each other. What do you call this type of plate boundary?
  4. In pairs, discuss the shape of the continents. Consider how the continents could fit together if you were to cut them out to use as a puzzle. Is there a clear pattern? Does it suggest where continents may have been joined together in the past?
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Which plate do you live on?

  • For students in Vietnam / Southeast Asia: You live on the Eurasian Plate. Vietnam is located well within the centre of the plate, far from active plate boundaries. Your risk from earthquakes and volcanoes is therefore relatively low, though not zero (minor seismic activity occurs in some areas).
  • Guide students to identify their own country on the map and measure their approximate distance from the nearest marked plate boundary.

b. Direction of plate movement between Pacific and Nazca plates:

  • The Pacific Plate moves in a generally northwest direction.
  • The Nazca Plate moves in a generally east/northeast direction.
  • They are moving away from each other (diverging) at the East Pacific Rise (a mid-ocean ridge). This is a constructive/divergent plate boundary.

c. Two plates moving towards each other (convergent boundary):

  • Example answers: The Nazca Plate and the South American Plate (subduction boundary — Andes Mountains). Or: the Indo-Australian Plate and the Eurasian Plate (collision — Himalayas).
  • When two plates move towards each other, this is called a convergent plate boundary (also called a destructive plate boundary if oceanic crust is subducted, or a collision boundary if both plates carry continental crust).

d. Shapes of the continents — could they fit together?

  • Yes — there is a clear pattern. The eastern coastline of South America fits remarkably closely against the western coastline of Africa, like puzzle pieces.
  • This is evidence for continental drift and the theory of Pangaea — the supercontinent that existed approximately 200 million years ago before breaking apart.
  • Additional evidence includes matching rock types, fossils of the same species, and similar mountain ranges found on both sides of the Atlantic Ocean.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Labelled sketch of a convergent/destructive plate boundary:

  • Your diagram must show: oceanic plate and continental plate with arrows showing both moving towards each other.
  • Label: subduction zone (where oceanic crust dives beneath continental crust), ocean trench (deep trench formed at the boundary), magma (rising through the continental crust), volcano (forming on the continental side), earthquake zone (along the Benioff zone where friction occurs), continental crust, oceanic crust.
  • Oceanic crust is subducted because it is denser than continental crust. It melts and forms magma in the mantle, which rises to create explosive volcanoes.

b. Type of plate boundary around the Ring of Fire:

  • The Ring of Fire is primarily characterised by convergent/destructive plate boundaries where oceanic plates (especially the Pacific Plate) are being subducted beneath surrounding continental plates (Eurasian, North American, South American).
  • Some sections also have conservative (transform) boundaries — e.g., the San Andreas Fault in California where the Pacific and North American plates slide past each other.
  • The intense subduction creates frequent, powerful earthquakes and explosive volcanic activity along the entire Pacific Rim.
