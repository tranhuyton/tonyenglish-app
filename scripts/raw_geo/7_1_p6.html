TASK 1: Study Sources C and D

Study Sources C and D: Global and regional urbanisation patterns

Source C: Global urban and rural population, 1950 to 2050 (actual and projected)
Source D: Share of population living in urban areas by region (1990, 2014, 2050 projected)
GEOGRAPHY TIP: Urban growth means the increase in the population size and/or physical surface area of an urban settlement. Urbanisation is the increasing proportion or percentage of a country's total population living in urban areas.
  1. a. Define the terms 'urbanisation' and 'urban growth'.
  2. b. Using Source C, in which year did the global urban population exceed the rural population?
  3. c. Using Source D:
    1. Compare and contrast the changes in urbanisation over time between Europe and Africa.
    2. Suggest reasons for the projected increase of urban population in Asia.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Definitions of key terms:

  • Urbanisation: An increase in the proportion or percentage of a country or region's total population living in towns and cities compared to rural areas. It reflects a structural shift in society from agrarian living to urban living.
  • Urban Growth: The absolute expansion in the total number of people residing in a town or city, or the physical spatial expansion of the built-up urban area (often measured in square kilometres or raw population numbers).

b. Year when global urban population exceeded rural population:

  • According to the crossing point of the solid red (Urban) and dashed black (Rural) lines in Source C, the global urban population exceeded the rural population in 2007–2008 (at approximately 3,400 million / 3.4 billion people).

c. i. Compare and contrast urbanisation between Europe and Africa (Source D):

  • Level of Urbanisation: Europe maintains a substantially higher overall level of urbanisation across all recorded time periods than Africa. In 1990, Europe was 70% urbanised compared to Africa's 31%; by 2014, Europe reached 73% while Africa reached 40%; and by 2050, Europe is projected to reach 82% while Africa reaches 56%.
  • Rate / Pace of Growth: Africa's rate of urbanisation is accelerating much faster than Europe's. Between 1990 and 2050, Africa's urban share is projected to surge by +25 percentage points (nearly doubling from 31% to 56%), whereas Europe experiences a much slower, gradual increase of only +12 percentage points (from 70% to 82%).
  • Underlying Context: Europe completed its primary rural-to-urban transition during the 19th-century Industrial Revolution (mature urbanisation stage), while Africa is currently undergoing rapid rural-to-urban migration coupled with high natural population increase.

c. ii. Reasons for the projected increase of urban population in Asia (32% in 1990 to 64% in 2050):

  • Rapid Industrialisation & Economic Restructuring: Major Asian emerging economies (e.g. China, India, Vietnam, Indonesia) have shifted from primary agriculture to secondary manufacturing and tertiary finance/services centered in cities.
  • Rural Push Factors: Mechanisation of farming reduces agricultural labor demand; rural poverty, low crop prices, small fragmented landholdings, and natural disasters (monsoon floods, droughts) force rural families off farms.
  • Urban Pull Factors: Cities offer higher formal and informal wages, employment in Special Economic Zones (SEZs) and factories, and superior access to higher education, hospitals, piped utilities, and modern retail.
  • Demographic Momentum & Reclassification: Young migrant populations in Asian cities contribute to high rates of natural increase, while national governments actively reclassify expanding rural towns into designated urban municipalities.
TASK 2: Study Sources E and F

Study Sources E and F: Projections of urban growth and the urbanisation pathway

Source E: Urban population growth in HICs and LICs, 1950 to 2050 (actual and projected)
Source F: The Urbanisation Pathway (S-curve model: LICs, MICs, HICs, and Counter-urbanisation)
  1. a. Using Source E, describe the predicted changes in the urban population in HICs and LICs.
  2. b. Explain why rates of urban growth are projected to be faster and higher in LICs than HICs.
  3. c. Give two push factors and two pull factors of rural-urban migration.
  4. d. Study Source F. Why do you think the pace of urbanisation begins to slow and then flatten out or decline as countries develop economically?
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Predicted changes in urban population in HICs vs. LICs (Source E):

  • High-Income Countries (HICs): Urban population increases very slowly, displaying a nearly flat trajectory. It rose from ~0.5 billion in 1950 to ~1.0 billion in 2020, and is projected to plateau at approximately 1.1 billion by 2050.
  • Low-Income Countries (LICs/MICs): Urban population shows explosive, near-exponential growth. Starting below HICs at ~0.35 billion in 1950, it overtook HICs around 1975, reached ~3.2 billion by 2020, and is projected to climb steeply to over 5.1 billion by 2050 (an increase of ~1.9 billion in 30 years).

b. Why urban growth rates are projected to be faster and higher in LICs than HICs:

  • Stage on the Urbanisation Pathway: LICs and emerging MICs are currently in Stage 2 (accelerating urbanisation), driven by rapid economic transformation and manufacturing growth. In contrast, HICs are in Stage 3/4 (mature or counter-urbanisation) where urban percentages have already stabilized.
  • Demographic Natural Increase: LIC cities have a very youthful age structure with high birth rates and falling infant mortality rates (due to urban medical access), creating massive natural population increase within cities.
  • Scale of Rural Migration: A large percentage of the total population in LICs still lives in rural agricultural areas, representing a vast reservoir of potential rural-to-urban migrants.

c. Push and Pull factors of rural-urban migration:

  • Rural Push Factors (repelling people from the countryside):
    • Severe rural underemployment and low, irregular wages in subsistence agriculture.
    • Environmental vulnerability: soil degradation, droughts, desertification, or flooding causing recurrent crop failures.
    • Inadequate basic social infrastructure: absence of secondary schools, electricity, clean piped water, and specialist medical care.
  • Urban Pull Factors (attracting people to the city):
    • Perception of abundant formal and informal employment with regular wages.
    • Better access to higher-quality education, healthcare hospitals, clean water, and reliable power grids.
    • Aspiration for social mobility, modern lifestyle, cultural diversity, and entertainment amenities.

d. Why the pace of urbanisation slows, flattens, or declines in developed economies (Source F):

  • Exhaustion of Rural Migrant Pool (Saturation Effect): When a country reaches Stage 3 (75–85% urban, like Italy or France), very few people remain in rural areas to migrate into cities.
  • Demographic Aging & Low Fertility: HICs experience low birth rates (often below the 2.1 replacement rate) and an aging population, causing natural decrease or stagnation in city populations.
  • Stage 4 Counter-Urbanisation: In advanced economies, affluent urban residents increasingly move out of dense, polluted, and congested urban cores into rural villages, market towns, or suburban green belts to seek larger homes, cleaner air, lower crime, and a better quality of life. This trend is enabled by high car ownership, high-speed rail links, teleworking, and broadband internet.
