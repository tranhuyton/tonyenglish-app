TASK 1: Housing Management in Favelas

Housing in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil

One challenge common in many cities in LICs and MICs is informal settlements. In Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, about one in five people live in informal settlements, or favelas. Different management strategies include government redevelopment (top-down) schemes and self-help or bottom-up schemes such as the Favela Bairro Project.

Figure 7.5: Favelas clinging to steep granite hillsides in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil
  1. a. What is the difference between 'top-down' and 'bottom-up' housing management schemes?
  2. b. Outline the advantages and disadvantages of the Favela Bairro Project for:
    1. Favela residents
    2. Urban planners
    3. The government
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Difference between Top-down and Bottom-up schemes:

  • Top-down schemes: Large-scale projects initiated, financed, and managed directly by central government authorities, international funding bodies, or corporate property developers, with minimal consultation or direct involvement from local residents (e.g. wholesale slum clearance and relocation to distant public housing tower blocks).
  • Bottom-up (self-help) schemes: Grassroots community-led initiatives where local residents are directly involved in planning, decision-making, and physical construction. The government or NGOs provide technical advice, building materials (breeze blocks, corrugated iron, cement), and legal tenure, empowering residents to upgrade their own homes and local infrastructure.

b. Advantages and Disadvantages of the Favela Bairro Project:

  • i. Favela Residents:
    • Advantages: Greatly improved quality of life through paved access roads for rubbish trucks and ambulances; clean piped drinking water; sewage systems replacing open gutters; formal electricity connections; new community crèches, sports facilities, and adult literacy courses. Residents retain their homes and strong community support networks without eviction.
    • Disadvantages: Formalisation introduces regular municipal utility bills and property taxes that low-income families struggle to pay; project funds did not cover every favela in Rio (creating inequality between upgraded and neglected favelas); drug gangs, militia violence, and insecurity often persist.
  • ii. Urban Planners:
    • Advantages: Cost-effective compared to bulldozing and rebuilding an entire district; preserves existing social fabric and informal economic clusters; integrates hillside favelas into Rio's broader transport networks (e.g. cable cars / Teleférico do Alemão).
    • Disadvantages: Extreme engineering challenges when laying water mains, sewage pipes, and drainage channels on steep, landslide-prone granite slopes with narrow, labyrinthine alleyways; resolving complicated informal property ownership is legally fraught.
  • iii. The Municipal Government:
    • Advantages: Improves the city's international image and social stability; reduces waterborne diseases (cholera, hepatitis) lowering public healthcare burdens; brings informal economic activities into the formal tax system.
    • Disadvantages: Enormously expensive (over $1 billion funded partly by the Inter-American Development Bank); severe budget overruns and corruption allegations; ongoing maintenance costs for infrastructure are difficult to sustain long-term.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Five examples of sustainable urban management from Source A:

  • 1. Renewable Energy Generation: Solar photovoltaic panels on building rooftops and wind turbines generating zero-carbon electricity.
  • 2. Integrated Clean Public Transit: Electric light rail (trams), electric zero-emission buses, and widespread EV charging stations.
  • 3. Active Travel Infrastructure: Dedicated separated cycle lanes and pedestrianised walkways promoting walking and cycling.
  • 4. Green Roofs and Urban Parks: Vegetation planted on roofs (green roofs) and expansive tree-lined parks creating urban carbon sinks and cooling heat islands.
  • 5. Energy-Efficient Building Architecture: Passive solar orientation, triple-glazed insulation, natural daylighting, and rainwater harvesting systems.

b. Explain one economic, one social, and one environmental strategy:

  • Economic Strategy: Developing integrated mass public transit (electric light rail, BRT). Explanation: Reduces business losses from traffic congestion, shortens worker commute times, lowers spending on imported fossil fuels, and stimulates economic productivity across the city.
  • Social Strategy: Expanding urban green spaces and active transport networks. Explanation: Provides free, accessible recreational space for all demographics, promoting physical exercise, lowering obesity and cardiovascular illness, and enhancing mental well-being and social cohesion.
  • Environmental Strategy: Green roofs and urban tree planting. Explanation: Plants absorb ambient carbon dioxide and filter fine particulate matter (PM2.5); green roofs absorb up to 70–80% of rainfall, mitigating urban surface runoff and preventing flash floods.

c. Case Study Example: Curitiba, Brazil (or Freiburg, Germany):

Curitiba's Integrated Sustainable Model:
  • Rede Integrada de Transporte (BRT): Dedicated bus express lanes with elevated glass boarding tubes where fares are pre-paid, allowing level-boarding in seconds. Over 70% of commuters use the BRT daily, cutting private car traffic by 25% and resulting in one of the cleanest air qualities in Brazil.
  • Floodplain Linear Parks: Instead of costly concrete river canalization, Curitiba preserved natural riverbanks as linear parks with retention lakes. Sheep graze the grass naturally, eliminating mowing costs.
  • Cambio Verde (Green Exchange): Low-income favela residents trade sorted recyclable plastics, glass, and metal for fresh seasonal fruits, vegetables, and bus tokens, drastically reducing municipal litter while alleviating malnutrition.

d. Evaluation of Strengths and Weaknesses:

  • Strengths: Long-term reduction in carbon emissions and urban air pollution; improved public health and quality of life; protection of biodiversity; resilient storm drainage.
  • Weaknesses: High initial capital expenditure requiring substantial public borrowing; potential "eco-gentrification" pricing out low-income citizens; success is contingent on long-term citizen compliance, behavioral change, and continuous political commitment across changing administrations.

TASK 1: Population Growth in Mumbai

Located on the west coast of India, Mumbai is a rapidly growing megacity. Mumbai's population has expanded dramatically over recent decades due to natural increase and massive rural-to-urban migration.

Source A: Population growth over time in Mumbai (1950–2020)
  1. a. In which year did Mumbai become a megacity?
  2. b. In which decade did Mumbai's population increase most rapidly?
  3. c. Describe the population growth of Mumbai between 1950 and 2020.
  4. d. Explain three reasons for rural-urban migration to Mumbai.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Year Mumbai became a megacity:

  • A megacity is defined as a metropolitan urban agglomeration with a population exceeding 10 million. According to Source A, Mumbai crossed the 10 million threshold in approximately 1984 (between 1980 at ~9.2m and 1990 at ~12.5m).

b. Decade of most rapid population increase:

  • 1980–1990 (or 1990–2000): During these decades, the population expanded by over 3.3 to 3.7 million people, marked by the steepest upward gradient of the growth curve.

c. Description of Mumbai's population growth (1950–2020):

  • Massive Overall Expansion: Population increased continuously and dramatically from ~3.2 million in 1950 to over 20.5 million by 2020 (more than a six-fold increase).
  • Initial Steady Growth (1950–1970): Rose steadily from ~3.2m to ~6.5m.
  • Accelerating Expansion (1970–2000): Experienced steep, exponential growth, doubling from ~6.5m in 1970 to over 16.0m by 2000.
  • Post-2000 Trend: Sustained growth toward 20.5 million, though the rate of increase shows slight deceleration as spatial constraints on Salsette Island limit further peripheral expansion.

d. Three reasons for rural-to-urban migration to Mumbai:

  • 1. Economic / Employment Pull: Mumbai is India's financial, commercial, and entertainment capital (generating 5% of India's GDP). It offers vast employment opportunities in the formal sector (banking, shipping at Jawaharlal Nehru Port, IT, textile manufacturing) and informal sector (construction, street trading, domestic service) with substantially higher wage potential than rural farming.
  • 2. Agricultural Vulnerability & Rural Debt (Push): Rural Maharashtra and neighboring states suffer from recurrent monsoonal failures, severe droughts, and crop failures. Debt burdens from costly commercial seeds/fertilisers force impoverished agricultural laborers to abandon farms.
  • 3. Superior Access to Services & Infrastructure (Pull): Rural villages often lack basic infrastructure (clean running water, reliable power, secondary schools, and healthcare clinics). Mumbai attracts families seeking quality medical care and educational opportunities for their children.
TASK 2: Stakeholder Perspectives on Dharavi Redevelopment
Figure 7.11: Dharavi informal settlement contrasting with modern high-rise tower blocks in Mumbai

Large-scale housing redevelopment schemes are controversial. Geographers need to identify and consider different viewpoints of those impacted by such schemes. Imagine that you are:

  1. a family currently living in Dharavi
  2. working for a property developer
  3. working for the city authorities

For each stakeholder position, think about your viewpoints and priorities regarding the proposed demolition and redevelopment of Dharavi.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

  • a. Family currently living in Dharavi:
    • Viewpoints & Concerns: Severe anxiety over destruction of livelihood and community networks. Dharavi is a vibrant economic hub generating over $1 billion annually, where homes double as workshops (leather tanning, pottery kilns, plastic recycling, textile embroidery). High-rise apartments cannot accommodate industrial activities or ground-floor commercial storefronts.
    • Fears: Disqualification from free replacement housing due to inability to prove residency before strict government cut-off dates (e.g. 2000); loss of mutual neighborhood support, communal childcare, and low-cost living.
    • Desires: Prefer on-site incremental slum upgrading (provision of private toilets, clean piped water, safe electrical wiring) that preserves existing community cohesion.
  • b. Property Developer:
    • Viewpoints & Priorities: Strongly advocates comprehensive demolition and modern high-rise redevelopment. Dharavi occupies over 500 acres of prime real estate in central Mumbai, directly adjacent to the high-value Bandra-Kurla Complex (BKC) financial district.
    • Commercial Motivations: Massive profit potential through constructing luxury residential apartments, premium office towers, and shopping malls. Argues that redevelopment modernises Mumbai's global skyline, replaces dangerous informal shacks with earthquake-resistant towers, and provides sanitary high-rise units for eligible residents.
  • c. City Authorities (Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai):
    • Viewpoints & Priorities: Balancing public health, economic modernization, and social stability. Aims to eradicate major public health hazards (open sewers, waterborne cholera/dysentery, dengue) and severe fire risks inherent to overcrowded shanties.
    • Municipal Goals: Redevelopment allows the municipality to construct wide roads for emergency vehicles, formal piped sewer and water networks, modern schools, and public clinics.
    • Administrative Challenges: Mitigating public protests, verifying resident eligibility, preventing mass displacement, and ensuring developers fulfill commitments to rehouse eligible slum dwellers without prioritizing private luxury profits.
TASK 3: Study Source B

Source B: News article from The Wire evaluating Mumbai's monorail project

Source B: News analysis evaluating the challenges and performance of Mumbai's monorail system

Evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of the Mumbai monorail transport project as a strategy to improve the quality of life of people living in the city.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

Strengths (Advantages):

  • Minimal Ground Footprint: The elevated monorail track sits on single concrete support pillars, taking up minimal street-level road space—ideal for Mumbai's densely built-up corridors where road widening is impossible.
  • High Maneuverability: Can navigate sharp horizontal turns and steep inclines that conventional heavy railway trains cannot negotiate.
  • Passenger Comfort & Safety: Provides air-conditioned, elevated transit above street-level traffic jams, waterlogged monsoon flooding, and vehicle exhaust fumes.
  • Suburban Regeneration: Designed to connect eastern industrial suburbs (Chembur to Jacob Circle) to stimulate economic investment in neglected districts.

Weaknesses (Disadvantages):

  • Poor Multi-modal Integration: Failed to connect directly with Mumbai's primary Suburban Railway stations (commuters had to walk up to 4 km to transfer) or major bus terminals, discouraging daily commuter usage.
  • Inconvenient Route Alignment: Routed through industrial wasteland rather than through core residential corridors or major business centers.
  • Low Ridership & Heavy Financial Losses: Designed to carry 125,000 passengers/day, but achieved fewer than 10,000–15,000 daily passengers, running at an unsustainable financial deficit.
  • Operational Failures & Accidents: Plagued by mechanical breakdowns, tire ruptures, component shortages, and a major fire in November 2017 that shut the system down for 10 months.
  • Inappropriate Technology Choice: Far lower carrying capacity than a heavy Metro line, but far more expensive to construct and maintain than a Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system.

Overall Evaluation / Conclusion:

While the Mumbai monorail offered innovative elevated engineering suited for narrow roads, it proved to be a flawed urban management strategy. Due to poor route planning, isolated station placement, lack of integration with the existing transit network, and frequent technical breakdowns, it failed to relieve Mumbai's severe road congestion or overcrowded commuter trains, ultimately having a minimal impact on improving the daily quality of life for the vast majority of citizens. Modern urban management must prioritize comprehensive, integrated mass transit (such as the Mumbai Metro Master Plan) over isolated prestige projects.
