1. How Earthquakes Occur (Seismic Processes)

An earthquake is the sudden, violent shaking of the ground caused by the instantaneous release of accumulated elastic strain energy stored in lithospheric rocks along a geological fault line.

Core Mechanics of an Earthquake Event

  • Elastic Rebound Theory: As tectonic plates grind past each other, massive frictional resistance prevents smooth movement. Rocks deform elastically like a bending bow, storing enormous shear stress over decades or centuries. When stress exceeds the rock's shear strength, the fault snaps, rebounding violently to its unstrained position and radiating shockwaves.
  • Focus (Hypocentre): The exact subterranean point or rupture zone within the crust or mantle where rock fracture originates and seismic waves begin.
  • Epicentre: The point on the Earth's surface directly vertically above the focus. It is the geographic location closest to the rupture origin and typically suffers the most violent ground acceleration and destruction.
  • Focal Depth Categories & The Benioff Zone:
    • Shallow-focus earthquakes (0–70 km): Produce the most catastrophic surface destruction because seismic energy dissipates very little before reaching human settlements. Found along all boundary types (especially conservative and constructive boundaries).
    • Intermediate-focus (70–300 km) & Deep-focus earthquakes (300–700 km): Occur almost exclusively at destructive subduction boundaries along the Wadati-Benioff Zone—the inclined dipping plane of the subducting oceanic slab descending into the mantle. Deep earthquakes rarely cause severe surface damage as waves attenuate during their ascent.

2. How Volcanoes Form and Erupt

Nghe cả phần

Volcanoes are openings or vents in Earth's crust through which molten rock (magma), dissolved toxic gases, and tephra escape onto the surface. Magma forms when oceanic crust subducts and melts in the hot mantle, or when decompression occurs at divergent rifts.

🌋 Cross-Section: Stratovolcano (Composite Cone) Anatomy & Eruption Dynamics

Magma Chemistry, Viscosity & Eruption Dynamics

The explosiveness of a volcanic eruption is dictated primarily by two interrelated geochemical factors:

  1. Silica (SiO₂) Content & Viscosity:
    • Basaltic Magma (Low Silica ~50%): Low viscosity (runny, fluid). Gases escape easily with minimal resistance. Generates gentle, effusive eruptions and broad, gently sloping volcanoes. Found at constructive boundaries and oceanic hotspots.
    • Andesitic / Rhyolitic Magma (High Silica ~60–70%): Highly viscous (sticky, sluggish). Traps escaping superheated steam and gases. Pressure builds until catastrophic explosive blasting shatters rock into fine ash and pyroclastic debris. Characteristic of destructive subduction margins.
  2. Dissolved Gas Pressure: Volatile compounds (water vapor H₂O, carbon dioxide CO₂, sulfur dioxide SO₂) expand rapidly as magma ascends toward low atmospheric pressure. In viscous magma, gas bubbles cannot coalesce or expand freely, turning the conduit into a pressurized explosive bomb.

3. Types of Volcanoes

Nghe cả phần

Volcanic landforms vary significantly depending on the nature of the erupted lava, the frequency of explosive episodes, and tectonic boundary setting:

Volcano Type Shape & Slope Profile Lava Type & Viscosity Eruption Style Global Examples
Shield Volcano Very broad base, gentle slopes (<10°), resembles an ancient warrior's shield laying flat. Basaltic lava (low silica, low viscosity, highly fluid; travels long distances). Effusive, non-explosive lava fountains and long lava rivers. Rare ash fall. Mauna Loa & Kilauea (Hawaii), Erta Ale (Ethiopia)
Stratovolcano (Composite Cone) Tall, symmetrical mountain with steep upper slopes (>30°) and narrower base. Formed of alternating strata of ash and lava. Andesitic / Rhyolitic lava (high silica, high viscosity; cools quickly near vent). Extremely violent, explosive. Produces pyroclastic flows, pumice, and massive ash plumes. Mount Fuji (Japan), Mount St. Helens (USA), Mount Pinatubo, Mount Etna
Cinder Cone (Ash/Scoria Cone) Smallest volcanoes (<400m high), steep symmetrical cone formed of loose scoria and volcanic cinders surrounding a bowl crater. Gas-charged basaltic / andesitic magma blown into fragments in mid-air. Strombolian explosive sputtering of glowing cinders and volcanic bombs. Parícutin (Mexico), Sunset Crater (USA)

Major Eruptive Features & Secondary Hazards

  • Pyroclastic Flows (Nuée Ardente): Superheated avalanches of incandescent rock fragments, gas, and volcanic ash racing down mountain slopes at speeds exceeding 200–500 km/h with temperatures up to 800°C. They instantly incinerate everything in their path.
  • Lahars (Volcanic Mudflows): Catastrophic slurry mixtures of volcanic ash, pulverized debris, boulders, and water rushing down river valleys at highway speeds. Triggered when hot pyroclastic surges melt summit snowpacks/glaciers, or when torrential monsoonal rains wash unconsolidated tephra deposits down steep drainages (e.g. Pinatubo 1991, Nevado del Ruiz 1985).
  • Lava Flows: Streams of molten rock emerging from vents or fissures. While basaltic lava flows can advance at 10–30 km/h and destroy farmland and infrastructure, their progress is usually gradual enough for populations to evacuate safely (e.g. Grindavík, Iceland 2023–2024).
  • Calderas: Colossal circular volcanic depressions (often 5 to 50 km wide) formed when an enormous explosive eruption empties the underlying magma chamber, causing the summit rock to collapse inward under its own weight (e.g. Santorini, Greece; Yellowstone; Crater Lake, USA).
  • Geothermal Features: Geysers (intermittent boiling water spouts driven by steam pressure) and Hot Springs (subterranean waters heated by shallow magma intrusions, heavily utilized for geothermal energy in Iceland and New Zealand).
