Practice Activities

Test your understanding of this topic

Activity 1

  • 1. Define migration.
  • 2. What is the difference between voluntary migration and forced migration?
  • 3. Discuss three significant push factors in migration.
  • 4. Suggest how the barriers to migration have changed over time.
  • 5. State the difference between a refugee and: a) an internally displaced person, b) an asylum seeker.
  • 6. What is economic migration?
💡 View Suggested Answers
  • 1. Migration is the movement of people from one place to another with the intention of settling temporarily or permanently in a new location.
  • 2. Voluntary migration is moving by choice for better opportunities, whereas forced migration occurs against a person's will due to conflict, persecution, or disasters.
  • 3. Push factors: 1) High unemployment/poverty, 2) Armed conflict and persecution, 3) Natural disasters or severe climate change.
  • 4. Physical barriers have decreased due to modern transport (air travel), while political barriers (visas, border controls) have generally become more restrictive.
  • 5. a) An IDP has fled their home but remains within their own country's borders, unlike a refugee who crosses international borders. b) An asylum seeker claims to be a refugee, but their claim has not yet been definitively evaluated.
  • 6. Moving specifically to find work, advance a career, or improve one's financial situation.

Activity 2

Figure 6.26 The top 20 recipient countries/territories of international remittances, by total in $US billion (a) and share of GDP (b), 2019–20
  • 1. State two positive and two negative impacts of international migration on countries of origin.
  • 2. Give three benefits of international migration for countries of destination.
  • 3. Present a brief analysis of Figure 6.26.
  • 4. With reference to an example, explain the term 'diaspora'.
  • 5. Discuss the four major challenges and barriers to migration identified in the World Development Report, 2023.
💡 View Suggested Answers
  • 1. Positive: Remittances sent home; reduced pressure on local jobs/resources. Negative: Brain drain (loss of skilled workers); family separation.
  • 2. Fills labour shortages, brings cultural diversity and innovation, and helps offset demographic ageing by adding working-age adults.
  • 3. The figure typically shows how migration flows are dominated by south-to-north and south-to-south patterns, and the significant economic reliance on remittances for developing nations.
  • 4. A diaspora is the dispersion of a people from their homeland. For example, the Indian diaspora comprises millions of people of Indian origin living in countries like the USA and UK, maintaining cultural ties.
  • 5. Challenges include matching migrant skills to host country needs, protecting migrants under international law, managing social integration, and addressing complex drivers like climate change.
