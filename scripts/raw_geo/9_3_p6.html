b. How the increase in internet users has driven the global tourism boom:

  • Disintermediation & Direct Online Booking: Online Travel Agencies (OTAs) such as Booking.com, Expedia, Airbnb, and Skyscanner have eliminated the need for traditional high-street travel agents. Consumers can search, compare, and instantly book budget flights, hotels, and homestays 24/7, significantly lowering holiday costs and friction.
  • Visual Marketing & Viral "Wanderlust": Social media platforms (Instagram, TikTok, YouTube) turn scenic locations into viral sensations. Striking photography and short video reels showcase "hidden gems" and Instagrammable viewpoints, creating spontaneous travel trends and directing millions of young tourists to previously obscure destinations (e.g. Maya Bay in Thailand, Cappadocia in Turkey).
  • Peer Reviews & Risk Reduction: Platforms like TripAdvisor, Google Maps reviews, and travel vlogs allow tourists to verify hotel cleanliness, restaurant quality, and safety beforehand. This builds consumer confidence to travel independently to remote or developing countries.
  • Digital Travel Logistics & In-Destination Navigation: Smartphone apps (Google Maps, translation tools, digital boarding passes, currency converters, ride-hailing apps like Uber/Grab) make traveling in non-English speaking nations much easier and less intimidating, encouraging solo travel and spontaneous excursions.

TASK 2

Study Source A: The Butler Model

Source A: The Butler Model of tourist destination lifecycle

Some people argue that the Butler Model is a simplistic way to explain the lifecycle of a tourist destination.

  1. Explain how the model could be used to ensure that tourism is sustainable.
  2. Do you think it is still relevant today? Justify your answer.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. How the Butler Model can be used to ensure tourism is sustainable:

  • Monitoring Carrying Capacity Thresholds: The Butler Model illustrates how tourist numbers grow over time across distinct stages: Exploration → Involvement → Development → Consolidation → Stagnation → (Rejuvenation or Decline). It helps destination planners recognize when visitor numbers are approaching or exceeding the environment's carrying capacity (the maximum number of tourists an area can support without causing environmental degradation or resident dissatisfaction).
  • Proactive Early Intervention: By recognizing the early warning signs of the Consolidation and Stagnation phases (e.g. overcrowded beaches, escalating property prices, traffic gridlock, water shortages), local governments can implement proactive management policies before irreparable damage occurs:
    • Imposing daily visitor caps and booking quota systems (e.g. Machu Picchu in Peru, Galapagos Islands, Venice entry fee).
    • Zoning regulations that prevent overbuilding along fragile coastlines and protect historical architecture.
    • Investing tourist tax revenues into municipal infrastructure (desalination plants, advanced sewage treatment, public transit).
    • Diversifying attractions to spread tourists across the region and through all four seasons (off-peak cultural, culinary, and ecotourism).
  • Guiding Rejuvenation: If a destination has already reached stagnation, the model shows that authorities must actively reinvent their tourist product (e.g. moving away from cheap, destructive mass package holidays towards upscale ecotourism, heritage trails, and green festivals) to prevent catastrophic economic decline.

b. Critical evaluation of whether the Butler Model is still relevant today:

Arguments for its continued relevance:

  • Accurate historical trajectory for classic mass resorts: The model perfectly mirrors the real lifecycle of traditional seaside destinations across the world (e.g. Blackpool and Torquay in the UK; Benidorm, Magaluf, and Ibiza in Spain; Pattaya and Phuket in Thailand). These began as quiet fishing hamlets, underwent explosive commercial development, reached overcrowding and stagnation, and are now actively battling to rejuvenate.
  • Diagnostic tool for modern overtourism: Global tourist hotspots like Venice, Barcelona, Amsterdam, and Maya Bay in Thailand entered clear stagnation stages where environmental degradation and intense resident backlash ("tourist go home" graffiti) forced governments to enact strict regulatory caps—matching the Butler trajectory.

Limitations and modern adjustments needed:

  • "Viral Tourism" bypasses stages: In the era of TikTok and Instagram, obscure natural viewpoints or quiet villages can leap from Exploration directly into hyper-Development and Stagnation within months, skipping decades of gradual "Involvement".
  • Vulnerability to sudden external shocks: The model assumes a smooth, gradual S-curve. It fails to capture sudden catastrophic crashes caused by global pandemics (COVID-19 halting all international flights overnight), political instability/wars, natural disasters (tsunamis, volcanic eruptions), or economic crises.
  • Deliberate equilibrium (non-growth models): Some modern destinations deliberately reject the growth curve. For example, Bhutan enforces a "High Value, Low Impact" policy with an obligatory daily tourist fee ($100/day) to maintain tourist numbers permanently within the sustainable Involvement phase, consciously preventing mass development.

Conclusion: The Butler Model remains highly relevant as a foundational conceptual guide to destination lifecycles and carrying capacities, but it must be applied flexibly to account for the speed of digital media, global volatility, and deliberate sustainable equilibrium strategies.

    • Extreme pedestrian gridlock & overcrowding: Historic, narrow urban thoroughfares (such as Las Ramblas and the Gothic Quarter) are packed shoulder-to-shoulder with tourists, obstructing free movement and pedestrian safety.
    • Commercial homogenisation & cultural displacement: The public streetscape has been completely taken over by tourism infrastructure (souvenir kiosks, international chain cafes, guided walking tours), crowding out traditional neighborhood community life.

  • Source C (Doha, Qatar — Coastal & Marine Plastic Pollution):
    • Direct environmental littering & unmanaged waste: Discarded single-use plastic water bottles, drink cups, food containers, and wrappers litter the intertidal shoreline.
    • Aesthetic degradation & loss of natural amenity: Transforms a scenic coastal ecosystem into an unhygienic, unsightly refuse dump, destroying its visual value.
    • Marine ecocide: Plastics washed out by tides endanger marine life through entanglement and ingestion. Over time, sunlight breaks these items into toxic microplastics that enter marine food webs and contaminate coastal seafood.

In pairs, discuss the following questions:

  1. There are a number of environmental benefits to the implementation of a successful ecotourism programme. For example, conservation and protection of land, involvement and support from locals, and ensuring sustainable income to maintain the natural environment.

    What are some of the challenges a destination promoting eco-tourism might face as it develops and attracts additional tourists?

  2. Create your own personal definition of sustainable tourism.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Challenges an ecotourism destination faces as it develops and expands:

  • Exceeding Fragile Ecological Carrying Capacities: The very popularity of an eco-destination can destroy what attracted visitors. High volumes of "eco-tourists" walking on wilderness trails cause severe soil compaction, path erosion, disruption of sensitive animal mating/nesting patterns, and damage to coral reefs from snorkeling fins and sunscreen chemicals.
  • The Problem of "Greenwashing": As green travel becomes profitable, conventional commercial operators rebrand ordinary resorts as "eco-friendly" (e.g. simply asking guests to reuse towels) while continuing unsustainable water extraction, diesel power generation, and untreated wastewater disposal. This dilutes genuine conservation efforts.
  • Economic Leakage & Corporate Takeover: Multinational resort chains and foreign tour operators often buy up prime eco-lands. The majority of tour revenues leak back to foreign headquarters, leaving local indigenous populations with only menial, low-paying service jobs (cleaners, porters) rather than managerial ownership or conservation funding.
  • Infrastructural Paradox: Transporting and accommodating growing numbers of eco-tourists requires constructing access roads, airstrips, jetties, water pipelines, and power lines. This construction inevitably fragments delicate rainforest or coastal habitats and introduces light, noise, and vehicular pollution into pristine reserves.
  • The "Long-Haul Flight Carbon Paradox": Most affluent eco-tourists travel thousands of miles on long-haul flights from North America or Europe to reach remote wilderness areas (e.g. the Amazon, Costa Rica, or Borneo). The massive carbon footprint of these flights contributes directly to global climate change, overshadowing local conservation benefits.
  • Cultural Commercialisation: Exposing isolated indigenous communities to constant tourist scrutiny can commodify ancient sacred rituals, erode traditional cultural values, and create economic dependencies on gifts or tourist tips.

b. Exemplar Definition of Sustainable Tourism:

"Sustainable tourism is tourism that takes full account of its current and future economic, social, and environmental impacts, addressing the needs of visitors, the industry, the environment, and host communities."
— United Nations World Tourism Organization (UNWTO)

Comprehensive Student Definition & The Three Core Pillars:

"Sustainable tourism is visiting a destination in a manner that actively preserves and enhances natural ecosystems, respects and celebrates local cultural heritage, and generates equitable, long-term economic prosperity for local host communities without compromising the ability of future generations to enjoy the same resources."

  • Pillar 1: Environmental Conservation (Planet):
    • Minimising resource consumption: using solar/renewable energy, conserving scarce freshwater, and adopting zero-single-use-plastic policies.
    • Preserving natural biodiversity and habitats by strictly limiting visitor footprints within carrying capacities.
  • Pillar 2: Socio-Cultural Integrity (People):
    • Preserving local cultural heritage, historic architecture, and traditional ways of life without commercial trivialisation.
    • Empowering residents by giving them a democratic voice in local tourism development decisions and protecting local housing from tourist displacement.
  • Pillar 3: Economic Equity & Viability (Prosperity):
    • Ensuring that tourism revenues remain within the local economy (minimising economic leakage) by employing local guides and purchasing food and crafts directly from local farmers and artisans.
    • Providing stable, year-round, fairly paid employment opportunities with proper working conditions.
