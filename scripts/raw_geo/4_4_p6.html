TASK 1
  1. Explain how the evacuation of an earthquake zone can help to reduce the impacts.
  2. In pairs, discuss how prediction, planning, and prevention (PPP) could decrease the need for the identified responses to tectonic hazards. Write a 200 to 250-word summary to show what you think the impact of a good PPP system would be on the required responses to a tectonic event.
  3. In pairs, discuss why PPP is important in the management of tectonic hazards. Consider the role that each 'P' plays in the protection of the population and infrastructure from tectonic hazards.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. How evacuation of an earthquake zone can help to reduce impacts:

  • Evacuation removes people from the most dangerous areas before or immediately after a tectonic event, directly reducing the number of casualties (deaths and injuries).
  • Timely evacuation means fewer people are trapped under collapsed buildings — reducing the demand for search and rescue operations and allowing emergency services to focus resources.
  • Evacuating hospitals, schools, and residential areas in advance of a predicted volcanic eruption or aftershock sequence prevents mass casualties in high-occupancy buildings.
  • However, the effectiveness of evacuation depends on: sufficient warning time, clear evacuation routes, public trust in authorities, and the willingness of the population to comply (some may refuse to leave, as seen in the Mount Agung case).

b. How Prediction, Planning, and Prevention (PPP) could decrease the need for emergency responses:

  • Prediction: Using seismographs, GPS ground deformation sensors, gas emission monitors, and satellite imagery, scientists can issue early warnings for volcanic eruptions and, to a lesser extent, earthquakes. Effective prediction allows authorities to evacuate populations before a disaster strikes, eliminating the need for large-scale emergency search-and-rescue and mass casualty response.
  • Planning: Pre-disaster planning includes creating and practising evacuation plans, stockpiling emergency food/water/medical supplies, training search and rescue teams, and establishing clear communication protocols. Well-prepared communities can respond far more efficiently and with less chaos, reducing fatalities and the scale of emergency response needed.
  • Prevention (mitigation): Enforcing earthquake-resistant building codes, retrofitting older structures, constructing tsunami sea walls, and using GIS hazard mapping to restrict development in high-risk zones all reduce the physical damage caused. If buildings do not collapse, emergency services are not overwhelmed with trapped casualties.
  • Overall impact of good PPP: A strong PPP system transforms tectonic disasters from unmanageable catastrophes into manageable events. The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan demonstrated that PPP can save lives even in major M9.0 earthquakes: Japan's strict building codes, tsunami warning systems, and community tsunami drills significantly reduced casualties compared to what a similarly powerful earthquake would cause in an unprepared nation.

c. Why PPP is important in the management of tectonic hazards:

  • Prediction provides the time to act — without prediction, populations cannot be warned, and evacuation cannot begin before lives are lost.
  • Planning provides the system — it ensures that when a hazard occurs, every person knows what to do, emergency services are ready, and supplies are in place. Without planning, even an accurate prediction is useless if there is no organised evacuation route.
  • Prevention provides the foundation — by reducing vulnerability through engineering and land-use planning, prevention ensures that even if prediction fails and planning is imperfect, the physical impact of the hazard is limited.
  • Together, PPP forms an integrated risk reduction cycle that is far more effective than any single approach alone.
TASK 2

  1. Research different prediction strategies to identify a tectonic risk.
  2. Using your research, create a poster showing three prediction strategies that you have identified. Your poster should explain how they can be used to reduce the impacts of a tectonic hazard.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

Three prediction strategies for identifying tectonic risk (for your poster):

  1. Seismograph Networks and Earthquake Monitoring:
    • Networks of seismographs measure ground vibrations constantly. A sudden increase in the frequency of small foreshocks in a region may indicate an imminent major earthquake or volcanic eruption.
    • In volcanic areas, rising magma causes characteristic patterns of micro-earthquakes (harmonic tremor) detectable on seismographs, providing advance warning of an eruption.
    • Impact: Allows authorities to issue alerts and begin evacuation before the major event, potentially saving thousands of lives.
  2. Ground Deformation Monitoring (GPS and Tiltmeters):
    • As magma rises or tectonic stress builds, the ground surface swells or tilts measurably. GPS sensors and tiltmeters placed on volcano flanks or along fault lines detect millimetre-scale changes in ground position.
    • In 2017, ground deformation data at Mount Agung (Bali, Indonesia) showed significant inflation of the volcanic edifice weeks before the eruption, enabling authorities to order the evacuation of 100,000 people.
    • Impact: Provides continuous real-time data, giving scientists time to raise alert levels and evacuate communities before eruptions occur.
  3. Gas Emission Monitoring:
    • As magma rises towards the surface, it releases sulphur dioxide (SO₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂), and hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) gases. Measuring increases in these gas emissions using spectrometers (COSPEC, FTIR) provides early warning of rising volcanic activity.
    • A dramatic spike in SO₂ emissions was detected at Mount Pinatubo (Philippines) in 1991, contributing to the decision to evacuate 60,000 people — one of the most successful volcanic evacuations in history.
    • Impact: Gas monitoring, combined with seismic and deformation data, creates a comprehensive early warning system that dramatically reduces the death toll from eruptions.

  • Fifth and sixth earthquake: 1966 − 1934 = 32 years
  • Note: Students should use the exact data from Source A — answers above are indicative based on known Parkfield earthquake history.

c. Why frequency cannot be used as reliable data to predict the next earthquake:

  • The intervals between Parkfield earthquakes vary considerably: 24 years, 20 years, 21 years, 12 years, 32 years, 38 years (1966 to 2004).
  • This variation demonstrates that earthquake recurrence intervals are irregular and unpredictable — despite being on the same fault segment, they do not occur at uniform time intervals.
  • Predicting "the next earthquake will occur in approximately X years" based on average interval would have failed for the 2004 earthquake (which was ~38 years after 1966, longer than any previous interval).
  • Tectonic stress accumulation on a fault is influenced by many complex variables (local geology, fluid pressure, coupling with adjacent fault segments) that cannot be captured by a simple time-interval pattern.

d. Is it possible to predict the magnitude of the next earthquake?

  • Partially — but with very limited reliability.
  • The Parkfield data shows that all six earthquakes were approximately M6.0. This consistency suggests that the segment consistently ruptures when it accumulates enough stress to produce a ~M6 event. In this sense, there is a rough expectation of magnitude.
  • However, this does not mean it is reliably predictable: the exact magnitude could be slightly higher or lower, and a future event on an adjacent segment could be much larger or smaller.
  • Scientists cannot currently predict earthquake magnitude with certainty — the same fault segment could produce a smaller swarm of M4–5 events or a single M7+ event depending on how far the rupture propagates.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. How flexible 'springs' in the foundation help protect the building during an earthquake:

  • This technique is known as base isolation. The flexible springs (or rubber bearings) absorb and dampen the seismic energy transmitted upward from the ground.
  • When the ground shakes, the isolators allow the building's foundation to move independently of the structure above — the building effectively "floats" on the springs.
  • This significantly reduces the amount of lateral (horizontal) seismic force that reaches the building's frame and walls, preventing structural damage and collapse.
  • The spring system can absorb seismic energy that would otherwise cause catastrophic failure of rigid, inflexible structures.

b. How being one storey high reduces the risk of injury and death:

  • Single-storey buildings contain far less mass than multi-storey buildings. During an earthquake, less mass = less inertial force acting on the structure, reducing the risk of collapse.
  • If a single-storey building does collapse, the maximum height of falling debris is limited — people inside face less risk of being buried under many storeys of rubble, improving survival chances.
  • Evacuation from a single-storey building is faster: there are no stairs to navigate, and all occupants are at ground level, enabling quicker escape when shaking begins.
  • Single-storey structures also tend to have simpler, more robust structural designs that are better able to withstand lateral seismic forces.

c. If a tsunami was triggered, what risk does this building pose to inhabitants?

  • A single-storey building is extremely vulnerable to a tsunami — it provides no elevated refuge for inhabitants. Tsunami waves can be several metres high, completely inundating a one-storey structure.
  • Inhabitants inside cannot climb to an upper floor to escape the floodwater. If the wave arrives quickly, there may be no time to evacuate to higher ground.
  • The building could be destroyed or swept away by the tsunami's force, killing those inside who survived the initial earthquake.
  • In tsunami-prone coastal areas, multi-storey buildings or designated tsunami evacuation towers are essential for life safety.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Meaning of the term 'lahar':

  • A lahar is a fast-moving flow of volcanic debris (ash, rock, and other material) mixed with water, typically occurring on or near volcanoes. It behaves like liquid concrete and can travel many kilometres down river valleys.
  • Lahars can be triggered by heavy rainfall washing loose volcanic ash off the volcano flanks, or by the rapid melting of snow and ice during an eruption.

b. Areas at highest risk from the eruption of volcano Villarrica:

  • With reference to Source C (the GIS hazard map), the areas at highest risk are those closest to the volcano — particularly the valleys and river channels that radiate outward from the summit.
  • Low-lying valleys on all sides of Villarrica are at very high risk from lahars, as water and ash mix on the slopes and flow rapidly down drainage channels.
  • Areas to the immediate east and west of the summit show high risk from lava flows, which travel along the steepest gradients.
  • The town of Pucón, located to the north on the shore of Lake Villarrica, is within a significant risk zone for both lahars and lava flows — a highly populated area at serious risk.

c. How the main airport being in Pucón may increase the risk of managing the evacuation:

  • Pucón itself is in a high-risk zone close to the volcano. If the volcano erupts and threatens Pucón, the airport — the main hub for mass evacuation — could itself be threatened by ash fall, lahars, or lava flows, potentially forcing its closure at the critical moment when it is most needed.
  • Ash fall from the eruption would make the runway dangerous and reduce visibility, grounding aircraft precisely when air evacuation of tourists, elderly, and mobility-impaired people is most urgently needed.
  • Road access to the airport from surrounding villages leads through potential lahar flow paths — if roads are blocked by lahars, evacuees cannot reach the airport.
  • This creates a dangerous bottleneck: the primary evacuation infrastructure is located in one of the highest-risk zones, making the overall evacuation plan far more difficult to execute safely.

d. Three pieces of advice to survive a volcanic eruption:

  1. Evacuate immediately when told to do so: Follow official instructions and evacuation orders without delay. Do not return to collect belongings — pyroclastic flows and lahars can travel at hundreds of km/h and provide virtually no survival time once they arrive.
  2. Protect yourself from ash: Wear a properly fitted N95 mask (or improvise with a damp cloth) to avoid inhaling volcanic ash, which can cause serious lung damage. Wear goggles to protect your eyes, and cover all exposed skin with long-sleeved clothing to prevent ash burns.
  3. Move uphill or to high ground and stay out of river valleys: Lahars travel rapidly along river channels and valleys. Moving to elevated terrain away from drainage channels dramatically reduces the risk of being caught in a lahar or lava flow.
