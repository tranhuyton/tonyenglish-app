TASK 1
  1. Create a spider diagram that shows how the environmental, social, and economic impacts of different energy sources are interconnected. Look for what impacts occur and how they relate to each category.
  2. In pairs, carry out research to identify three cities around the world that suffer from high levels of smog. Give reasons for why these particular cities suffer from smog and then compare your findings with others in your class.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Spider Diagram: Interconnected Environmental, Social, and Economic Impacts of Energy Sources:

Central Hub: Interconnected Impacts of Energy Production and Use

Branch 1: Environmental Impacts (Driving Social & Economic Consequences):

  • Air pollution (SO₂, NOx, particulates): Forms toxic smog and acid rain → Social impact: Triggers respiratory illnesses (asthma, chronic bronchitis) and eye irritation → Economic impact: Heavy healthcare costs and lost working days.
  • Greenhouse Gas Emissions (CO₂): Drives global climate change → Environmental impact: Sea-level rise, severe droughts, intensified cyclones → Social impact: Food and water insecurity, climate migration → Economic impact: Trillions in infrastructure damage and crop losses.
  • Habitat Destruction: Opencast coal mining and oil spills (e.g. Niger Delta) devastate forests and marine ecosystems → Social impact: Destroys indigenous livelihoods → Economic impact: Collapse of local fisheries and ecotourism.

Branch 2: Economic Impacts (Influencing Social Well-being):

  • Reliable & Affordable Energy Supply: Fuels industrial manufacturing, transport, and commerce → Economic impact: Drives GDP growth, creates employment, generates tax revenues.
  • Energy Price Volatility: Sudden fossil fuel price spikes → Social impact: Pushes low-income households into severe fuel poverty (having to choose between food and heat).
  • High Capital Costs for Renewables: Building solar/wind farms or nuclear plants requires billions in upfront capital → May increase energy bills temporarily in the short term, but provides free fuel and price stability long-term.

Branch 3: Social Impacts (Feedback into Economic & Environmental Spheres):

  • Public Health Crisis from Fossil Fuels: High morbidity and premature mortality reduce labor productivity and strain national healthcare budgets.
  • Energy Access & Quality of Life: Access to electricity allows refrigeration of vaccines/food, night-time study for children, and reduces indoor smoke from burning fuelwood.
  • Just Transition for Coal Workers: Phasing out coal mines creates structural unemployment and poverty in mining towns unless governments fund retraining for renewable sector jobs.

b. Three cities suffering from high levels of smog and reasons why:

  1. New Delhi, India:
    • Reasons: Heavy concentration of coal-fired thermal power plants; millions of vehicles using low-grade diesel; industrial emissions; seasonal burning of agricultural crop stubble (paddy residue) by farmers in neighbouring Punjab and Haryana.
    • Geographical/Climatic factor: Situated in an inland basin. During winter, a strong temperature inversion traps cold, stagnant air and pollutants close to the ground, preventing smoke from dispersing and creating hazardous "gas chamber" smog levels (AQI > 500).
  2. Beijing, China:
    • Reasons: Surrounding Hebei province has historically had the world's densest concentration of coal-fired steel mills, cement factories, and coal power stations; massive motor vehicle traffic (over 6 million cars).
    • Geographical factor: Beijing is bordered to the north and west by the Yan Mountains. Southern winds push industrial pollutants toward the city, where they become trapped against the mountain barriers under low-pressure winter inversion layers. (China's "War on Pollution" has significantly improved air quality recently through coal bans and EV adoption).
  3. Lahore, Pakistan (or Los Angeles, USA):
    • Reasons: Low-grade fuel emissions from industrial brick kilns, steel mills, and two-stroke rickshaws; widespread burning of municipal solid waste; crop stubble burning.
    • Climatic factor: Calm winter wind patterns and inversion layers trap thick, yellowish toxic smog for weeks, forcing school closures and flight cancellations.
TASK 2: Study Source A

Health hazards caused by coal power stations

A country that currently produces most of its electricity from coal power is looking at expanding the number of coal-fired power stations. The long-term air pollution caused by these power stations is a burden on the healthcare system. The air pollution that the power stations cause results in the death of approximately 5000 people a year and leads to almost 3000 early births of babies each year.

Almost 27,000 cases of bronchitis in children are caused by the pollution from the power plants every year, and as many as 1.48 million working days are lost each year due to the population being ill. Pollution from these power stations leads to heart and lung disease and increases the risk of many other types of diseases.

The government has found that almost half of the €10.9 billion spent on health care each year is spent on people living near the coal power stations.

Source A: Newspaper article about coal power stations

  1. In pairs, read through the article on the health hazards caused by coal power stations. Then discuss if you think moving towards cleaner energy sources would be better for:
    1. The health of the population
    2. The health of the environment
    3. The stability of the economy.
  2. Write a 200 to 300-word letter to the government motivating for a change in the source of energy in the country.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Impact of moving towards cleaner energy sources:

  • i. Health of the population: Greatly improved. Transitioning away from coal eliminates fine particulate matter (PM2.5), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and heavy metals (mercury). This will directly save approximately 5,000 lives annually, prevent 3,000 premature births, and eliminate 27,000 cases of childhood bronchitis, dramatically reducing asthma, lung cancer, and cardiovascular hospitalisations.
  • ii. Health of the environment: Coal is the single largest emitter of carbon dioxide (CO₂). Phasing out coal power stations will drastically cut national greenhouse gas emissions, directly contributing to global climate change mitigation. Furthermore, eliminating sulfur and nitrogen emissions will end the acid rain that acidifies freshwater lakes, leaches toxic aluminium from soils, and defoliates surrounding forests.
  • iii. Stability of the economy: While upfront capital investment for renewable power (solar PV, wind turbines, grid batteries) is substantial, clean energy delivers immense net economic returns:
    • Recovers nearly €5.5 billion annually in healthcare expenditures currently drained by treating illnesses near coal stations.
    • Recovers 1.48 million lost working days each year, boosting national industrial productivity and GDP.
    • Shields the national economy from volatile global coal import prices and international carbon border adjustment taxes.
    • Creates skilled, long-term employment in renewable installation, engineering, and maintenance.

b. Model Letter to the Government (approx. 270 words):

To the Minister for Energy and Climate Affairs,
Re: Urgent Appeal to Halt Coal Expansion and Transition to Clean Renewable Energy

Dear Minister,

I am writing to express grave concern regarding the government's proposal to expand our domestic coal-fired power stations. While reliable electricity is fundamental for economic progress, expanding coal power represents an unacceptable and unsustainable burden on our citizens' health, national finances, and environment.

Official healthcare statistics demonstrate the catastrophic toll of our existing coal infrastructure. Toxic air pollution from these plants claims approximately 5,000 lives annually, causes nearly 3,000 premature births, and inflicts chronic bronchitis on 27,000 innocent children every year. Beyond the profound human suffering, this constitutes a severe economic drain. Treating illnesses directly attributable to coal emissions consumes nearly half of our nation’s €10.9 billion annual healthcare budget, while 1.48 million working days are lost each year due to sickness—severely dampening industrial productivity.

Continuing to invest in 19th-century fossil fuel technology when the costs of solar photovoltaic, onshore wind, and modern energy storage have plummeted makes neither environmental nor fiscal sense. The billions currently squandered on treating avoidable respiratory disease should instead be redirected as capital investment into clean, domestic renewable infrastructure. Clean energy will permanently eliminate the healthcare deficit, restore our workforce's vitality, protect our waterways from acid precipitation, and ensure national energy sovereignty independent of fossil fuel markets.

I respectfully urge the government to cancel all proposed coal expansions immediately and publish a binding clean energy transition roadmap for a healthier and prosperous future.

Yours sincerely,
A Concerned Citizen and Geography Student

TASK 3: Study Source B
Source B: Global energy consumption per capita by resource in 1965 and 2022 (in kWh)
  1. Which non-renewable resource showed the largest increase between 1965 and 2022?
  2. Which renewable energy resource was already being used in 1965?
  3. In 1965, the total estimated energy consumption per capita (per person) was 12,975 kWh, while in 2022 it was 20,889 kWh.
    1. Calculate the difference in energy consumption per capita between 1965 and 2022.
    2. Suggest why energy consumption per capita has increased so significantly between 1965 and 2022.
  4. Describe and explain the change in energy mix between 1965 and 2022.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Non-renewable resource with largest increase (1965–2022):

  • Oil showed the largest absolute increase, expanding from ~4,800 kWh per person in 1965 to over 6,800 kWh per person in 2022.
  • Natural Gas experienced the fastest proportional surge among non-renewables, rising from ~2,000 kWh per capita in 1965 to over 5,000 kWh per capita in 2022 (a massive 150% increase).

b. Renewable energy resource already in use in 1965:

  • Hydroelectric Power (HEP) was already well-established in 1965 (represented by the light blue diagonal hatched bar segment at the end of the 1965 bar). Modern wind and solar power were virtually non-existent at utility scale in 1965.

c. Energy consumption calculations and explanation:

  • i. Calculation of the difference:
    20,889 kWh (in 2022) − 12,975 kWh (in 1965) = 7,914 kWh per person increase (an overall increase of +61.0% per capita).
    Note: Because global population also grew from 3.3 billion to 8.0 billion over this period, total global energy consumption actually multiplied more than fourfold!
  • ii. Reasons for the dramatic per capita increase:
    • Industrialisation of Emerging Economies (MICs): The rapid economic rise of densely populated nations, notably China, India, and Southeast Asia, shifted hundreds of millions of people into energy-intensive industrial manufacturing and electrified urban life.
    • Rising Global Living Standards & Urbanisation: Mass adoption of domestic electrical appliances (refrigerators, washing machines, microwaves, televisions), personal computers, smartphones, and especially energy-hungry air conditioning in warming climates.
    • Explosion in Motorised Transport & Aviation: Vast expansion in private car ownership, inter-city road haulage, and affordable international commercial aviation, consuming immense volumes of refined oil fuels.

d. Describe and explain the change in energy mix between 1965 and 2022:

  • Description of the change:
    • 1965: The global mix was heavily concentrated in just three sources: Coal (~47%), Oil (~37%), and minor Natural Gas and HEP. Nuclear and modern renewables (solar, wind) were non-existent.
    • 2022: The total bar is 61% longer. Fossil fuels still dominate (~80% combined), but the composition has diversified substantially: Natural Gas has grown to rival coal; Nuclear power has become a major contributor; and modern renewables (solar PV, onshore/offshore wind, biofuels) now form a rapidly expanding segment.
  • Explanation of the change:
    • Rise of Natural Gas: Driven by advancements in combined-cycle gas turbines, hydraulic fracturing, and Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) tankers, making gas preferred over coal because it burns cleaner (emitting 50% less CO₂ and no sulfur).
    • Advent of Nuclear Energy: Commercial deployment surged from the 1970s onwards (accelerated by the 1973 oil crisis) as nations sought carbon-free, high-density baseload power.
    • Technological Breakthroughs in Renewables: Massive manufacturing economies of scale (especially in China) caused the levelised cost of solar panels and wind turbines to plummet by 80–90% between 2010 and 2022, making them cheaper than new fossil fuel plants.
    • International Climate Agreements: Global environmental treaties (Kyoto Protocol, Paris Agreement) and government carbon pricing spurred rapid investments into clean energy diversification.
