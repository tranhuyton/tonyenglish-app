TASK 1

As globalisation increases, companies expand into new regions and need to be aware of different cultural values across regions.

In pairs, pick two countries or regions.

  1. a Discuss the possible differences in consumer culture.
  2. b Suggest how these differences would affect the relationship between a business from one country and its consumers and employees from the other country.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Sample chosen countries: Western country (e.g., UK/USA) and an Asian or Middle Eastern country (e.g., China, India, or Saudi Arabia).

a) Differences in consumer culture:

  • Dietary preferences and religious customs: Western diets often center around beef, dairy, and wheat, whereas in India beef is avoided for religious reasons (sacred to Hindus), and in Muslim-majority countries all meat must be Halal. Asian cuisines generally favor rice, soy, pork, and spicy flavors.
  • Shopping habits and payment methods: Consumers in countries like China overwhelmingly use mobile wallets (WeChat Pay, Alipay) and live-stream shopping, while Western consumers still rely heavily on credit/debit cards or cash.
  • Perception of foreign brands: In some emerging economies (LICs/MICs), Western brands are perceived as status symbols reflecting prestige and modern lifestyle, whereas in HICs they may simply be viewed as convenient everyday items.

b) Impact on relationships with consumers and employees:

  • Relationship with consumers: Businesses must practice glocalisation (adapting global products to suit local tastes and norms). Failure to respect cultural values can result in consumer boycotts, marketing failures, or accusations of cultural insensitivity.
  • Relationship with employees: Workplace culture varies considerably—for example, attitudes toward hierarchy (strict deference to managers in some Asian cultures vs. flatter, open feedback in Western companies), expectations around working hours, work-life balance, and religious observances (e.g., designated prayer times, Ramadan, or lunar calendar festivals). Management must adopt culturally sensitive leadership to maintain high morale and avoid industrial disputes.
TASK 2

Study Source A.

Source A: McDonald’s restaurant in Yangshuo Old Town tourist centre, Guangxi Province, China

As the world’s largest restaurant chain, serving almost 70 million customers every day in over 100 countries, McDonald’s has impacted economies and cultures throughout the world.

Referring to Source A:

  1. a Explain how McDonald’s is an example of globalisation.
  2. b Discuss the social, economic, and environmental benefits and challenges that globalisation brings to:
    1. McDonald’s
    2. the areas around the world in which McDonald’s operates.
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) How McDonald’s is an example of globalisation:

  • Worldwide brand presence: Originating in the United States, McDonald's has expanded across international borders into over 100 countries, serving nearly 70 million customers daily.
  • Evidence from Source A: The photograph demonstrates how American commercial culture and corporate branding (the iconic Golden Arches logo) have penetrated even ancient, culturally sensitive locations like Yangshuo Old Town in Guangxi, China, sitting alongside traditional architectural styles, historic stone bridges, and karst limestone mountains.
  • Cultural exchange & glocalisation: McDonald’s adapts its global menu and operational strategy to integrate local Chinese tastes (e.g., offering congee, taro pies, and localized drinks) while retaining its global brand identity.

b) Benefits and challenges of globalisation:

i) To McDonald’s:

  • Benefits: Access to vast new consumer markets (e.g., China’s 1.4 billion population); economies of scale lowering ingredient and packaging costs; reduced dependence on the saturated domestic US market; high global brand recognition.
  • Challenges: Stiff competition from domestic fast-food competitors; political/trade tensions between countries; vulnerability to supply chain disruptions and currency fluctuations; costs of customizing products (glocalisation); brand image risks regarding public health and obesity.

ii) To the areas around the world where McDonald’s operates:

  • Social:
    • Benefits: Provides convenient, clean, air-conditioned dining spaces; creates flexible part-time and entry-level employment for youth and students.
    • Challenges: Contributes to health issues such as obesity and cardiovascular disease due to high sugar/fat/salt content; causes cultural erosion and homogenization ("McDonaldization") of local food traditions.
  • Economic:
    • Benefits: Generates foreign direct investment (FDI); creates jobs in restaurant operations, logistics, delivery, and local agriculture/food processing; pays taxes to local governments.
    • Challenges: Outcompetes and displaces local independent street vendors and family restaurants; large portions of corporate profits are repatriated back to the USA rather than retained locally.
  • Environmental:
    • Benefits: McDonald's global sustainability pledges (e.g., transitioning to 100% recycled/certified packaging, energy-efficient store equipment) can encourage higher local green standards.
    • Challenges: High volume of single-use packaging generates litter around tourist destinations; large carbon and water footprints associated with intensive livestock farming, global logistics, and refrigeration.
TASK 3

Split the class in two for a debate. One side should argue that TNCs are good for LICs. The other side should argue that TNCs are bad for LICs.

Remember that you can use some points both for and against. For example:

  • increase revenue into the region
  • technology development in the region
  • increased tax revenue for government
  • offers new employment opportunities.
💡 View Suggested Answers (Debate Preparation Guide)

Arguments FOR: TNCs are GOOD for LICs

  • Direct Job Creation: TNCs provide reliable paid employment in manufacturing, assembly, and service centers for thousands of local people, lifting households out of absolute poverty.
  • Technology & Skills Transfer: Introduces modern machinery, digital inventory systems, and managerial expertise. Local workers acquire valuable vocational skills that can spread across the domestic economy.
  • Infrastructure Investment: TNCs often invest in or demand improvements to local transport links (ports, roads, railways), electricity grids, and telecommunications, benefiting the wider region.
  • Government Tax Revenue: Taxes paid on profits and worker incomes can be utilized by the host government to finance healthcare, public education, and welfare programs.
  • Multiplier Effect: Local businesses gain contracts to supply materials, catering, packaging, security, and transport to the TNC, boosting the entire regional economy.

Arguments AGAINST: TNCs are BAD for LICs

  • Low Wages & Sweatshop Conditions: TNCs often choose LICs to exploit low minimum wages and lax labour regulations, leading to hazardous working conditions, long hours, and poor worker rights.
  • Profit Repatriation (Leakage): The majority of profits are sent back to company headquarters in HICs rather than reinvested into the host community or country.
  • Environmental Exploitation: TNCs take advantage of weak environmental laws in LICs, causing air pollution, water contamination from untreated industrial effluents, and illegal dumping of toxic waste.
  • Economic Vulnerability ("Footloose"): TNCs have no deep loyalty to the host nation. If labour costs rise or another country offers larger tax breaks, factories can close overnight, leaving mass unemployment.
  • Displacement of Local Firms: Domestic manufacturers and artisan producers cannot compete with the massive marketing budgets and economies of scale of TNCs, causing local closures.
TASK 4
  1. a Define and explain the term Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR).
  2. b Using an example of a TNC that you have studied, briefly describe their CSR strategy and evaluate what the impact of this is on society.
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) Definition and explanation of Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR):

  • Definition: Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) is a self-regulating business model that helps a company be socially accountable to itself, its stakeholders, and the public.
  • Explanation: Rather than solely focusing on maximizing short-term profits for shareholders, a company with an active CSR policy takes responsibility for the social, ethical, and environmental impacts of its operations. This includes ensuring fair treatment and living wages for employees throughout global supply chains, eliminating child/forced labour, minimizing carbon footprints and industrial waste, and investing in local community initiatives such as education and clean water.

b) Case Study Example & Evaluation: H&M Group (or Nike / Apple):

  • CSR Strategy Description:
    • Environmental initiatives: In 2013, H&M introduced one of the world's largest garment collecting programs, collecting over 155,000 tonnes of used clothing in store drop-boxes to promote textile recycling and circular fashion, aiming to use 100% recycled or sustainably sourced materials.
    • Social initiatives: Programs aimed at promoting gender equality, female workplace empowerment, and skill development in manufacturing supplier countries like Bangladesh and Cambodia, alongside publishing supplier lists to enhance supply-chain transparency.
  • Evaluation of Societal Impact:
    • Positive impacts: Provides urban consumers worldwide with practical recycling access; raises consumer awareness about textile waste; provides vocational training and improved health/safety standards for garment workers in developing nations.
    • Critiques & Limitations: Critics argue that the underlying "fast-fashion" business model relies on continuous mass overproduction (billions of inexpensive garments annually), which consumes immense volumes of water and energy. Furthermore, sub-contracted factory workers in LICs still frequently face low wages and precarious working hours, leading some commentators to label fast-fashion CSR initiatives as "greenwashing" designed primarily to protect brand reputation.
