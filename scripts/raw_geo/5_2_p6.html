TASK 1

Study Source A: An annotated photograph that shows the extent of Jakobshavn Isbrae, a glacier on the west coast of Greenland, between 1851 and 2006.

Source A: The retreat of Jakobshavn Isbrae glacier, Greenland (1851 – 2006)
  1. Approximately how much did Jakobshavn Isbrae recede between 1851 and 2006?
  2. Which time period shows the greatest rate of melting over the timescale shown?
  3. Explain the impacts of melting glaciers due to global warming. Consider both local and global consequences.
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) Approximate glacier retreat distance (1851–2006):

  • Between 1851 and 2006, Jakobshavn Isbrae receded by approximately 40 to 45 km (acceptable range: 40 – 46 km).
  • Calculation method: Using the 10 km scale bar in the top right corner, measuring along the central axis of the fjord from the 1851 terminus line (bottom-left) to the 2006 calving front (top-right) yields approximately 4 to 4.5 times the length of the 10 km bar.

b) Time period showing greatest rate of melting / retreat:

  • Time period: 2001 to 2006 (with the most dramatic acceleration occurring between 2001 and 2003).
  • Evidence of rate: Over just 5 years (2001–2006), the glacier retreated by roughly 10 to 12 km, representing an average rate of 2.0 to 2.4 km per year.
  • Comparison: Between 1851 and 1953 (102 years), the glacier receded ~25 km, averaging only ~0.25 km per year. The retreat rate in the early 2000s was therefore nearly 10 times faster than the historical average rate.

c) Impacts of melting glaciers (Local and Global consequences):

  • Local consequences:
    • Freshwater supply disruption: Glaciers act as natural frozen reservoirs. Rapid melting causes initial summer flooding and glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs), followed by severe long-term freshwater shortages for drinking, agriculture, and hydropower.
    • Ecological & hunting disruption: Heavy ice-melt into coastal fjords alters water salinity and temperature, disrupting local marine ecosystems (krill, fish stocks, seals) and threatening the traditional livelihoods of indigenous Greenlandic hunters and fishermen.
    • Geological hazards: Retreating ice exposes steep, unsupported rock walls, increasing the frequency of catastrophic rockfalls and landslides into fjords, potentially triggering local tsunamis.
  • Global consequences:
    • Eustatic sea-level rise: Land-based glacial ice melt directly transfers gigatons of meltwater into oceans, accelerating global sea-level rise. This leads to chronic coastal inundation, heightened storm surges, and the permanent displacement of low-lying communities (e.g. Bangladesh delta, Maldives, Pacific island nations).
    • Albedo positive feedback loop: Bright, reflective white ice (albedo ~0.8) is replaced by dark bedrock or open ocean water (albedo ~0.1). The darker surfaces absorb vastly more solar radiation, warming the region further and accelerating planet-wide heating.
    • Disruption of thermohaline circulation (AMOC): A massive discharge of cold freshwater into the North Atlantic reduces seawater density and suppresses deep-water sinking, threatening to slow or destabilize the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (Gulf Stream), which could trigger extreme weather shifts in Europe and North America.
TASK 2

Study Source B: Drought in Ethiopia has affected an estimated 24 million people since 2015.

Source B: Severe drought impacts and water shortages in Ethiopia
  1. Define the following key terms:
    • Glacial period
    • Drought
    • Climate change
    • Global warming potential
  2. In pairs, using Source B, explain what the short-term and long-term impacts of droughts could be on people. Categorise the long-term impacts of drought as either social, economic, or environmental. You could capture this information in a table.
  3. In your pairs, discuss if you think men and women are affected differently by drought. Carry out research to supplement your discussion.
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) Definitions of key terms:

  • i. Glacial period: An extended interval of geological time within an ice age characterized by colder global temperatures and the widespread advance and expansion of polar continental ice sheets and mountain glaciers (alternating with warmer interglacial periods).
  • ii. Drought: A prolonged, continuous period of abnormally low or deficient precipitation compared to the regional statistical average, leading to severe hydrological deficits, depleted soil moisture, and water supply shortages.
  • iii. Climate change: A long-term, sustained shift and alteration in global or regional temperatures, precipitation patterns, and extreme weather event frequencies, predominantly driven since the Industrial Revolution by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions.
  • iv. Global warming potential (GWP): A standardized relative index measuring how much heat (infrared energy) a specific greenhouse gas traps in the atmosphere over a set period (typically 100 years), expressed relative to the equivalent mass of carbon dioxide (CO₂, benchmarked at 1).

b) Short-term and long-term impacts of drought on people:

Short-term impacts: Acute drinking water shortages, rapid wilting and failure of seasonal subsistence crops, severe livestock dehydration and distress sales, rationing of domestic tap water, and initial reliance on emergency water trucking.

c) Differing impacts of drought on men and women (Gendered analysis):

  • Disproportionate burden on women and girls:
    • In rural subsistence communities, traditional gender roles assign domestic water fetching, cooking, and child care to women. During severe droughts, women and girls must trek 10–20 km daily under extreme heat to find water, leading to severe physical exhaustion, spinal injuries, and heightened danger of assault along isolated paths.
    • Girls are frequently withdrawn from school to help collect water, reinforcing cycles of gender educational disparity.
    • In food-scarce households, cultural customs often dictate that women eat last and smallest portions, resulting in acute maternal malnutrition and complications during pregnancy.
  • Impacts on men:
    • Men bear primary responsibility for livestock rearing and income. They are forced into dangerous, long-distance pastoral distress migrations with remaining animals or must leave families to seek precarious low-wage work in urban slums.
    • Heightened competition for remaining boreholes and water wells leads to violent clashes and armed resource conflict among rival pastoralist groups.
TASK 3
"The impacts of climate change on people are worse than the impacts on the environment."

Do you agree with this statement? Discuss in groups of four. Two people should argue for the impact on people being worse, and two people should argue for the impacts on the environment being worse. Remember to support your ideas with examples. After your discussion, come to a group conclusion and share your ideas with the rest of the class.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Debate Framework & Model Arguments:

1. Arguments that impacts on PEOPLE are worse:

  • Loss of life and health emergencies: Heatwaves (e.g. European heatwave of 2003 with 70,000 deaths), catastrophic flooding in Pakistan (2022, killing over 1,700 people), and the spread of vector-borne illnesses (malaria, dengue) into higher elevations directly harm human life.
  • Famine and economic ruin: Prolonged droughts decimate food supplies in the Sahel and Horn of Africa, pushing millions into starvation. Economic damage to homes, transport, and energy infrastructure costs trillions of dollars.
  • Displacement and geopolitical instability: Sea-level rise and desertification create hundreds of millions of "climate refugees", sparking cross-border migration pressures and violent resource wars.

2. Arguments that impacts on the ENVIRONMENT are worse:

  • Irreversible extinction and biodiversity collapse: Humans can use wealth and technology to adapt (air conditioning, sea walls), but wild animal and plant species cannot. Rising sea temperatures have caused catastrophic bleaching of over 50% of the Great Barrier Reef; warming accelerates the sixth mass extinction event.
  • Loss of irreplaceable biomes: Arctic tundra permafrost melting releases massive subterranean methane reserves; the Amazon rainforest risks passing a critical tipping point into dry savanna.
  • Permanent physical transformations: Ocean acidification chemically dissolves calcium carbonate shells and coral skeletons; sea-level rise permanently erodes delicate coastal mangrove and delta ecosystems.

3. Balanced Conclusion & Synthesis:

A high-scoring answer recognizes that the distinction between people and the environment is a false dichotomy. Human civilization is entirely dependent on functioning ecosystems for oxygen, pollination, freshwater purification, and soil fertility. Therefore, environmental destruction directly causes human suffering, and the two cannot be separated.

TASK 4

In pairs, conduct some research to create a fact file for a country that will benefit from the impacts of climate change, and a country that will suffer. Present your findings to the rest of the class and discuss if this is fair and what the solutions could be.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Fact File Case Studies: Benefiting vs. Suffering Country

Country Benefiting: Canada / Russia

  • Longer growing seasons: Warming allows northern expansion of agriculture, boosting grain and wheat crop yields.
  • Maritime shipping routes: Melting Arctic sea ice opens the Northwest Passage and Northern Sea Route, slashing transit times between Europe and Asia by 30–40%.
  • Resource accessibility: Thawing permafrost and retreating ice ease access to rich Arctic oil, gas, and mineral reserves.
  • Reduced winter energy demand: Warmer winters lower domestic space heating fuel consumption and cold-related mortality.

Country Suffering: Bangladesh / Tuvalu

  • Catastrophic sea-level rise: A 1-meter sea-level rise would inundate nearly 18% of Bangladesh, displacing over 25–30 million people.
  • Island submersion: Low-lying Pacific atoll nations like Tuvalu (max elevation ~4.5m) face complete loss of habitable land and statehood.
  • Saline intrusion: Saltwater enters delta soils and rivers, destroying drinking water and ruining staple rice agriculture.
  • Intensified extreme storms: Higher cyclone frequencies and storm surges destroy coastal dikes and flood millions of homes.

Discussion on Fairness (Climate Injustice) and Proposed Solutions:

  • The Ethical Injustice: Developing countries that suffer the most catastrophic losses have contributed negligible historical greenhouse gas emissions (Tuvalu <0.001%, Bangladesh ~0.4%), whereas wealthy industrialized nations generated the overwhelming majority of emissions to build their economic wealth.
  • International Solutions:
    • Loss and Damage Fund: Operationalize international climate reparation funds (established at COP27/28) where historic HIC emitters compensate vulnerable LICs for irreparable climate losses.
    • Technology Transfer & Adaptation Funding: Transfer green technology, climate-resilient crop seeds, and subsidized flood-defense engineering grants to frontline nations.
    • Legal Status for Climate Refugees: Establish formal international legal recognition and resettlement treaties under the UN for populations displaced by sea-level rise.
