Study Source A.

Source A: Different types of farming (Figures 10.1 - 10.5)
  1. a Which of the images represents subsistence farming? Explain your selection.
  2. b Which of the images do you think represents the most expensive type of farming to set up? Explain your selection.
  3. c What type of farming is represented in Figure 10.3? How does it differ from the farming in Figure 10.1, 10.2 and 10.4?
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) Subsistence farming:

Figure 10.5 represents subsistence farming (a woman working in the field growing food for her family). She is using manual labour and simple hand tools (a hoe) to produce crops intended purely for family survival and household consumption, with no large machinery or commercial sale. (Figure 10.1 also shows small-scale organic gardening for personal/family consumption).

b) Most expensive type of farming to set up:

Figure 10.4 (farming in greenhouse tunnels where climate and water is carefully controlled) represents the most expensive type of farming to set up. It requires extensive capital investment for construction of polytunnels/greenhouses, automated irrigation and misting systems, ventilation, sensors (temperature, humidity, light), and ongoing electrical power. (Figure 10.2 also requires very high capital for commercial arable machinery such as tractors and combine harvesters).

c) Farming in Figure 10.3 and how it differs:

  • Type of farming: Figure 10.3 represents pastoral farming (specifically extensive sheep farming / livestock rearing).
  • Differences: Figures 10.1, 10.2, and 10.4 are all examples of arable farming (cultivating plants and growing crops in soil or controlled greenhouse environments). In contrast, Figure 10.3 involves rearing animals (grazing sheep in open pastures) to produce wool, milk, and meat.
TASK 2

Study Sources B, C, and D.

Source B: An aeroponics system
Source C: A hydroponics system
Source D: An aquaponics system
  1. a What is the main benefit of aquaponics to a farmer, in comparison to aeroponics and hydroponics?
  2. b Name two similarities and one difference between hydroponics and aeroponics.
  3. c Discuss the limitations of these types of farming with a partner. Then, write 100–150 words explaining why a subsistence farmer may find it challenging to use a hydroponics, aquaponics or aeroponics system to grow crops.
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) Main benefit of aquaponics:

Aquaponics produces two food outputs from a single integrated system: crops (vegetables) and fish (a high-value source of protein). Additionally, it creates a closed-loop natural nutrient cycle: fish excrement is converted by beneficial bacteria into nitrates that feed the crops, which naturally filter and clean the water returned to the fish, substantially reducing the ongoing need and cost for purchased chemical nutrient solutions.

b) Similarities and difference between hydroponics and aeroponics:

  • Similarities:
    • Both are soilless farming methods that grow crops in controlled environments.
    • Both feed plants using mineral-rich, water-based nutrient solutions, using up to 95% less water than conventional agriculture.
  • Difference:
    • In hydroponics, plant roots are directly submerged in an oxygenated nutrient water bath beneath a floating platform. In aeroponics, plant roots are suspended in air and periodically sprayed with a fine nutrient mist via timed nozzles.

c) Why subsistence farmers face challenges with these systems:

Subsistence farmers face major barriers when attempting to implement soilless farming systems. First, the initial capital cost for pumps, pipes, storage tanks, sensors, and lighting is prohibitively high for families living on low incomes. Second, these systems require a continuous and reliable electricity supply; power cuts in rural LIC areas would cause misting nozzles or aeration pumps to stop, causing plant roots to dry out and die within hours. Third, they demand technical expertise to monitor and maintain water pH, electrical conductivity, and chemical nutrient balance. Lastly, spare parts and commercial nutrients are often unavailable in remote rural markets, making these advanced methods impractical for smallholders.

TASK 3

Study Source E.

Source E: A flow diagram showing the inputs, processes, and outputs of farming systems
  1. a Name two outputs a farmer could potentially get from farming chickens.
  2. b Describe how the inputs and outputs for subsistence farming may differ from the inputs and outputs from commercial farming.
  3. c Explain how an output from a farm can become an input to the same farm.
  4. d Do you think that meat should be represented as a standard output from a pastoral farm? Consider the process required to produce meat when giving your answer.
  5. e If you were to start a farm in your region, what do you think the main limitations would be? Consider the inputs, such as the space available, the climate, and the equipment you would need. What type of farming would you need to employ?
💡 View Suggested Answers

a) Outputs from chicken farming:

Eggs and meat (chicken/poultry). Other valid outputs include feathers and chicken manure (organic fertilizer).

b) Differences between subsistence and commercial farming inputs & outputs:

  • Inputs: Subsistence farming uses high human labour (family members), simple hand tools, saved seeds, and low capital. Commercial farming uses high capital investment, heavy machinery (tractors, harvesters), chemical fertilisers, pesticides, and hired labour.
  • Outputs: Subsistence farming outputs are modest and diverse, intended for direct consumption by the farmer’s family with little or no surplus. Commercial farming outputs are large, specialized (monoculture), and intended entirely for market sale and financial profit.

c) How outputs become inputs on the same farm:

Animal manure (an output of pastoral farming) is recycled and spread onto fields as organic fertiliser (an input for arable crops). Similarly, harvested grain or crop residue (output) can be fed to livestock (input), and harvested seeds can be saved and planted in the next growing season.

d) Whether meat should be represented as a standard output from a pastoral farm:

While meat is the ultimate commodity consumed by the public, most pastoral farms output live animals (cattle, sheep, pigs) which are transported to abattoirs and processing plants off-farm for slaughter. However, for an integrated farm that slaughters on-site, or when considering the agricultural system as a whole, meat is indeed the primary intended output of livestock rearing.

e) Local farming limitations and choice of farming type:

(Answers will vary according to local region): Key physical limitations typically include limited land area in urban/suburban zones, climate factors (heavy rainfall, typhoons, or hot dry seasons), and soil quality. In areas with constrained space and high land costs, intensive arable farming (such as polytunnel horticulture or market gardening) is most suitable to produce high-value vegetables year-round using controlled inputs.
