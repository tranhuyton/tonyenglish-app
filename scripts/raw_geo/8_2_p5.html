Activity 1

Table 8.6 A comparison of three different countries
  1. a) Define primary product dependency.
    b) What are the disadvantages of a country being primary product dependent?
  2. What do you understand by the terms:
    a) productive capabilities
    b) growth collapse?
  3. Compare LICs, MICs and HICs for three of the indicators in Table 8.6 (page 185).
  4. a) What is a tiger economy?
    b) Identify the factors responsible for the development of the first generation of tiger economies in the 1960s.
  5. In what ways do some of the HICs identified by the World Bank differ from the traditional developed countries, long recognised by the United Nations?
💡 View Suggested Answers
  1. a) Primary product dependency is an economic condition where a country relies heavily on one or a small number of primary products (like agricultural produce or raw minerals) for a large proportion of its export earnings.
    b) Disadvantages include vulnerability to global price fluctuations, limited value-addition compared to manufacturing, depletion of natural resources, and vulnerability to natural disasters or climate impacts that affect crop yields.
  2. a) Productive capabilities refer to the skills, infrastructure, technology, and capital that enable a country to produce goods and services efficiently and competitively.
    b) Growth collapse refers to a severe and prolonged economic downturn or reversal of development, often triggered by factors like falling commodity prices, debt crises, civil war, or political instability.
  3. For example: LICs typically have high primary employment, low GNI per capita, and low life expectancy. MICs have rapidly growing industrial sectors, medium GNI per capita, and rising life expectancy. HICs have dominant tertiary/quaternary sectors, very high GNI per capita, and high life expectancy.
  4. a) A tiger economy is the economy of a country which undergoes rapid economic growth, usually accompanied by an increase in the standard of living.
    b) The first generation of Asian tiger economies (e.g., South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong) developed in the 1960s due to factors like export-oriented industrialisation, significant investment in education, a disciplined and relatively low-cost workforce, state-led development policies, and advantageous geographical locations for trade.
  5. Some newer HICs (like wealthy Middle Eastern oil-exporting states) achieved high-income status primarily through the export of natural resources rather than broad-based industrialization. Consequently, they may still lag behind traditional developed countries in certain social indicators, technological diversification, or democratic institutions.

Activity 2

  1. What are the particular disadvantages for development in landlocked countries and small island countries?
  2. a) Define neo-colonialism.
    b) How can neo-colonialism hinder development in LICs?
  3. Briefly discuss the importance of trade and investment to economic development.
  4. Describe social investment.
  5. Draw a diagram to illustrate dependency theory.
  6. How important is access to modern technology for the development process?
💡 View Suggested Answers
  1. Landlocked countries lack direct access to seaports, making trade more expensive and slower due to reliance on neighboring countries' infrastructure. Small island countries suffer from isolation, high transport costs, limited land and resources, vulnerability to sea-level rise and extreme weather, and small domestic markets that make achieving economies of scale difficult.
  2. a) Neo-colonialism is the use of economic, political, cultural, or other pressures to control or influence other countries, especially former dependencies, maintaining an unequal power dynamic resembling colonialism.
    b) Neo-colonialism can hinder development by ensuring LICs remain suppliers of cheap raw materials and consumers of expensive manufactured goods from HICs. High debt burdens to foreign institutions, multinational corporations extracting profits without reinvesting locally, and conditional aid can all prevent genuine, self-sustaining development.
  3. Trade allows countries to earn foreign exchange, specialize in producing goods where they have a comparative advantage, and access broader markets. Investment provides the capital needed for building infrastructure, industrializing, acquiring technology, and creating jobs, thereby driving economic growth.
  4. Social investment refers to spending on public services such as education, healthcare, and housing that improve the wellbeing and productive capacity of the population, thereby fostering long-term social and economic development.
  5. Diagram description: The diagram typically shows a "Core" of wealthy, developed countries and a "Periphery" of poorer, developing countries. Arrows demonstrate the flow of cheap raw materials and cheap labor from the Periphery to the Core, and the flow of expensive manufactured goods and profit repatriation from the Core to the Periphery, illustrating a chain of exploitation.
  6. Access to modern technology is crucial because it can dramatically boost productivity (e.g., in agriculture and manufacturing), improve healthcare delivery, expand access to education, integrate local businesses into global markets, and enable 'leapfrogging' over older, dirtier, or more expensive technologies.
