5.1 The Natural and Human Causes of Climate Change

A rigorous scientific investigation into the mechanisms of Earth's climate system: distinguishing weather from climate, examining geological and instrumental evidence, unravelling Milankovitch orbital cycles and volcanic forcing, and analysing the human-driven enhanced greenhouse effect.

1

Weather, Climate & the Concept of Climate Change

In physical geography, precise scientific definitions are critical to avoid common misconceptions:

Weather

The day-to-day conditions of the atmosphere at a specific time and location (e.g., precipitation, temperature, wind velocity, atmospheric pressure, humidity). Highly variable and dynamic over hours or days.

Climate

The long-term statistical average of weather conditions calculated over a standardised reference period—traditionally at least 30 consecutive years (World Meteorological Organization standard).

Climate Change

A statistically significant and sustained variation in either the mean state of the climate or in its variability, persisting over extended periods (typically decades or longer), whether driven by natural cycles or anthropogenic forcing.

Natural Drivers of Climate Change

Earth's climate has constantly oscillated throughout the Quaternary Period (the past 2.6 million years) between cold glacials (ice ages) and warmer interglacials. Three primary natural mechanisms drive these oscillations:

1. Eccentricity (Orbital Shape): ~100,000 Years
Earth's orbit stretches from nearly circular to slightly elliptical. When the orbit is highly elliptical, the Earth receives approximately 23% more solar radiation at perihelion (closest approach) than at aphelion. This long cycle corresponds closely with the major 100,000-year glacial-interglacial pulses of the Quaternary period.

Volcanic Eruptions & Sulfate Aerosol Cooling

Major explosive volcanic eruptions inject massive plumes of ash, dust, and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) straight into the stratosphere. In the stratosphere, SO₂ reacts with water vapour to form highly reflective sulfate aerosols. These microscopic droplets scatter incoming shortwave solar radiation back into space, causing a net global cooling effect (volcanic winter) lasting 1 to 3 years.

Figure 5.6: Global temperature decline following the June 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines. The massive injection of sulfur aerosols lowered global mean temperatures by approximately 0.5°C for nearly two years.

Solar Variations & Sunspot Cycles

The sun's energy output varies on an 11-year solar cycle driven by magnetic sunspot activity. When sunspots are numerous, solar radiation increases slightly (by roughly 0.1%). Historical periods of prolonged low sunspot numbers, such as the Maunder Minimum (1645–1715), coincided with the coldest epochs of the Little Ice Age in the Northern Hemisphere.

Human Forcing: The Enhanced Greenhouse Effect (EGHE)

Figure 5.7: The fundamental physical distinction between the Natural Greenhouse Effect (left) and the Human Enhanced Greenhouse Effect (right).

⚡ Interactive Radiation Balance Simulator

The Natural Mechanism: Shortwave solar radiation passes through the atmosphere unhindered. Earth absorbs this radiation and re-emits it as longwave infrared heat. Naturally occurring greenhouse gases trap a fraction of this infrared energy, keeping global temperatures at an average of +15°C (without this, Earth would be a frozen -18°C).

The Anthropogenic Greenhouse Gases

Human activities release four primary heat-trapping greenhouse gases into the atmosphere:

Gas & Formula Main Anthropogenic Sources Atmospheric Lifetime Global Warming Potential (GWP - 100yr)
Carbon Dioxide (CO₂) Fossil fuel combustion (coal, oil, gas) in power plants and transport; deforestation (slash-and-burn, reduced carbon sink capacity); cement manufacturing. 300 to 1,000 years 1 (Reference standard)
Methane (CH₄) Enteric fermentation in livestock (cattle, sheep); anaerobic decomposition in flooded rice paddies; landfill waste sites; fugitive leaks from natural gas pipelines; thawing Arctic permafrost. c. 12 years 28 – 36 times more potent than CO₂
Nitrous Oxide (N₂O) Synthetic agricultural nitrogen fertilizers; livestock manure management; chemical synthesis of nylon and nitric acid; vehicle catalytic converters. c. 114 years 265 – 298 times more potent than CO₂
Halocarbons (CFCs & HFCs) Refrigeration systems, air-conditioning coolants, industrial aerosol propellants, foaming agents. Entirely synthetic (man-made). Up to 50,000 years 1,000 to 14,000 times more potent than CO₂

🎯 IGCSE Geographical Core Summary

  • Natural cycles operate on millennial timescales: Milankovitch cycles explain glacial-interglacial pacing over 20,000 to 100,000 years; volcanic aerosols produce short-term cooling for 1–3 years.
  • Human forcing is rapid and unprecedented: In just 150 years since the Industrial Revolution, human activities have driven CO₂ levels from 280 ppm to over 420 ppm.
  • The mechanism is radiative trapping: Human greenhouse gases absorb longwave terrestrial infrared radiation that would otherwise escape to space, re-radiating heat back down to Earth.
  • Methane and N₂O have high GWP: While CO₂ is the most abundant by volume, CH₄ and N₂O are far more potent heat-trappers per molecule.
