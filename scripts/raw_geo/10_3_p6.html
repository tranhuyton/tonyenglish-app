Source A: Farmer selling rain gauge during severe drought
  1. In pairs, discuss the message in the cartoon. Then, create a flow chart to show how the lack of need for a rain gauge on the farm impacts the farmer and food production in a region.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Cartoon message & Flow Chart of drought impacts:

  • Message of the Cartoon: The cartoon shows a farmer putting his rain gauge up for sale ("FOR SALE: Rain Gauge - As New / Never Used"). This is a satirical commentary on severe, prolonged meteorological drought. Rainfall has become so non-existent that measuring rain is futile, and the farmer is desperately selling off farming equipment simply to survive.
  • Flow Chart of Drought Impacts on the Farmer & Food Production:
[1. Prolonged Meteorological Drought] (Months/years of below-average rainfall & extreme heat)
   ↓
[2. Agricultural Drought & Water Depletion] (Soil moisture evaporates; irrigation reservoirs, rivers, and groundwater aquifers dry up)
   ↓
[3. Crop Failure & Livestock Mortality] (Staple crops wither before harvest; pasture grass dies; livestock perish from dehydration and starvation)
   ↓
[4. Farm Financial Collapse] (Farmer suffers 100% harvest loss, falls into debt with banks/seed lenders, sells off equipment/land, faces bankruptcy)
   ↓
[5. Regional Food Shortages & Hyper-Inflation] (Agricultural markets face supply collapse; food prices soar, making basic food unaffordable for low-income families)
   ↓
[6. Severe Malnutrition & Climate Out-Migration] (Hunger and disease spread; rural families abandon farmlands to migrate to urban slums or refugee camps in search of food aid).

b. Regional food production and recent weather impacts (e.g. Vietnam / Regional Case Studies):

  • Vietnam (Mekong River Delta): The "rice bowl" of Vietnam produces over 50% of the nation's rice and 70% of its fruit exports. In recent years, El Niño-induced prolonged droughts combined with upstream hydroelectric dams have caused record-low river discharge. This has triggered severe saltwater intrusion up to 60–90 km inland, destroying tens of thousands of hectares of paddy fields and fruit orchards, and creating drinking water crises for hundreds of thousands of households.
  • Central Highlands (Dak Lak / Gia Lai): Severe dry seasons cause drought in major commercial coffee and pepper plantations, forcing farmers to over-pump deep groundwater, causing the water table to plummet by dozens of meters.
  • Global examples (California, Spain, Horn of Africa): Heatwaves and multi-year droughts have forced mandatory water rationing for agriculture, leading to fallowed fields and soaring global olive oil, almond, and wheat prices.
TASK 2: Study Source B

The impacts of COVID-19 on world hunger

The COVID-19 pandemic started in 2019, and by 2021, at least 828 million people were affected by hunger. This was 150 million more than 2019 and 46 million more than 2020.

Source B: The impacts of COVID-19 on global food insecurity by region
  1. Which region experienced the largest change in food insecurity between 2019 and 2021?
  2. Describe the change in the number of people suffering from hunger from 2019 to 2021.
  3. In pairs, discuss the change that you observe in food insecurity by region, including the following areas:
    1. Did any of the data surprise you?
    2. Which regions had lower increases in food security than the world average, and which areas had higher increases?
    3. Give three suggestions for why these trends occurred.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Region with the largest change in food insecurity (2019–2021):

  • Based on Source B (UN FAO data), Latin America and the Caribbean experienced the sharpest percentage surge in moderate-to-severe food insecurity (jumping by over 9 percentage points from ~31.7% to over 40.6%).
  • Africa also experienced a massive absolute increase, rising from ~54.3% in 2019 to approximately 57.9% in 2021, maintaining by far the highest baseline level of food insecurity of any global continent.

b. Change in the number of people suffering from hunger (2019 to 2021):

  • In 2019 (pre-pandemic), approximately 678 million people were suffering from chronic hunger worldwide.
  • By 2021, this figure climbed to at least 828 million people.
  • This represents an increase of 150 million additional people pushed into hunger in just two years (with an increase of 104 million in 2020 alone, followed by another 46 million in 2021). This erased more than a decade of global progress toward UN Sustainable Development Goal 2 (Zero Hunger).

c. Regional Trends, Observations, and Explanations:

  • i. Surprising observations: Many students are surprised that even upper-middle-income regions like Latin America suffered such an explosive surge in hunger, showing how vulnerable urban populations are when service/informal sector jobs vanish overnight, even in relatively developed countries.
  • ii. Regions below vs. above world average:
    • Lower than world average increase: Northern America and Europe (remained very low and stable at ~8% due to extensive government financial support, unemployment stimulus, and food bank safety nets).
    • Higher than world average increase: Latin America & the Caribbean and Sub-Saharan Africa, where increases significantly exceeded the global average rate of change.
  • iii. Three major causes for these trends:
    1. Economic devastation in the informal labor sector: In LICs and MICs, a large percentage of workers rely on daily informal cash labor (street vendors, transport, market trade, construction). COVID-19 lockdowns and curfews eliminated household earnings instantly. Unlike in HICs, governments in developing nations lacked the fiscal resources to provide furlough schemes or cash stimulus, leaving families unable to buy food.
    2. Breakdown of food supply chains and border logistics: Border closures, grounded commercial aviation, port container shortages, and domestic movement restrictions prevented agricultural products from reaching markets, while migratory farmworkers could not travel to harvest seasonal crops, resulting in fresh food rotting in fields while city prices surged.
    3. Surging food inflation and rising agricultural input costs: Shipping freight rates multiplied up to fivefold, fuel prices rose, and key agricultural inputs (chemical fertilisers, seeds) became scarce. Higher production and import costs were passed directly to consumers, rendering basic staple foods unaffordable for the poorest 20% of the population.
TASK 3: Study Source C

Table 10.2: Techniques for protecting farmlands from degradation and soil erosion

  • Water management: Minimising overuse of water; selecting crops with low water requirements; preventing over-abstraction of groundwater.
  • Preventing total loss of vegetation: Controlling grazing intensity (stocking density) to protect soils from wind and water erosion.
  • Farmer training: Crop rotation to restore soil nutrients; contour ploughing; building stone bunds/terraces; planting windbreaks (rows of trees).

  3. Explain what a farmer could do to protect the soils in a dry region like this.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Definition of the term 'desertification':

"Desertification is the persistent degradation of land in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas resulting from various factors, including climatic variations (such as prolonged drought) and unsustainable human activities (such as overgrazing, deforestation, and overcultivation)."
— UN Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD)

b. Evidence of potential desertification in Source C (supported by Table 10.2):

  • Sparse, degraded vegetative cover: The photograph shows an extremely arid landscape with only patchy, dry, stunted shrubs and no continuous grass carpet. The vast majority of the topsoil is completely bare and directly exposed to the elements.
  • Evidence of overgrazing (human factor): A dense flock of sheep is grazing on the few remaining plants. Sheep have cleft lips that allow them to crop vegetation extremely close to the ground, often pulling up roots entirely. When too many animals graze on fragile rangeland (high stocking density), plants cannot regrow, leading to total loss of vegetative cover.
  • Soil vulnerability: Without deep plant root networks to bind the soil particles together, and without leaf canopies to buffer against baking solar radiation and wind, the exposed topsoil dries into loose dust that is readily stripped away by wind erosion or washed away in sudden flash downpours (sheet erosion), leaving barren desert pavement.
  • Link to Table 10.2: Table 10.2 explicitly identifies that "preventing total loss of vegetation... through preventing overgrazing" is critical to stop desertification. Source C displays the exact opposite scenario: uncontrolled pastoral grazing on a fragile, arid ecosystem.

c. Actions a farmer can take to protect dryland soils (based on Table 10.2):

  • Rotational Grazing & Reduced Stocking Density: Divide grazing lands into fenced paddocks and rotate sheep between them. This prevents animals from grazing any single area down to the bare roots, giving shrubs and perennial grasses sufficient time to regenerate and restabilize the soil.
  • Planting Shelterbelts / Windbreaks: Plant rows of drought-tolerant native trees and bushes (e.g. Acacia, Neem, or saltbush) perpendicular to prevailing winds along field boundaries. These reduce wind velocity at ground level, preventing dust storms and wind erosion of fine topsoil.
  • Contour Ploughing & Bunds (Stone Lines): Build low stone lines ("magic stones" / bunds) along the natural contours of slopes. These act as barriers that slow down rainwater runoff, trap eroded soil particles, and allow rainwater to infiltrate deeply into the soil rather than washing topsoil away.
  • Water Harvesting & Drip Irrigation: Construct small earth dams, zaï planting pits (small holes filled with compost to collect rainfall), and use efficient drip irrigation lines. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to plant roots with minimal evaporation, avoiding the fatal problem of soil salinisation caused by flood irrigation.
  • Mulching & Organic Fertilisation: Leave crop residues and dry straw on the ground surface to shade the soil, reduce moisture evaporation, and decompose into organic humus that improves the soil's water-holding capacity.
