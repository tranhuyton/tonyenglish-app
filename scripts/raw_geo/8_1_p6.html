Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Global pattern of life expectancy:

  • Highest life expectancy (75–80+ years): Concentrated in High-Income Countries (HICs) located in North America (USA, Canada), Western Europe (UK, Germany, France, Scandinavia), East Asia (Japan, South Korea), and Australasia (Australia, New Zealand).
  • Moderate life expectancy (65–74 years): Found across Middle-Income Countries (MICs) in Latin America (Brazil, Argentina), North Africa, parts of the Middle East, and parts of Asia (China, Thailand).
  • Lowest life expectancy (under 60 years, with some under 50): Heavily clustered in Low-Income Countries (LICs) throughout Sub-Saharan Africa (e.g. Chad, Central African Republic, Nigeria).

b. Why life expectancy varies across the world:

  • Healthcare accessibility: HICs invest heavily in modern hospitals, advanced medical treatments, vaccines, and high doctor-to-patient ratios. LICs suffer from severe shortages of medical staff, clinics, and essential medicines.
  • Clean water and sanitation: Piped treated water and closed sewer systems in HICs virtually eliminate deadly waterborne diseases (cholera, dysentery). In contrast, poor sanitation in LICs drives high infant and child mortality.
  • Nutrition and food security: HICs enjoy abundant, diverse, and secure food supplies, whereas LICs frequently face seasonal malnutrition, famine, and food deficits that weaken immune systems.
  • Infectious disease burden & conflict: Tropical diseases (malaria, tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS) along with civil wars, political instability, and extreme poverty in LICs drastically lower average lifespans.

c. Is life expectancy alone a good indicator of development?

  • Strengths: It provides a reliable overarching snapshot of a country's long-term investments in sanitation, healthcare, and nutrition.
  • Limitations: As a national average, it conceals extreme internal inequalities between the wealthy elite and impoverished rural/slum residents. Furthermore, it does not measure economic income, educational attainment, or subjective quality of life (living longer does not guarantee living happily or healthily).
  • Conclusion: While valuable, it is best used as part of a composite index like the Human Development Index (HDI) alongside educational and economic metrics.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Access to quality toilets as a development indicator:

  • High-quality flush toilets require complex municipal infrastructure: underground drainage, continuous pressurized clean water, and sewage treatment facilities.
  • Proper sanitation prevents human waste from contaminating groundwater and food, drastically cutting infant mortality from diarrhoeal diseases and improving dignity, safety, and community hygiene.

b. Other household items indicating development:

  • Refrigerators: Proves stable 24/7 electrical connection and allows hygienic preservation of perishable food.
  • Washing machines: Requires piped pressurized water and saves massive amounts of domestic labor time.
  • Computers & Broadband Internet: Reflects digital literacy, connectivity, access to knowledge, and higher disposable income.
  • Clean cooking stoves (gas/electric vs open fire): Indicates access to modern clean energy, preventing lethal indoor smoke inhalation.

c. Advantages and disadvantages of household photographs:

  • Advantages: Relatable, visual, and humanizing; allows people to understand everyday material conditions in a way abstract GDP numbers cannot convey.
  • Disadvantages: Subjective and cannot be easily quantified or aggregated into national statistics; prone to sample bias (one family's home may not represent the national average); cultural preferences can sometimes be misinterpreted as poverty.

d. Dollar Street comparison (Model paragraph):

When comparing a low-income family in rural Burundi (earning ~$1.50/day) with a high-income family in Sweden (earning ~$70/day), both share the common human essentials: providing shelter, cooking meals, resting after work, and ensuring safety for their children. However, the differences in living standards are stark. The Burundian family cooks over an open wood fire in a single mud-brick room, sleeps on straw mats on an earthen floor, uses an outdoor pit latrine, and collects water in yellow jerrycans from a distant well. In contrast, the Swedish family lives in a multi-room house with central heating, an induction stove, a tiled indoor bathroom with hot running water, private bedrooms, and high-speed internet.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Key indicators of HDI:

  • Health dimension: Life expectancy at birth.
  • Education dimension: Mean years of schooling (adults 25+) and Expected years of schooling (school-age children).
  • Income dimension: Gross National Income (GNI) per capita adjusted for Purchasing Power Parity ($PPP).

b. Unexpected results in Table 8.3:

  • Singapore vs Switzerland: Singapore's GNI per capita ($90 919) is far higher than Switzerland's ($66 933), yet Switzerland is ranked 1st and Singapore 10th because Switzerland has significantly higher mean years of schooling (13.9 vs 11.9) and longer life expectancy (84.0 vs 82.8).
  • USA vs Japan: USA's GNI ($64 765) is 53% higher than Japan ($42 274), yet Japan ranks nearly the same (Rank 24 vs 21) because Japan's life expectancy is 7.7 years higher (84.9 vs 77.2). USA life expectancy is lowered by healthcare inequality and opioid/chronic illness crises.
  • Morocco vs India: Morocco has a notably higher life expectancy (74.0 years) than India (67.2 years), but its mean years of schooling (5.9 years) is lower than India's (6.7 years), reflecting historic educational deficits.

c. Global pattern of HDI:

  • Pattern: Very High HDI (≥0.800) is clustered in North America, Western/Northern Europe, East Asia, and Australasia. Medium/High HDI (0.550–0.799) covers most of Latin America, North Africa, and Southeast/South Asia. Low HDI (<0.550) is concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and conflict nations like Afghanistan.
  • Reasons: Variances in colonial history, political governance, industrial diversification, technological infrastructure, and public health spending.

d. Trends in HDI scores over time:

  • Global HDI scores have risen consistently since 1990 due to global public health campaigns, expanding school enrollment, and economic globalization. Temporary dips correlate with civil wars, severe economic crises, and the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020–2021.

e. Is HDI better than single indicators?

  • Yes, HDI is significantly superior. Purely economic indicators (GDP per capita) ignore whether wealth reaches ordinary citizens or funds schools and hospitals. Purely social metrics ignore a nation's financial capacity. HDI effectively combines economic resources with human capabilities (health and education), providing a well-rounded measurement of development.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Definitions:

  • Development: The process of positive, sustained change that improves people's economic prosperity, social opportunities, political freedoms, and overall quality of life.
  • Standard of living: The material well-being of individuals, measured by financial income and access to physical goods and services (e.g. GDP per capita, ownership of housing, appliances).
  • Quality of life: The general well-being and happiness of individuals, encompassing subjective factors like health, civil liberties, security, clean environment, and work-life balance.

b. Usefulness of development indicators:

  • Quantitative indicators provide objective, standardized data to track progress over time and compare countries globally. However, they struggle to capture intangible elements like human rights, happiness, or local cultural values.

c. Surprises in Table 8.4 & Problems of single indicators:

  • Surprises:
    • The USA has a higher death rate (10/1000) than Morocco (6.2/1000), and Cuba has a very high death rate (15/1000). This occurs because HICs have ageing populations with more elderly people, whereas developing nations have younger demographics where fewer people die annually.
    • Cuba has a 100% literacy rate (higher than the USA's 86%), reflecting intense government funding in universal education despite a lower economic income.
  • Problems of using a single indicator: Single indicators can be profoundly misleading. If judging solely by Death Rate, Morocco would look more developed than the USA. If using Literacy Rate, Cuba would rank top. Single indicators fail to capture the multi-faceted complexity of national development.

d. World Bank investigation (Scatter graph & anomalies):

  • General relationship: Strong positive correlation between economic wealth (GNI per capita) and social indicators (e.g. Life expectancy or secondary school completion).
  • Anomalies:
    • Positive anomaly (High social, moderate income): Countries like Cuba and Costa Rica achieve high life expectancy and near 100% literacy through prioritizing public healthcare and education over military or luxury spending.
    • Negative anomaly (High income, low social): Oil-rich nations (e.g. Equatorial Guinea) have very high GNI per capita from petroleum exports, yet low life expectancy and literacy due to severe wealth concentration among a small elite and neglect of public welfare.
