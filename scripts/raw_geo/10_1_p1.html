10.1 How Our Food is Produced: Agricultural Systems

A rigorous spatial examination of global agriculture: classifying farming types across arable, pastoral, and mixed systems; evaluating subsistence versus commercial incentives; analyzing extensive versus intensive operational scales; and deconstructing agricultural systems through the Input-Process-Output (IPO) model.

1

Classification of Farming Types & Operational Scales

Agriculture is the world's most widespread primary economic activity, occupying approximately 38% of the planet's ice-free land surface. In IGCSE Geography, all agricultural operations are categorized along three fundamental analytical axes:

Axis 1 • What is Produced

Arable vs Pastoral vs Mixed

  • Arable Farming: Dedicated strictly to growing crops (e.g. cereals, vegetables, tubers, fruits) from the soil. Involves no commercial livestock.
  • Pastoral Farming: Involves the rearing and tending of livestock (e.g. beef/dairy cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, poultry) for meat, wool, hide, or dairy.
  • Mixed Farming: A diversified system combining both crop cultivation and livestock rearing on the same farm holding, allowing manure recycling.
Axis 2 • Economic Objective

Commercial vs Subsistence

  • Commercial Farming: Crops and livestock are produced explicitly for sale at domestic or international markets to generate financial profit.
  • Subsistence Farming: Agricultural produce is grown solely to feed the farmer and their immediate family, with little or no surplus for market exchange.
  • Cash Cropping: Commercial cultivation of high-value crops (e.g. coffee, cocoa, palm oil) often displacing local food crops in LEDCs.
Axis 3 • Input/Area Ratio

Intensive vs Extensive

  • Intensive Farming: High inputs of capital and/or labor per unit of land area, resulting in very high crop yields per hectare (e.g. wet rice farming, glasshouses).
  • Extensive Farming: Small inputs of labor and capital spread over a vast land area, yielding low output per hectare but high output per worker (e.g. Canadian wheat).

The Agricultural System: Input-Process-Output (IPO) Framework

A farm functions as an open system. It receives natural and human inputs, transforms them through biological and manual processes, and generates both positive and negative outputs. Surplus income or seeds are fed back into the system for the next cycle.

High-Tech Frontiers: Hydroponics, Aeroponics & Vertical Farming

As conventional arable land faces soil degradation and climate extremes, high-income nations and land-scarce cities are decoupling crop production from open fields through controlled-environment agriculture (CEA):

Figure 10.7: High-tech vertical farming using stacked LED-lit nutrient trays (Hodder Education).

Technological Mechanisms: Hydroponics suspends plant roots directly in nutrient-rich water solutions (eliminating soil pathogens and reducing water consumption by 90%). Aeroponics mists bare roots suspended in air with high-pressure nutrient fog, increasing oxygen uptake and growth rates. Stacked vertically in indoor warehouses, these systems produce up to 10× to 20× more food per square meter than traditional fields, completely immune to outdoor droughts or hailstorms.

🎓 Cambridge IGCSE Exam Strategy: 7-Mark Agricultural Questions
In exam 7-mark case study questions on agricultural systems, always classify the farm precisely along all three axes (Arable vs Pastoral, Commercial vs Subsistence, Intensive vs Extensive), categorize inputs into physical versus human, describe specific farming processes, and identify both commercial outputs and environmental byproducts.
