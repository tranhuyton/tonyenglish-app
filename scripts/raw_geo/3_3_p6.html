TASK 1: Study Source A

Source A: Vertical layers within the tropical rainforest

Source A: Vertical stratification of the tropical rainforest
  1. a. Describe the structure of a tropical rainforest.
  2. b. Why does so little light reach the rainforest floor? How does that impact the plants on the rainforest floor?
  3. c. Explain how a reduction in sunlight would change this ecosystem.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Structure of a tropical rainforest:

  • Four Distinct Stratified Layers:
    • Emergent Layer (35–50+ metres): Giant solitary hardwood trees (e.g. kapok, Brazil nut) with wide buttress roots that punch through the canopy to reach sunlight, exposed to high winds and intense heat.
    • Canopy Layer (20–35 metres): A dense, continuous, interlocking ceiling of evergreen tree foliage. This layer absorbs 70–80% of sunlight and houses up to 90% of all rainforest animal species (monkeys, birds, insects).
    • Understorey Layer (5–20 metres): Shaded, humid intermediate layer comprising younger trees, tree ferns, dwarf palms, and climbing woody vines (lianas) that loop around tree trunks to climb toward light.
    • Rainforest Floor (0–5 metres): Extremely dark, damp ground layer receiving only 1–2% of daylight. Dominated by decomposing leaf litter, rapid fungal and bacterial decay, herbaceous shade-tolerant plants, and surface-spreading tree roots.

b. Why so little light reaches the forest floor and its botanical impact:

  • Why little light reaches the floor: The dense, multi-tiered canopy of broad, overlapping evergreen leaves and epiphytes intercepts up to 98% of incoming solar radiation, casting deep shade below.
  • Impact on ground-layer plants: Ground vegetation is sparse except along riverbanks or treefall gaps. Plants must develop specialised shade adaptations: broad, dark-green leaves packed with chlorophyll to capture faint filtered light; drip tips to shed water and prevent fungal growth; and parasitic or saprophytic nutrient absorption from the thick rotting humus layer.

c. How a reduction in sunlight would change this ecosystem:

  • Reduced Photosynthesis & Productivity: Sunlight is the primary abiotic energy source driving the rainforest's high primary productivity ($2,200 ext{ g/m}^2/ ext{year}$). Lower sunlight drastically slows tree growth, fruit flowering, and biomass production.
  • Collapse of Understorey Species: Plants in the lower layers already survive on the brink of light starvation; any further light reduction would cause mass mortality of understorey seedlings and ground herbs.
  • Trophic Decline: Herbivores (insects, frugivorous birds, bats, primates) that depend on canopy flowers, seeds, and nectar would decline, triggering severe population crashes across secondary consumers (snakes, predatory birds, jaguars).
TASK 2: Study Source B

Source B: Low-pressure atmospheric system and equatorial rainfall

Source B: Formation of clouds and convective rainfall over the equatorial low-pressure belt

This diagram shows the low-pressure system and formation of clouds over the equator (0° latitude) when the sun is directly overhead. In June, the sun is over the Tropic of Cancer, and in December, it is over the Tropic of Capricorn.

  1. a. Explain why clouds and thunderstorms form over the equator.
  2. b. Explain why storms take place mostly later in the afternoon. You can also use Source C to help you answer this question.
  3. c. Explain why the equatorial region does not have summer and winter seasons.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Why clouds and thunderstorms form over the equator:

  • Intense Insolation & Evapotranspiration: The equator receives direct, high-angle solar radiation year-round. This heats the ground, rivers, and dense vegetation, driving massive evaporation and forest transpiration.
  • Convection Currents & Low Pressure: Warm, buoyant, moisture-laden air expands, becomes less dense, and rises rapidly, creating the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) low-pressure belt.
  • Condensation & Thunderheads: As rising air cools at the dry and saturated adiabatic lapse rates, water vapour condenses around condensation nuclei to form massive, towering cumulonimbus clouds, resulting in heavy daily convectional downpours accompanied by lightning and thunder.

b. Why thunderstorms occur primarily in the late afternoon:

  • Thermal Lag of Ground Heating: As shown in Source C, solar light intensity rises from 6 a.m. and peaks around midday. However, time is required for the earth and tree canopy to absorb this insolation and transfer heat to the lower atmosphere via conduction and convection.
  • Maximum Instability by 2–4 p.m.: Peak ground temperatures ($28.3^\circ ext{C}$) are attained in early-to-mid afternoon. By this time, convective updrafts have reached maximum buoyancy and moisture saturation, causing rapid vertical cloud development that unleashes torrential afternoon rainstorms between 3 p.m. and 5 p.m.

c. Why the equatorial region lacks distinct summer and winter seasons:

  • Constant High Angle of Solar Insolation: Because the equator sits at 0° latitude, the noon sun remains almost directly overhead (between 66.5° and 90° altitude) throughout the entire year, experiencing minimal seasonal variation in solar ray angle.
  • Equal Day Lengths: Day length remains virtually constant at approximately 12 hours of daylight and 12 hours of darkness every day of the year. Consequently, heat input is sustained year-round, resulting in an annual temperature range of less than 2–3°C (diurnal temperature range is greater than the seasonal range).
TASK 3: Study Source C

Source C: Temperature and light intensity over a 24-hour period in a tropical rainforest

Source C: Diurnal variations in temperature (°C) and light intensity (Lux) over 24 hours
  1. a. Using the highest temperature as 28.3 °C and the lowest temperature as 23.4 °C, calculate the daily temperature range for the tropical rainforest.
  2. b. With reference to Source C, compare the change in light intensity with the change in temperature in a tropical rainforest over a 24-hour period.
  3. c. Explain the relationship between the light intensity and temperature in the rainforest.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Calculation of daily (diurnal) temperature range:

  • Maximum temperature: 28.3°C (recorded around 2–3 p.m.)
  • Minimum temperature: 23.4°C (recorded at dawn, 6 a.m.)
  • Calculation: $28.3^\circ ext{C} - 23.4^\circ ext{C} =$ 4.9°C.

b. Comparison of light intensity vs. temperature changes over 24 hours:

  • Morning Rise: Both variables start at their lowest points around 6 a.m. (Light = 0 Lux, Temp = 23.4°C). Light intensity increases rapidly and steeply, exceeding 1,500 Lux by 10 a.m. and peaking around 12 p.m. to 2 p.m. at approximately 2,600–2,700 Lux. Temperature rises more gradually, climbing steadily from 23.4°C to reach its peak of 28.3°C shortly after 2 p.m.
  • Afternoon Divergence & Decline: Light intensity drops sharply after 2:30–3:00 p.m. due to the thickening cover of cumulonimbus thunderstorm clouds, dropping to 0 Lux by 6:30 p.m. (sunset). In contrast, temperature decreases very gradually, retaining heat and falling by only ~4°C through the night back to 24°C at 6 a.m.
  • Thermal Lag: The temperature peak lags approximately 1 to 2 hours behind the peak light intensity.

c. Explanation of the relationship:

  • Direct Thermal Driving: Solar light intensity provides electromagnetic radiation that heats the vegetative canopy and forest ground. As light intensity rises, the absorbed energy warms the surrounding air through sensible heat transfer.
  • Why the lag occurs: It takes time for incoming solar energy to be absorbed by solid matter and re-radiated as longwave thermal energy to heat the ambient air mass.
  • Nighttime Heat Retention: Even though light drops to 0 Lux at night, high humidity, cloud cover, and dense canopy cover act like an insulating blanket (greenhouse effect), trapping escaping terrestrial radiation and explaining the small diurnal range of only 4.9°C.
TASK 4: Study Source D

Source D: Morphological and behavioural adaptations in tropical rainforest species

Source D: Long-tailed hermit hummingbird feeding in the Amazon rainforest
  1. a. In pairs, discuss how you think the long-tailed hermit hummingbird has adapted for feeding from flowers found in the Amazon rainforest. Suggest three features that show how it has specialised for this type of feeding.
  2. b. In pairs, research a rainforest animal, bird, or insect that has adapted to live in the tropical rainforest. Create a diagram, such as a mind map or flowchart, giving the information on the organism you have identified. Share your findings with the rest of the class.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Three specialised feeding adaptations of the Long-tailed Hermit Hummingbird (Phaethornis superciliosus):

  • 1. Long, Downcurved Bill (Morphological): Perfectly co-evolved to match the exact curvature of deep, tubular rainforest flowers (e.g. Heliconia and passionflowers). This exclusive shape enables the hummingbird to reach deep nectar chambers that insects and other bird species cannot access.
  • 2. High-Speed Hovering Flight Mechanics (Locomotory): Rotatable shoulder joints allow wings to beat in a figure-eight pattern at up to 50–80 beats per second. This enables stationary hovering in mid-air directly in front of fragile floral blossoms without needing a landing perch.
  • 3. Extensible Grooved Tongue (Physiological): Possesses a specialised, forked tongue that laps up nectar at rapid speeds through capillary action, extracting high-calorie sugars needed to fuel its high metabolic rate.

b. Model Case Study for Research Presentation: The Three-Toed Sloth (Bradypus tridactylus):

Adaptations for Canopy Survival:
  • Curved Claws & Suspensory Grip: Possesses three elongated, curved claws (8–10 cm) on each foot that act as natural meat-hooks, enabling the sloth to hang effortlessly upside-down from high canopy branches without expending muscle energy.
  • Extremely Slow Metabolic Rate: Moves at only 0.25 km/h, consuming low-nutrient, fibrous leaves that are broken down over up to a month in a multi-chambered stomach. Its slowness prevents detection by visual aerial predators such as the Harpy Eagle.
  • Algal Camouflage (Symbiosis): Its thick grooved fur hosts symbiotic green algae (Trichophilus welckeri), giving the coat a greenish hue that blends seamlessly into canopy foliage and provides additional shelter for moth species.
  • Specialised Hair Direction: Fur parts along the stomach and grows downwards towards the back, allowing torrential equatorial rainfall to run off quickly while hanging upside-down.
