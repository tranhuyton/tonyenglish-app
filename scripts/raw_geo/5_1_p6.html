Source B: Total emissions of carbon dioxide and global temperature change between 1880 and 2018
  1. a. Describe the graph.
  2. b. In the year 2000, what was the total human output of carbon dioxide?
  3. c. What is the range of total human output of carbon dioxide between 1880 and 2018?
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Description of the graph:

  • Overall Trend: Both cumulative human carbon dioxide emissions and global temperature anomaly show a clear positive, upward trend from 1880 to 2018.
  • 1880 to 1940: CO2 output grew relatively slowly (from ~0 Gt to ~200 Gt). Global temperatures fluctuated with no sustained upward trend (between -0.4°C and +0.2°C).
  • 1940 to 2018 (Exponential Growth): After 1950, human CO2 output accelerated exponentially, soaring from ~200 Gt to over 1500 Gt by 2018. In direct response, global mean temperatures rose sharply and steadily from around 0°C to +1.0°C by 2018.
  • Correlation: There is a strong positive correlation between increasing greenhouse gas output and rising global surface temperatures.

b. Total human output of carbon dioxide in the year 2000:

  • Reading the solid black curve at the year 2000 on the left-hand axis gives approximately 1000 Gt (acceptable range: 950 – 1050 Gt).

c. Range of total human output between 1880 and 2018:

  • Value in 1880: approximately 0 Gt.
  • Value in 2018: approximately 1500 Gt (acceptable range: 1450 – 1550 Gt).
  • Range calculation: 1500 Gt − 0 Gt = 1500 Gt (acceptable range: 1450 – 1550 Gt).

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Mathematical Calculations for Pie Chart Angles (Total = 360°):

  • Energy (63%): 63 × 3.6° = 226.8° (or 227°)
  • Industry (23%): 23 × 3.6° = 82.8° (or 83°)
  • Agriculture, forestry, and land use (10%): 10 × 3.6° = 36.0°
  • Waste (4%): 4 × 3.6° = 14.4° (or 14°)
  • Check: 226.8° + 82.8° + 36.0° + 14.4° = 360.0° (100%).
  • Drawing Guidelines: Draw a neat circle with a compass, use a protractor to mark angles accurately, and include a descriptive title and color key/legend.

b. Differences between Source C (Global) and the USA pie chart:

  • Industry sector: Direct industrial emissions in the USA (23%) are more than four times higher than the global average in Source C (5.2%).
  • Energy sector: The USA has a lower proportion of emissions from energy (63%) compared to the global total (73.2% in Source C), a difference of ~10.2 percentage points.
  • Agriculture, forestry, and land use: Globally, agriculture and deforestation make up 18.4% of emissions, whereas in the USA it accounts for only 10% (almost half the global proportion).
  • Waste sector: Relatively similar proportions, with the USA at 4% and global emissions at 3.2%.

c. Three possible reasons for these differences:

  • 1. Massive industrial economy: The USA is a highly advanced industrial superpower with extensive domestic chemical manufacturing, petroleum refining, and steel production, causing industry to make up a much larger share (23% vs 5.2%).
  • 2. Less land-use change and deforestation: Many developing countries experience extensive tropical deforestation and traditional biomass clearing. The USA has stable or expanding forest management laws and highly intensive, mechanized agriculture, keeping land-use emissions lower (10% vs 18.4%).
  • 3. Energy transition and efficiency: The USA has progressively shifted energy generation toward natural gas and renewables while implementing stricter energy-efficiency codes in buildings and appliances compared to the global average.
TASK 4: Study Table 5.1

Table 5.1: The global warming potential of greenhouse gases

Greenhouse gas GWP over 100 years
Carbon dioxide (CO2) 1
Methane (CH4) 29.8
Nitrous oxide (N2O) 273
F-gases 1000–10 000

Whether the source is natural or human, different greenhouse gas emissions warm the atmosphere differently. This is called the global warming potential (GWP). Fluorinated gases (F-gases), for example, are 10 000 times more efficient at trapping heat than carbon dioxide.

  1. a. Using Table 5.1 and the information presented earlier in this topic, which greenhouse gas do you think is the most significant cause of global warming? Which is the least significant? Support your ideas with evidence.
  2. b. Why is it important to address the emission of all greenhouse gases and not just focus on one particular gas?
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Most and least significant causes of global warming:

  • Most significant cause: Carbon dioxide (CO2)
    • Evidence: Despite having the lowest Global Warming Potential (GWP = 1), CO2 is emitted in vastly larger quantities than any other gas (over 70–75% of all global emissions). Furthermore, CO2 persists in the atmosphere for centuries, creating a cumulative heat-trapping blanket that drives the vast majority of net global warming.
  • Least significant cause: F-gases (Fluorinated gases)
    • Evidence: Although F-gases possess exceptionally high warming potentials (GWP 1 000 to 10 000 times that of CO2), they are emitted in very small quantities and represent only around 2–3% of total global greenhouse gas emissions by radiative forcing.

b. Why it is important to address all greenhouse gases simultaneously:

  • High short-term potency: Gases like methane (CH4, GWP = 29.8) and nitrous oxide (N2O, GWP = 273) trap heat far more aggressively than CO2. Reducing methane emissions provides rapid, immediate cooling effects that can help avert near-term climate tipping points.
  • Diverse economic sectors: Different gases stem from distinct anthropogenic activities: CO2 from fossil fuel combustion and transportation; CH4 from agriculture (rice paddies, ruminant livestock) and landfills; N2O from nitrogen fertilizers; and F-gases from refrigeration and air conditioning. Targeting only CO2 leaves massive agricultural and industrial emissions completely unchecked.
  • Comprehensive climate mitigation: To keep global temperature anomalies within safe international limits (e.g. 1.5°C), deep reductions across all greenhouse gases are required concurrently.
