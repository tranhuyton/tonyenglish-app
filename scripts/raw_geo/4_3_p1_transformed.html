The Impact of Tectonic Hazards

Analyze physical and human vulnerability factors, primary vs secondary hazard effects, measurement scales, and case study contrasts.

1. Factors Influencing Hazard Impact (Physical vs. Human)

Nghe cả phần

A natural event only becomes a natural disaster when it affects a vulnerable human population that lacks the resilience or capacity to cope. The severity of death, destruction, and economic loss is shaped by a complex interplay of physical and human variables:

🌍 Physical Factors

  • Magnitude & Energy: Higher Mw or VEI generates vastly more ground shaking or explosive blast energy.
  • Focal Depth: Shallow earthquakes (<70 km) lose little energy before reaching the surface.
  • Distance from Epicentre: Destruction is highest near the rupture and attenuates with distance.
  • Local Geology & Liquefaction: Unconsolidated water-saturated soils lose strength during shaking, behaving like quicksand (liquefaction).
  • Secondary Hazards: Offshore fault rupture can trigger catastrophic tsunamis.

👥 Human Factors & Vulnerability

  • Level of Economic Development (HIC vs LIC): HICs afford early warning networks, seismic engineering, and rapid medical mobilization.
  • Population Density: Dense megacities near fault zones amplify casualty counts exponentially.
  • Building Standards & Enforcement: Strict building codes prevent collapse; weak enforcement leads to brittle "pancake" collapse.
  • Disaster Preparedness: Routine evacuation drills, emergency supplies, and trained search-and-rescue teams save thousands.
  • Quality of Governance: Stable institutions coordinate relief effectively, while corruption delays aid.

4. Comparative Case Studies: HIC vs. LIC Impacts

Nghe cả phần

The contrast between events in High-Income Countries and Low-Income Countries provides definitive proof that human vulnerability and economic resources govern survival rates:

Case Studies Map

Major Tectonic Disaster Case Studies (HIC vs LIC)

🇯🇵 Case Study 1: Earthquake & Tsunami in an HIC — Tohoku, Japan (2011)

  • Parameters: 11 March 2011 | Magnitude: Mw 9.0 (4th largest globally since 1900) | Epicentre: 70 km offshore Sendai | Depth: 30 km.
  • Primary Impacts: Very few buildings collapsed directly from ground shaking due to world-leading seismic building codes. However, coastal land subsided by up to 1 metre.
  • Secondary Impacts: Triggered a colossal tsunami reaching run-up heights over 40 metres that overtopped 10-metre seawalls, inundating 560 km² of coastline. Flooded the backup diesel generators at Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Plant, causing a Level 7 triple meltdown.
  • Casualties & Economic Cost: ~18,000 fatalities (over 90% caused by drowning in the tsunami, not building collapse). Economic loss exceeded $235 billion—the costliest natural disaster in human history.

🇭🇹 Case Study 2: Earthquake in an LIC — Port-au-Prince, Haiti (2010)

  • Parameters: 12 January 2010 | Magnitude: Mw 7.0 | Epicentre: 25 km southwest of capital Port-au-Prince | Shallow focal depth: 13 km along the Enriquillo-Plantain Garden fault.
  • Primary Impacts: Catastrophic pancake collapse of over 250,000 homes, hospitals, schools, and the Presidential Palace due to cheap, unreinforced brittle concrete construction without building codes.
  • Secondary Impacts: Complete collapse of civil governance and emergency communications. Dead bodies left in streets contaminated groundwater; crowded refugee camps suffered a severe cholera outbreak that killed an additional 9,000 people.
  • Casualties & Economic Cost: Over 220,000 deaths and 300,000 injured; 1.5 million people left homeless. Economic damages represented over 120% of Haiti's entire annual GDP.

🇵🇭 Case Study 3: Volcanic Eruption in an MIC — Mount Pinatubo, Philippines (1991)

  • Parameters: 15 June 1991 | Volcanic Explosivity Index: VEI 6 | Ejected over 10 billion tonnes of magma and 20 million tonnes of SO₂.
  • Co-occurring Crisis: Typhoon Yunya struck simultaneously; torrential rains mixed with volcanic ash fallout to trigger massive lahars that buried entire river systems and bridges.
  • Early Warning Success: The Philippine Institute of Volcanology (PHIVOLCS) and USGS successfully predicted the eruption. Timely evacuation of over 75,000 people saved tens of thousands of lives.
  • Impacts: 847 fatalities (mostly from roofs collapsing under wet ash). Global atmospheric temperatures dropped by 0.5°C over the following 18 months due to the stratospheric aerosol shield.
