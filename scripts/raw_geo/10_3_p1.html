10.3 The Challenges of Food Supply: Insecurity & Solutions

A critical geographical investigation into the root causes of food shortages: contrasting physical vulnerabilities against political and economic drivers; deconstructing Malthusian versus Boserupian theories; evaluating the Nigerian food crisis; and assessing sustainable solutions from terracing and precision irrigation to food aid.

1

Defining Food Security & Measuring Global Hunger

According to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), Food Security exists when "all people, at all times, have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs for an active and healthy life."

1. Availability
Sufficient quantities of food of appropriate quality, supplied through domestic production or commercial imports.
2. Access
Individuals having adequate purchasing power and economic resources to acquire nutritious food at local markets.
3. Utilisation
Nutritional absorption via clean drinking water, sanitation, healthcare, and safe food preparation.
4. Stability
Continuous resilience against sudden climatic shocks, economic recessions, or conflict interruptions.

Theoretical Debate: Thomas Malthus vs Ester Boserup

The Malthusian Pessimistic View (1798)

Pessimist

Malthus posited that food production increases arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) while population grows exponentially (1, 2, 4, 8, 16). Eventually, population outstrips food supply capacity, triggering inevitable "Malthusian checks": famine, epidemic disease, and violent resource wars.

The Boserupian Optimistic View (1965)

Optimist

Ester Boserup argued that "necessity is the mother of invention." As population approaches carrying capacity, agricultural innovation is stimulated: farmers introduce terracing, drainage, chemical fertilisers, Green Revolution high-yielding varieties (HYVs), and genetically modified (GM) crops, raising land productivity.

Sustainable Strategies: Soil Conservation & Advanced Irrigation

Figure 10.22: Incan Terracing (Peru)

Step-like flat earth shelves cut into steep Andean mountainsides slow surface water runoff, retain moisture, and halt soil erosion.

Figure 10.23: Sprinkler Irrigation (Spain)

Modern automated center-pivot sprinklers deliver water uniformly to commercial crops across arid landscapes, boosting yields.

Figure 10.24: Soil Salinisation (France)

Over-irrigation in hot Mediterranean climates causes rapid evaporation, drawing dissolved salts to the surface and forming toxic white crusts.
