10.4 How Our Energy is Produced: Sources & Systems

Deconstructing the global energy system: distinguishing primary fuels from secondary energy carriers; evaluating the economic and physical constraints of non-renewable fossil stocks versus infinite renewable flows; investigating the fuelwood crisis across developing nations; and mapping the global energy transition ladder.

1

Foundational Energy Taxonomy: Primary vs Secondary Energy

Energy is the fundamental currency of modern industrial society, powering transportation, domestic heating, commercial agriculture, and digital communications. In geographical analysis, energy resources are categorized along two key classifications:

Classification 1 • Physical State

Primary vs Secondary Energy

  • Primary Energy: Energy found in nature in an unprocessed, raw state that has not undergone any anthropogenic technical conversion (e.g. coal, crude oil, natural gas, raw uranium ore, solar radiation, moving wind, river water, fuelwood).
  • Secondary Energy: Useful energy forms produced by chemically refining or transforming primary energy sources (e.g. electricity generated from coal/gas/nuclear/hydro, or petrol and diesel refined from crude oil).
Classification 2 • Longevity & Depletion

Non-Renewable vs Renewable Resources

  • Non-Renewable (Finite): Finite stocks formed over millions of years of geological time (fossil fuels: coal, oil, gas) or mineral reserves (uranium). Once extracted and combusted, they cannot be replenished on a human timescale.
  • Renewable (Infinite Flow): Continuous energetic flows driven by solar radiation, planetary gravity, or subterranean geothermal heat (solar, wind, hydroelectric power, tidal, geothermal) that naturally replenish and cannot be exhausted.

The Fuelwood Crisis: Energy Poverty in Low-Income Nations

While high-income nations debate nuclear power and grid batteries, over 2.5 billion people worldwide (nearly one-third of humanity) rely on fuelwood, charcoal, and dried animal dung as their primary source of domestic energy for cooking and winter heating.

Figure 10.27: A rural woman carrying a heavy basket of harvested fuelwood from a mountain forest in Nepal (Hodder Education).
1. Health Impacts
Indoor Air Pollution
Burning raw biomass indoors on open three-stone fires releases toxic carbon monoxide and particulates, killing over 3 million women and children annually (WHO).
2. Gender & Social Burden
Time Poverty & Education
Women and young girls spend up to 4–6 hours daily walking long distances to forage for wood, preventing girls from attending school and perpetuating generational poverty.
3. Environmental Degradation
Deforestation & Soil Loss
Rapid population growth causes wood harvesting to exceed forest replenishment rates, stripping hillsides (e.g. Nepal, Haiti), triggering landslides, and accelerating desertification.

🎓 Cambridge IGCSE Exam Strategy: Energy Systems & Fuelwood Crisis
In exam questions on energy supply, always provide precise technical classifications: clearly separate non-renewable exhaustible fossil fuels like coal from infinite renewable flows like geothermal, and explain the physical-geographical conditions required to site hydroelectric dams or offshore wind arrays.
