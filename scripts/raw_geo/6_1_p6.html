Study Sources A and B

  1. With the help of Sources A and B, briefly describe the growth of the human population over time.
  2. It took all of human history until around 1800 years for the world population to reach one billion. How many years did it take for the world's population to increase from:
    1. one to two billion?
    2. six to seven billion?
  3. By 2100, there are likely to be over three billion more people living in the world than today. Suggest two problems which this could cause. Explain why you think each of these problems will be difficult to solve.
  4. The figures for 2100 are an estimate. Explain why it is difficult to predict exact population numbers for the future.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Brief description of human population growth over time (Sources A and B):

  • For most of human history, the world population grew very slowly due to high birth rates being offset by equally high death rates (disease, famine, war).
  • Around 1800, during the Industrial Revolution, improvements in food production, sanitation, and medicine caused death rates to fall while birth rates remained high — triggering rapid, exponential population growth.
  • The rate of growth accelerated dramatically through the 20th century. Source B shows that LICs contributed most of this growth, while HICs' populations grew much more slowly or stabilised.
  • By 2022, the global population reached 8 billion and is projected to continue rising, though the rate of increase is expected to slow as more countries enter later stages of the Demographic Transition Model.

b. Number of years to add each billion:

  • i. One to two billion: Approximately 123 years (reached 1 billion ~1804; reached 2 billion ~1927).
  • ii. Six to seven billion: Approximately 12 years (reached 6 billion ~1999; reached 7 billion ~2011).
  • This dramatic reduction in the time to add each billion reflects the accelerating pace of global population growth through the 20th century.

c. Two problems caused by over 3 billion more people by 2100, and why they are hard to solve:

  • Problem 1: Food and water insecurity — Feeding an additional 3 billion people will require a massive increase in agricultural production, straining already-depleted freshwater resources, increasing deforestation, and accelerating soil degradation. This is difficult to solve because: (i) climate change is simultaneously reducing agricultural productivity in many regions; (ii) most future population growth will occur in countries already struggling with food insecurity (sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia); and (iii) shifting to more sustainable food systems (e.g., reducing meat consumption) requires large-scale behavioural and economic change.
  • Problem 2: Increased greenhouse gas emissions and climate change — More people consuming energy, food, and goods will significantly accelerate climate change, even if per-person consumption falls. This is difficult to solve because: (i) rapidly growing LICs have the right to develop economically; (ii) transitioning away from fossil fuels requires enormous investment in infrastructure; and (iii) the political will to sacrifice short-term economic growth for long-term environmental stability is often lacking, especially in rapidly industrialising nations.

d. Why it is difficult to predict exact population numbers for the future:

  • Unpredictable events: Pandemics (e.g., COVID-19), wars, famines, and natural disasters can abruptly alter birth rates, death rates, and migration patterns in ways that are impossible to foresee.
  • Changing fertility rates: Birth rates are influenced by education levels, female empowerment, cultural norms, and government policy — all of which can shift rapidly in unexpected directions.
  • Government policy changes: A country could adopt a sudden pro- or anti-natalist policy that dramatically alters its demographic trajectory.
  • Medical breakthroughs: Future advances in medicine could extend life expectancy further, or a new pandemic could reverse gains in mortality. These cannot be reliably predicted decades in advance.
  • Migration: International migration flows are highly sensitive to political, economic, and environmental factors that change unpredictably.
TASK 2

Study Source B

  1. Describe the differences in population growth and projected growth between HICs and LICs between 1950 and 2050.
  2. In pairs, discuss possible reasons for why LICs are likely to see the biggest population growth.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Differences in population growth between HICs and LICs, 1950–2050:

  • LICs (Low Income Countries) have experienced and are projected to experience rapid, dramatic population growth. In 1950, LIC populations were relatively small, but by 2050 they are projected to be vastly larger — much of this growth concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia.
  • HICs (High Income Countries) have seen much slower, more gradual growth, with some HICs (e.g., Japan, South Korea, several European countries) approaching population decline or stagnation by 2050 due to very low birth rates.
  • The gap between LIC and HIC population trajectories widens dramatically over time. By 2050, LICs will account for the overwhelming majority of global population growth.

b. Why LICs are likely to see the biggest population growth:

  • High birth rates: In many LICs, women have an average of 4–6 children per family. Cultural norms, religion, and limited education about family planning all contribute to high fertility.
  • High infant mortality driving compensatory births: Where infant mortality is high, parents have more children to ensure enough survive to support them in old age — a rational economic response to an insecure environment.
  • Limited access to contraception and family planning services: Many LICs lack widespread access to affordable contraception, meaning unplanned pregnancies are common.
  • Economic dependence on children: In subsistence farming societies, children are an economic asset — providing labour in the fields and care for parents in old age — creating a strong incentive for large families.
  • Falling death rates: Improvements in basic healthcare, vaccines, and food security have dramatically reduced death rates in LICs, while birth rates have not yet fallen correspondingly — creating rapid natural increase.

Source C: Statement by Antonio Guterres, UN Secretary-General, 2022

The UN announced 15 November 2022 as 'The day of eight billion' - the date that the world's population reached eight billion. Do you agree with the statement given by Antonio Guterres, UN Secretary-General? Write a short speech to justify your response. Then, in small groups, listen to each other's speeches and try to reach a group conclusion.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

Do you agree with Antonio Guterres? Sample speech framework:

Arguments FOR (agreeing with Guterres):

  • Reaching 8 billion reflects remarkable human achievements: global life expectancy rose from ~30 years in 1900 to over 70 today. Falling childhood mortality, advances in medicine, and improved food security have allowed billions more people to survive and thrive.
  • Greater human diversity — in culture, science, innovation, and creativity — increases our collective capacity to solve global problems. A larger, more interconnected global community has produced more scientists, doctors, engineers, and artists than at any other point in history.
  • A large, young population in LICs could provide a "demographic dividend" — a surge in productive workers that drives economic growth, as experienced in East Asia from the 1960s–1990s.

Arguments AGAINST (disagreeing with Guterres):

  • 8 billion people consuming resources is placing enormous strain on the planet. The Earth's ecological footprint already exceeds its biocapacity — we consume more than Earth can sustainably regenerate each year. Adding more people accelerates this crisis.
  • Climate change, biodiversity loss, deforestation, ocean acidification, and freshwater depletion are directly worsened by population growth. Calling this a "celebration" ignores the shared environmental responsibility Guterres himself mentions.
  • The world's resources are distributed extremely unequally — billions still lack clean water, adequate nutrition, and basic healthcare. Celebrating population growth while inequalities remain vast seems hollow.

Examiner Tip: Strong responses will acknowledge both sides, use specific geographical examples, and reach a justified personal conclusion. Avoid one-sided arguments.

TASK 4

In pairs:

  1. Discuss how birth rates, death rates, and migration influence rates of population growth.
  2. Explain how each of the following can affect population growth rates:
    1. government policy
    2. net migration
    3. percentage of women in employment.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. How birth rates, death rates, and migration influence population growth:

  • Population growth is calculated by the formula: Natural increase = Birth Rate − Death Rate (expressed per 1,000 people). Total population change = Natural increase + Net Migration.
  • High birth rate + low death rate = rapid natural increase (as in Stage 2 of the DTM — e.g., Nigeria today).
  • Low birth rate + low death rate = slow growth or stability (Stage 4 — e.g., UK).
  • Low birth rate + low death rate + negative net migration = population decline (Stage 5 — e.g., Japan, Bulgaria).
  • Positive net migration (more people arriving than leaving) adds to the total population regardless of the natural rate — e.g., the UAE where migration dominates population growth.

b. Factors affecting population growth rates:

  • i. Government policy:
    • Pro-natalist policies aim to increase birth rates: e.g., France offers generous maternity/paternity leave, childcare subsidies, and tax credits for families with three or more children — successfully raising France's birth rate above the European average.
    • Anti-natalist policies aim to reduce birth rates: China's former One Child Policy (1979–2015) dramatically reduced China's birth rate but also led to gender imbalance and an ageing population crisis.
    • Policies also influence migration: open-border policies increase immigration; strict quotas reduce it.
  • ii. Net migration:
    • Positive net migration increases population, bringing in typically working-age adults who also tend to have higher fertility rates than the host population, further boosting growth.
    • Negative net migration (emigration exceeding immigration) reduces the population and often causes a "brain drain" — loss of skilled workers — which can reduce economic output and further discourage immigration.
  • iii. Percentage of women in employment:
    • Countries where women have high rates of employment (e.g., Scandinavia, Japan) tend to have lower fertility rates. When women are economically independent, they typically delay marriage and childbearing, and have fewer children overall.
    • Female education and career opportunities are consistently among the strongest predictors of falling birth rates — as seen across East Asia (South Korea, Japan) where female employment has risen sharply as birth rates have fallen to among the world's lowest.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Difference between pro-natalist and anti-natalist policy:

  • Pro-natalist policy: A government strategy designed to increase the birth rate and encourage larger families. Used by countries with ageing or declining populations that need more workers and young people. Examples: France, Sweden, Russia, Singapore.
  • Anti-natalist policy: A government strategy designed to reduce the birth rate and slow population growth. Used by countries experiencing rapid population growth that strains resources and services. Examples: China (One Child Policy), India (family planning campaigns).

b. Which poster is pro-natalist and which is anti-natalist?

  • Source C is PRO-NATALIST. The translation "One is lonely. The best gift for your child is a younger sibling" explicitly encourages parents to have a second child. This reflects China's reversal of the One Child Policy — after 2015, China introduced a Two Child Policy and later a Three Child Policy (2021) to address its rapidly ageing population.
  • Source D is ANTI-NATALIST. The translation advises families to "wait three years before your second child" and promotes access to free family planning methods. This is designed to encourage spacing of births and reduce overall fertility rates — typical of anti-natalist campaigns in LICs (likely India or a similar country) aiming to slow rapid population growth.

c. Effectiveness of the poster images at promoting their message:

  • Source C's effectiveness: The emotional appeal to parental love ("the best gift") is psychologically effective — it reframes having a second child as an act of generosity rather than a burden. However, its effectiveness depends on whether the government also provides economic incentives (childcare subsidies, parental leave) to back up the emotional message. Posters alone rarely change behaviour without supporting financial policies.
  • Source D's effectiveness: The message is practical and informative — it tells people where to get free family planning services. This is more actionable than a purely emotional appeal. However, effectiveness depends on whether target audiences can read, whether health centres are accessible, and whether cultural or religious norms create resistance to family planning.

d. Incentives populations may need to follow their respective policies:

  • Pro-natalist country (Source C — China): Financial benefits (baby bonuses, child tax allowances), paid maternity and paternity leave, subsidised childcare and education, housing subsidies for families with multiple children, reduced working hours for parents. Without tangible financial benefits, the high cost of raising children in modern urban China makes simply "wanting siblings" an insufficient motivation.
  • Anti-natalist country (Source D): Free and accessible contraception and family planning services, female education and employment opportunities (educated women consistently choose to have fewer children), financial incentives for smaller families (e.g., additional government aid), community health workers to advise on family planning, and removal of cultural/social pressures to have large families.

Study Source E (The Demographic Transition Model)

  1. Explain three factors that cause fertility rates to be high in some countries but low in others.
  2. Make a copy of the Demographic Transition Model. Shade in the natural increase.
  3. Explain the reasons for declining mortality in Stage 2.
  4. Suggest why it takes some time before a fall in fertility follows a fall in mortality.
  5. In pairs, discuss whether you think the strengths of the DTM outweigh the weaknesses of the DTM.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Three factors causing fertility rates to be high in some countries but low in others:

  • 1. Access to contraception and family planning: In countries where modern contraception is freely available and culturally accepted, women can choose to have fewer children. Where access is limited (due to cost, infrastructure, or cultural restrictions), unintended pregnancies are more common and fertility rates remain higher. Example: fertility rates in sub-Saharan Africa average ~4.5 children per woman; in Western Europe ~1.6.
  • 2. Level of female education and employment: Educated women marry later, have greater control over reproductive decisions, and tend to have significantly fewer children. Female education is consistently the single most powerful predictor of lower fertility. Example: Bangladesh reduced its fertility rate from 6.9 (1975) to 2.0 (2023) largely through expanding female education and employment.
  • 3. Economic dependence on children: In subsistence farming societies with weak pension systems, children are an economic asset — providing free labour and old-age security. Parents rationally have large families to ensure economic survival. In wealthy urban societies with pension systems and no need for child labour, children are an economic cost, discouraging large families.

b. Shade in the natural increase on the DTM:

  • The area of natural increase on the DTM is the gap between the Birth Rate line (higher) and the Death Rate line (lower). This gap is widest in Stage 2 (rapidly rising population), narrows through Stage 3, and closes in Stage 4 (birth and death rates nearly equal). In Stage 5, death rates may exceed birth rates, producing natural decrease.
  • Students should shade the region between the Birth Rate and Death Rate lines from Stage 1 through Stage 4, using a colour to represent population increase. Where death rates exceed birth rates (Stage 5), shade a different colour for natural decrease.

c. Reasons for declining mortality in Stage 2:

  • Improved food supply: Agricultural improvements (new crop varieties, better farming techniques, improved storage and distribution) meant fewer people died from famine and malnutrition.
  • Advances in medicine and healthcare: Development of vaccines (smallpox, cholera, typhoid), antibiotics, and improved surgical techniques dramatically reduced deaths from previously fatal infectious diseases.
  • Improved sanitation and clean water: Construction of sewage systems and clean water infrastructure eliminated the main transmission routes of cholera, typhoid, and dysentery — diseases that had caused massive urban mortality.
  • Better living conditions: As industrialisation raised living standards, nutrition improved and crowded, disease-prone living conditions were gradually alleviated.

d. Why a fall in fertility takes time to follow a fall in mortality (lag):

  • Cultural lag: Social norms about ideal family size are deeply embedded in culture and religion. Even after childhood death rates fall, it takes a generation or more for people to internalise that they no longer need many children to ensure some survive.
  • Persistent economic rationale: Even as mortality falls, families in LICs still rely on children for agricultural labour and old-age support. The economic incentive to have large families persists until alternative systems (pensions, formal employment) develop.
  • Time for education and female empowerment: Rising literacy, female education, and access to contraception — the key drivers of falling birth rates — take decades to reach entire populations, especially in rural areas.
  • Demographic momentum: Even as fertility falls, the large cohort of children born during the high-fertility period enters its own reproductive years, maintaining a high number of births simply due to the large size of the parental generation.

e. Do the strengths of the DTM outweigh its weaknesses?

Strengths:

  • Provides a clear, simple framework for understanding how and why populations change over time as countries develop economically.
  • Successfully describes the demographic trajectory of most European countries and many Asian countries (Japan, South Korea, China).
  • Useful for predicting future population structure and planning for the social and economic consequences (e.g., ageing population, need for pensions).

Weaknesses:

  • Based primarily on the historical experience of Western European countries during industrialisation — may not apply universally to LICs following a different development path.
  • Does not account for the impact of government policy, war, disease (HIV/AIDS significantly distorted sub-Saharan Africa's demographic transition), or sudden economic shocks.
  • Ignores migration, which can dramatically alter population structures independently of birth and death rates.
  • Stage 5 is speculative — no country has yet completed a full demographic transition to sustained natural decrease, so its characteristics are uncertain.

Conclusion: The DTM is a valuable and widely applicable model, but it is best treated as a general guide rather than a universal law. Its strengths in describing the broad trends of demographic change outweigh its weaknesses for most IGCSE purposes.
