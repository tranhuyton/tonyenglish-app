TASK 1

Study Source A.

Source A: Top countries of origin for migrants in the UK, 2013
  1. Explain the difference between:
    1. immigration and emigration
    2. voluntary and forced migration
    3. a refugee and an asylum seeker.
  2. Use Source A to identify the top two countries of origin for migrants in the UK.
  3. Suggest reasons why the UK has a high number of migrants from these top two countries.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Key definitions:

  • i. Immigration vs. emigration:
    • Immigration is the movement of people into a country from another country, with the intention of settling there permanently or for an extended period.
    • Emigration is the movement of people out of a country to settle in another country. From the perspective of the origin country, these people are emigrants; from the perspective of the destination country, they are immigrants.
  • ii. Voluntary vs. forced migration:
    • Voluntary migration is movement by personal choice, motivated by the desire for better opportunities — e.g., economic migrants seeking higher-paid jobs, students studying abroad, or retirees moving for a better climate.
    • Forced migration occurs when people have no real choice but to leave — driven by war, persecution, famine, natural disaster, or climate change. They may face death or serious harm if they remain. Refugees and internally displaced persons (IDPs) are examples of forced migrants.
  • iii. Refugee vs. asylum seeker:
    • An asylum seeker is a person who has left their home country and formally applied for refugee status in another country. They are waiting for their application to be processed and their claim assessed.
    • A refugee is a person whose asylum application has been accepted — they have been legally recognised as unable to return to their home country due to a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group (UN 1951 Refugee Convention definition). Refugees have legal rights to protection in the host country.

b. Top two countries of origin for migrants in the UK (from Source A, 2013):

  • Based on Source A, the top two countries of origin were India and Poland.
  • (India represents a long-standing Commonwealth migration link; Poland represents post-2004 EU free movement migration.)

c. Why the UK has a high number of migrants from India and Poland:

  • India:
    • Historical and colonial ties: India was part of the British Empire until 1947. British Nationality Act 1948 granted Commonwealth citizens the right to live and work in the UK, establishing migration networks that persist today.
    • English language: English is an official language in India, reducing the language barrier for Indian migrants in the UK.
    • Economic opportunities: The UK's healthcare system (NHS), IT sector, and financial services have particularly high demand for skilled Indian professionals (doctors, nurses, software engineers).
    • Established diaspora: Existing Indian communities in the UK provide social networks, support systems, and employment connections that attract further migration.
  • Poland:
    • EU freedom of movement: Poland joined the EU in 2004. Unlike most EU members, the UK immediately granted Polish citizens the right to work without restriction, triggering a large wave of Polish economic migration.
    • Economic wage gap: Average wages in Poland were significantly lower than in the UK in 2004–2016. Polish workers could earn far more in the UK for equivalent work — a powerful economic pull factor.
    • Construction and service sector demand: The UK experienced a significant economic boom in the mid-2000s, creating high demand for workers in construction, hospitality, food processing, and logistics — sectors where Polish migrants readily found employment.
TASK 2

Study Source B.

Source B: Key migration routes from North Africa to Europe
  1. Investigate and outline the specific push and pull factors that have caused migration for any one of the routes between North Africa and Europe.
  2. Can you think of any events that might occur that would prevent migrants from reaching their desired destination?
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Example: Libya–Italy route (Central Mediterranean)

Push factors from North Africa/Sub-Saharan Africa:

  • Conflict and political instability: Libya has experienced prolonged civil war since 2011. Countries further south (Somalia, Eritrea, Sudan, Mali) have ongoing conflicts, terrorism, or authoritarian repression that make life dangerous.
  • Poverty and economic deprivation: Extremely low wages, unemployment, and lack of economic opportunity across much of sub-Saharan Africa push people to seek better livelihoods elsewhere.
  • Persecution: Political dissidents, religious minorities, and ethnic groups face persecution, detention, and violence in many origin countries.
  • Climate change and drought: Increasing desertification in the Sahel region is destroying agricultural livelihoods, forcing people off their land.

Pull factors to Europe (Italy):

  • Safety and security: EU countries offer protection from conflict and persecution under international refugee law.
  • Higher wages and living standards: Italy and the wider EU offer significantly higher wages, better healthcare, education, and social services than most origin countries.
  • Established migrant communities: Existing diaspora networks in Italy provide practical support and employment connections for new arrivals.
  • Legal pathways: The ability to claim asylum in the EU provides a legal framework for protection.

b. Events that might prevent migrants from reaching their desired destination:

  • Dangerous sea crossings: The Mediterranean Sea crossing is one of the world's deadliest migration routes. Overcrowded, unseaworthy vessels capsize in rough seas. Over 25,000 people are estimated to have died crossing the Mediterranean since 2014.
  • Interception and pushback by coast guards: EU and Libyan coast guards intercept migrant boats and return them to Libya (often to detention centres where migrants face abuse).
  • Border closures and fences: Countries can close their land borders or erect physical barriers (e.g., Hungary's fence) to prevent irregular entry.
  • Stricter immigration and asylum policies: Governments can change laws to make it harder to claim asylum or receive refugee status, reducing successful applications.
  • Human trafficking and exploitation: Many migrants fall victim to traffickers who take their money, abandon them in deserts, or exploit them as forced labourers before they can continue their journey.
  • Detention and deportation: Migrants intercepted within a country may be detained and deported before they can apply for asylum.

d. Additional impacts students might identify:

  • Demographic: Migrants (often young adults) in destination countries boost the working-age population and fertility rate, helping offset ageing.
  • Environmental: Rapid in-migration can increase pressure on housing, land use, and urban infrastructure in destination cities.
  • Political: Immigration can fuel political polarisation, nationalist movements, and policy debates about borders and identity in destination countries.
  • Cultural: Migrants introduce new foods, arts, religions, and languages — enriching the cultural life of destination countries, though sometimes causing cultural tensions.

    • the destination country/countries
    • the migrants themselves.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

Example: Syrian refugee crisis (Source C)

a. Causes of forced migration — Syrian Civil War:

  • The Syrian Civil War began in 2011 following anti-government protests during the Arab Spring. The Assad government responded with violent military crackdowns.
  • The conflict escalated into a complex multi-sided civil war involving government forces, rebel groups, Kurdish militias, ISIS, and foreign powers (Russia, Iran, USA, Turkey).
  • Mass aerial bombing of civilian areas (including hospitals and schools), use of chemical weapons, sieges of cities, and widespread human rights abuses created a humanitarian catastrophe.
  • By 2020, over 500,000 Syrians had died and more than 13 million (over half the pre-war population) had been displaced — either internally or as international refugees.

b. Where people fled to:

  • The majority of Syrian refugees fled to neighbouring countries: Turkey (host to ~3.6 million Syrians — the world's largest refugee population), Lebanon (~865,000 — making up ~13% of Lebanon's entire population), and Jordan (~660,000).
  • Many attempted the dangerous journey to Europe, particularly Germany, Sweden, and Greece, triggering the "European migrant crisis" of 2015.

c. Table of impacts of the Syrian refugee crisis:

Category Origin Country (Syria) Destination Countries Migrants Themselves
Social Population collapse — cities like Aleppo and Homs lost 80%+ of residents; schools, hospitals, and social fabric destroyed. Lebanon's schools, hospitals, and housing stretched severely (13% of population are Syrian refugees). Cultural tensions between host and refugee communities. Severe trauma, family separation, loss of home and community. Children lost years of education. Women and children vulnerable to exploitation in transit.
Economic GDP collapsed by over 60%. Brain drain — doctors, engineers, teachers left. Infrastructure destroyed. Economy dependent on foreign aid. Turkey, Jordan, and Lebanon spent billions on refugee services, straining national budgets. However, refugee camps also created local economic activity (construction, services). Most lost all savings, property, and assets. Many forced to work illegally for below-minimum wages. Some professionals unable to have qualifications recognised in host countries.
Political Government legitimacy contested; ongoing civil war; Assad government reconstructing with Russian/Iranian support. The 2015 migration crisis fuelled right-wing nationalist movements across Europe; contributed to Brexit debate in UK. Strained EU solidarity on refugee burden-sharing. Stateless or with uncertain legal status. Long asylum processing times cause prolonged uncertainty and stress.

Note: Students choosing the Ukrainian refugee crisis (Source D) should apply the same framework — causes (Russian invasion, February 2022), destinations (Poland, Germany, Czech Republic, Moldova), and impacts on origin/destination/migrants.
