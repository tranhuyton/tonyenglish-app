Cambridge IGCSE Geography • Topic 7: Changing Towns and Cities

7.2 The Opportunities and Challenges of Urbanisation

Examine the twin faces of rapid city growth: unlocking economic productivity and agglomeration economies versus severe challenges including urban sprawl, gentrification displacement, squatter settlements (favelas), and environmental degradation.

1. Economic & Social Opportunities of Urbanisation

Cities are the primary growth engines of the global economy, generating over 80% of global GDP. Urbanisation provides immense advantages for businesses, governments, and individual residents through agglomeration economies:

Economic Dynamism & Agglomeration
  • Clustering of Talent & Capital: Spatial concentration of banks, tech incubators, venture capital, and prestigious universities accelerates innovation.
  • Formal Employment & Higher Incomes: Access to legal, salaried jobs in corporate finance, manufacturing, legal services, and creative tech with wages 2x to 5x higher than rural farm labor.
  • Supply Chain Efficiency: Lowers transportation and logistics costs due to proximity between suppliers, manufacturers, ports, and consumer markets.
Social, Educational & Cultural Vitality
  • Specialized Healthcare & Education: Concentrated presence of tertiary referral teaching hospitals, specialized cancer facilities, top-tier secondary schools, and universities.
  • Cosmopolitan Multiculturalism: Cultural diversity in cuisine, arts, music, and international entrepreneurship.
  • Infrastructure Economies of Scale: Delivering piped water, high-speed fiber internet, and public bus/metro transit costs far less per capita in dense cities than in dispersed rural villages.

Gentrification vs. Social Segregation: Case Study Cape Town

Gentrification is the process where affluent, middle-class individuals purchase and renovate run-down properties in low-income inner-city neighborhoods. While it improves the physical housing stock and attracts cafes and businesses, it drastically drives up rents and property taxes, forcing traditional working-class residents out.

3. Challenges in LICs & NEEs: Squatter Settlements & Favelas

When the rate of rural-to-urban migration outstrips the municipal government's capacity to build roads, lay water pipes, and construct affordable housing, incoming migrants establish squatter settlements (also termed favelas in Brazil, bustees in India, or shanty towns).

4. Managing Squatter Settlements: The Favela-Bairro Upgrade Scheme

Historically, governments responded to squatter settlements with authoritarian demolition and forced eviction. However, modern urban planning favors in-situ upgrading and site-and-service schemes, recognized globally as far more humane, cost-effective, and sustainable.

Comparative Cutaway: Squatter Settlement Hazards vs. The Favela-Bairro Upgrade Scheme

Contrasting the acute physical and social vulnerabilities of spontaneous informal slums with integrated municipal improvement schemes.

Landslide & Collapse Risk: Precarious shacks built on 40° steep deforested hillsides with no foundations; mudslides wipe out homes. • Zero Sanitation: Raw sewage runs down open unpaved street gutters; contamination spreads cholera, hepatitis, and typhoid. • Water Poverty: No municipal piped drinking water; residents pay extortionate prices (5x–10x city rate) to private mafia water tankers. • Fire Hazards ("Gatos"): Web of pirated illegal electric wires sparks catastrophic fires; zero fire-engine road access. • Drug Cartel & Gang Rule: Absence of formal police presence; cartels control territory; youth trapped in violent informal economy. Life Expectancy: Often 15–20 years lower than wealthy zones FAVELA-BAIRRO UPGRADE (Integrated Site & Service) Government Solutions & Investments ($300M+): • Engineered Retaining Walls: Reinforced concrete barriers stabilize steep slopes, preventing fatal mudslides during monsoons. • Underground Sewage & Drainage: Paved streets with covered sewer pipes eradicate waterborne diseases and allow ambulance access. • Piped Chlorinated Water & Legal Grid: Formal electricity meters installed with safe wiring and free municipal refuse collection points. • Public Transit Integration: Cable car networks (Teleférico) connect mountaintop neighborhoods to central rail stations in 15 mins. • Legal Land Titling: Formal property deeds grant tenure security, incentivizing residents to build permanent brick homes and open shops. Community Transformation: Slum transformed into recognized neighborhood

Detailed Case Study: Favela-Bairro Project (Rio de Janeiro, Brazil)

Core Components of the $300 Million Favela-Bairro Program:
  • Slope Stabilization: Construction of massive reinforced concrete retaining walls and engineered stormwater channels, saving thousands of lives during seasonal tropical deluges.
  • Basic Utility Reticulation: Laying underground water pipes, connecting homes to chlorinated water and legal sewage disposal, reducing waterborne diarrheal disease by over 60%.
  • Public Transport Accessibility: Installing the Teleférico do Alemão cable car system, allowing residents to fly above steep hills to the central commuter rail line in 15 minutes instead of a 2-hour treacherous walk.
  • Social Amenities: Building health clinics, primary schools, leisure parks, and free daycares, freeing mothers to secure formal employment.
  • Land Tenure Security: Issuing formal legal property deeds, turning illegal squatters into recognized homeowners who take pride in upgrading their properties with brick and tile.
💡 Cambridge IGCSE Examination Strategy: Topic 7.2

When answering 7-mark case study questions on urban challenges:
1. Differentiate HIC vs. LIC problems: Do not discuss favelas or lack of clean water when asked about an HIC city (discuss traffic congestion, air quality, urban sprawl, or gentrification instead).
2. Quote specific case study facts: Mention Rio de Janeiro's Favela-Bairro project, cable car systems, concrete retaining walls, or Phoenix's automobile sprawl eating up desert land.
3. Evaluate solutions objectively: Note both benefits and limitations (e.g., gentrification upgrades buildings but prices out working-class locals; upgrading favelas improves services but municipal budgets cannot cover every slum).
