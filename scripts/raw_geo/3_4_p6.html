TASK 1: Study Source A

Source A: Palm oil plantation expansion

Driven by demand for palm oil in food and industrial demand, global oil palm production has more than doubled over the last 20 years. Between 1997 and 2018, palm oil plantations have expanded from 100 thousand km² to 210 thousand km², and the production of palm oil has increased from 100 million tons to 300 million tons. This has resulted in the deforestation of large areas of tropical rainforest.
  1. a. Calculate the percentage increase in the area under palm oil plantations between 1997 and 2018.
  2. b. Explain why planting oil palms in place of tropical rainforest decreases biodiversity.
  3. c. In pairs, discuss the sustainable use of rainforests. What do you think could be done to protect the tropical rainforests that are being felled for palm oil, while still protecting the palm oil industry? Write a list of expanded bullet points, giving your main ideas.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Calculation of percentage increase:

  • Area in 1997: 100 thousand km²
  • Area in 2018: 210 thousand km²
  • Increase in area: $210 - 100 = 110 ext{ thousand km}^2$
  • Percentage increase: $ rac{110}{100} imes 100\% =$ 110%.

b. Why replacing rainforest with oil palms decreases biodiversity:

  • Monoculture vs Stratified Multi-layered Forest: Primary tropical rainforests feature hundreds of tree species per hectare and four stratified vertical layers supporting complex microhabitats. Palm oil plantations are single-species monocultures planted in uniform grids, with clear ground cover and zero vertical structural diversity.
  • Destruction of Ecological Niches: Forest canopy mammals, primates (e.g. orangutans, gibbons), bats, and specialised insects lose their ancestral feeding and nesting trees. Oil palm trees produce only one type of fruit, offering no food for native rainforest herbivores.
  • Chemical Contamination & Fragmentation: Heavy use of chemical pesticides, herbicides, and chemical fertilisers degrades soil biodiversity and pollutes forest streams, while plantation roads fragment wildlife corridors and expose remaining forest fragments to edge effects.

c. Sustainable strategies to protect rainforests while supporting palm oil production:

  • RSPO Certification & Zero-Deforestation Standards: Mandate compliance with the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil (RSPO) certification, ensuring that no primary rainforest, peatland, or High Conservation Value (HCV) areas are cleared.
  • Planting Exclusively on Degraded Land: Enforce national zoning laws restricting new oil palm expansion to previously cleared, degraded agricultural land or abandoned pastures, strictly banning any encroachment into virgin forest.
  • Yield Intensification Through Biotechnology: Invest in high-yielding seed varieties, better agricultural extension services, and precision agriculture so smallholders can produce more palm oil per hectare without clearing more land.
  • Preserving Riparian Corridors & Forest Buffers: Legally require plantation developers to preserve continuous forest strips along river systems (riparian buffer zones) to maintain wildlife migration corridors, preserve water quality, and avoid soil runoff.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Country with an increase in rainforest cover share from 2010:

  • Brazil: Between 2010 and 2020, Brazil's line clearly turns upward from approximately 56% to nearly 59% due to reforestation initiatives, stronger satellite monitoring (DETER/PRODES), and the expansion of indigenous reserves and protected areas.

b. Why some countries only display data starting in 1990:

  • Standardised Satellite Remote Sensing: High-resolution satellite Earth observation programs (e.g. Landsat) and FAO Global Forest Resources Assessments (FRA) were only standardized globally around 1990. Developing countries (such as Costa Rica, Bolivia, Cameroon, and Sri Lanka) lacked comprehensive historical aerial photography or ground survey records before 1990.

c. Description of change in rainforest cover for the Philippines:

  • Severe Historical Collapse (1934–1990): Forest cover dropped catastrophically from 57% in 1934 to an all-time low of approximately 21% by 1990 (a massive loss of 36 percentage points, driven by commercial logging and post-war agricultural resettlement).
  • Subsequent Modest Rebound & Stabilization (1990–2020): Rebounded slightly to around 26% in the mid-1990s following national commercial logging bans and reforestation laws, before stabilizing between 23% and 24% through to 2020.

d. General trend for most countries in the graph:

  • Consistent Downward Decline: For the overwhelming majority of tropical countries (Costa Rica, Bolivia, Cameroon, Sri Lanka), the trend is a steady and ongoing decrease in the percentage of land covered by rainforest due to persistent agricultural conversion, logging, and infrastructure development.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Guidelines for Annotated Sketch Diagram of Source C:

  • Stepped Benches / Terraces: Wide horizontal terraces cut into the steep hillside to stabilise open-pit mining operations and provide access for heavy haul trucks.
  • Exposed Red Subsoil & Bedrock: Stripped surface devoid of vegetation, exposing iron-rich mineral ores.
  • Surrounding Primary Canopy: Abrupt, sharp boundary where intact dense green tropical rainforest meets the excavated mining crater.
  • Access Infrastructure: Winding dirt haul roads connecting extraction pits to ore processing facilities.

b. How deforestation and torrential rainfall affect this steep slope:

  • Loss of Interception & Root Binding: Deforestation removes the canopy leaves that intercept tropical downpours and the deep root network that mechanically anchors regolith to bedrock.
  • Accelerated Gully Erosion: Torrential equatorial rainstorms strike bare slopes directly, causing severe splash erosion and deep gully incisions across terrace faces.
  • Mass Movement (Landslides & Mudflows): Saturated soil increases pore water pressure and shear stress, triggering catastrophic slope collapse and mudslides.
  • River Siltation & Toxic Pollution: Runoff washes mine tailings and heavy metals into nearby rivers, choking aquatic life, silting reservoirs, and poisoning drinking water for downstream communities.

c. Why lack of topsoil severely hinders rehabilitation:

  • Destruction of Gersmehl Nutrient Cycling: Over 80% of rainforest nutrients are held in the living biomass and rapidly recycled through a shallow leaf litter layer. Tropical subsoils are deeply leached, acidic, and sterile. Stripping topsoil removes all organic nitrogen, phosphorus, and microbial flora.
  • Soil Compaction & Desiccation: Heavy excavation equipment compacts the bare ground into an impenetrable hardpan that repels water and prevents roots from penetrating.

Improved Answers for "IMPROVE THIS ANSWER" (Source D):

Improved Answer 1 (Describing Trends with Data):
Between 2000 and 2015, global tropical deforestation rates fluctuated within a moderate band of approximately 2.2 to 3.2 million hectares per year. In 2016, there was a sudden, dramatic spike where tree cover loss more than doubled, reaching an all-time peak of approximately 5.6 million hectares. Following 2016, the deforestation rate dropped sharply back to roughly 3.2 million hectares in 2017 before climbing steadily again to approximately 4.0 million hectares by 2020–2023. Overall, the long-term trend shows a net increase in annual deforestation from ~2.3 million hectares in 2000 to nearly 4.0 million hectares in 2023.

Improved Answer 2 (Explaining Causes with Specific Geographical Terminology):
The persistent deforestation and its fluctuations are driven by global economic demands and climatic events. The dramatic 2016 spike was largely caused by a powerful El Niño event, which produced severe drought and extreme heat across the Amazon and Southeast Asia, causing agricultural slash-and-burn fires to rage out of control into primary rainforest. The sustained baseline rate of 3 to 4 million hectares per year is driven by commercial agribusiness: cattle ranching (which accounts for ~80% of Amazon clearing), industrial soy farming, and oil palm plantations in Indonesia and Malaysia. Logging infrastructure, illegal mineral mining, and government-sponsored road construction (e.g. Trans-Amazonian Highway) open remote forests to commercial settlers.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Model Summary (approx. 290 words): How ecotourism, responsible logging, and wildlife protection support sustainability:

Sustainable rainforest management requires harmonising environmental preservation with economic livelihoods. Three interconnected strategies—ecotourism, responsible logging, and wildlife conservation—provide a robust framework to achieve this.

Ecotourism offers a viable, non-destructive economic alternative to slash-and-burn agriculture and open-pit mining. By creating revenue through national park entrance fees, local naturalist guides, and eco-lodges, it directly incentivises indigenous communities and regional governments to preserve standing forests rather than clear them for quick timber sales. Revenue generated can be reinvested into anti-poaching forest ranger patrols, local healthcare, and schooling.

Responsible logging, championed through selective felling and Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) guidelines, extracts commercial timber while preserving broader forest integrity. Only fully mature trees of designated species are felled, following strict 25- to 40-year regeneration cycles that leave surrounding sub-canopy layers and seed trees intact. Directional felling and designated skid tracks minimise collateral damage to neighbouring trees, safeguarding canopy cover, soil root binding, and hydrological cycles.

Wildlife protection, enforced through national reserves and bans on trade in endangered species, safeguards crucial ecological functions. Rainforest animals—such as toucans, bats, and agoutis—are vital primary seed dispersers and pollinators. Protecting biodiversity maintains the trophic equilibrium of the forest, ensuring natural botanical regeneration and long-term ecosystem resilience. Together, these measures demonstrate that standing forests possess greater long-term economic and ecological value than cleared pasture.

b. One other method to manage rainforest ecosystems (referencing Table 3.4):

  • Agroforestry (Shade-grown multi-cropping): Growing valuable cash crops (e.g. cacao, coffee, vanilla, medicinal herbs) under the shade of preserved primary canopy trees. This prevents clear-felling for open cattle pastures, preserves soil fertility, prevents erosion, and provides stable diversified income for rural families.
  • Alternative valid methods: Debt-for-Nature Swaps (HICs forgive sovereign debt in exchange for enforceable rainforest reserve creation), or REDD+ carbon credit schemes (paying nations to keep forests standing as carbon sinks).

c. Four key challenges in implementing sustainable rainforest management:

  • 1. Vast Scale and Inadequate Enforcement Resources: Tropical rainforests cover millions of square kilometres with dense tree cover and inaccessible terrain. Environmental agencies in LICs/MICs lack the budget, satellite technology, and patrol personnel to detect and intercept illegal logging and wildcat gold miners (garimpeiros).
  • 2. Economic Poverty & Subsistence Pressure: Impoverished local populations often have no other economic alternative to feed their families, forcing them into illegal charcoal burning, bushmeat poaching, or slash-and-burn farming.
  • 3. Powerful Corporate Lobbies & Political Corruption: Multibillion-dollar cattle ranching, timber, and mining conglomerates wield massive political influence, frequently lobbying governments to weaken environmental regulations, grant concessions, or ignore indigenous land rights.
  • 4. Land Tenure Insecurity & Conflict: Many indigenous communities lack legally demarcated land titles, making it difficult to legally defend their ancestral forests against violent encroachment and illegal land grabbers.
