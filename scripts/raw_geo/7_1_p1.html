Cambridge IGCSE Geography • Topic 7: Changing Towns and Cities

7.1 Where People Live

Investigate the spatial organization of human settlements: defining urban and rural spaces, analyzing site and situation factors, mastering Walter Christaller's Central Place Theory and the Settlement Hierarchy, understanding global urbanization rates, and tracking the four phases of the Urbanisation Cycle.

1. Urban and Rural Settlements: Site and Situation Factors

A settlement is any place where people live, establish homes, and construct communities. Human settlements range in scale from a solitary, isolated farmstead in the Scottish Highlands to colossal megacities exceeding 30 million residents, such as Tokyo or Shanghai.

Geographers classify settlements into two overarching categories based on functional characteristics:

Rural Settlements (Countryside)
  • Economic Base: Predominantly primary sector economic activities (agriculture, forestry, livestock farming, fishing, and mining).
  • Physical Characteristics: Low population density, spacious open green land, low building heights, and limited specialized services.
  • Community Scale: Hamlets, small farming villages, and scattered homesteads where social connections are tightly knit.
Urban Settlements (Towns & Cities)
  • Economic Base: Dominated by secondary (manufacturing) and tertiary/quaternary sectors (commerce, banking, education, IT, administration).
  • Physical Characteristics: High population density, high density of paved roads, multistory infrastructure, and complex municipal networks.
  • Functional Role: Acts as an economic, political, and cultural hub serving a broad regional or international sphere of influence.

Site vs. Situation: Physical Factors Influencing Early Settlements

When early humans transitioned from nomadic foraging to permanent settlement, the choice of location was governed by rigorous physical geographical advantages:

• Site (Vị trí điểm): The actual physical land upon which a settlement is built (relief, elevation, soil type, water table).
• Situation (Vị trí thế): The location of the settlement relative to surrounding physical and human features (trade routes, river crossings, neighboring settlements).

Key Historical Site Factors:
  • Wet-point Site: Located near a reliable freshwater source (spring, stream) in an otherwise arid area.
  • Dry-point Site: Built on slightly elevated ground to escape seasonal marsh flooding.
  • Defensive Site: On a high hilltop, incised meander loop, or rocky promontory to repel invaders (e.g., Edinburgh Castle, Durham).
  • Bridging Point: Established where a river is narrow or shallow enough to be bridged or forded (e.g., Cambridge, Oxford).
  • Aspect & Shelter: South-facing slopes in the Northern Hemisphere receiving maximum sunlight and sheltered from cold north winds.
  • Nodal Point (Crossroads): At the convergence of multiple natural valleys or trade routes, fostering commercial growth.

2. The Settlement Hierarchy & Central Place Theory

If you examine all the settlements within an entire administrative county or nation, an orderly mathematical pattern emerges: there are vast numbers of tiny villages, fewer towns, and only a tiny handful of major cities. Geographers arrange these into a Settlement Hierarchy.

Central Place Theory (Walter Christaller, 1933)

To explain why services and settlements organize themselves in this hierarchical geometry, German geographer Walter Christaller formulated Central Place Theory, establishing three fundamental concepts:

1. Range of a Good or Service (Phạm vi tiếp cận): The maximum distance a consumer is willing to travel to purchase a particular good or service. For everyday low-order convenience goods (bread, milk, newspapers), people travel only a few hundred meters. For specialized high-order comparison goods (designer fashion, high-end motor vehicles, tertiary university degrees, brain surgery), people are willing to travel tens or hundreds of kilometers.
2. Threshold Population (Dân số ngưỡng): The minimum number of permanent customers required to generate enough demand and revenue for a service or commercial business to remain profitable. A newsagent requires a threshold of only ~500 people; a medium supermarket needs ~5,000 people; a specialized department store or major surgical hospital needs a threshold of 100,000+ residents.
3. Sphere of Influence (Vùng ảnh hưởng / Lưu vực phục vụ): The geographic catchment area from which a central place draws its customers. High-order central places have gigantic spheres of influence that completely overlap and encompass the smaller spheres of influence of surrounding lower-order villages.

3. Settlement Patterns and Morphologies

Beyond hierarchical rank, settlements exhibit distinct spatial shapes or layout patterns across the landscape, largely dictated by physical terrain and historical transport axes:

1. Nucleated (Clustered)

Buildings are clustered closely together, commonly radiating around a central focal node such as a village green, church, or crossroads.

Causes: Collective defense in medieval times, agricultural commons, or scarcity of localized potable spring water.
2. Linear (Ribbon)

Buildings follow a long, narrow line strung along a physical constraint or transport spine, such as a major road, river bank, canal, or valley floor.

Causes: Steep valley sides that restrict building to a narrow flood bench, or maximizing direct access to a transport artery.
3. Dispersed (Scattered)

Isolated dwellings, farms, and cottages scattered across wide geographic distances with no recognizable central gathering point.

Causes: Extensive pastoral livestock farming (hill sheep), rugged mountainous topography, or thick forest cover.

4. The Process and Global Rate of Urbanisation

Urbanisation is defined as the increasing proportion of a country's total population living in urban areas (towns and cities) compared to rural areas. It is an unstoppable global structural transition.

Crucial Distinction: Urban Level vs. Urban Rate

⚠️ Common Exam Confusion: Level vs. Rate

• Level of Urbanisation (%): The current proportion of people residing in urban centers. HICs (e.g., UK 84%, Japan 92%, Australia 86%) have high levels of urbanisation, but their annual growth rate is virtually zero or slowing.
• Rate of Urbanisation (% per year): The speed at which an urban population is growing. LICs and NEEs (e.g., Nigeria, India, Bangladesh) currently have lower levels of urbanisation (35%–50%), but exhibit the fastest rates of urbanisation on Earth (>3% to 5% annually) due to massive rural-to-urban migration and high natural increase.

💡 Cambridge IGCSE Examination Strategy: Topic 7.1

When answering questions on settlement and urbanization:
1. Use Central Place terminology correctly: Always integrate the terms range, threshold population, and sphere of influence when explaining why low-order services are found in villages whereas high-order services require large cities.
2. Clearly explain Site vs. Situation: Remember that "site" is the ground underneath the town (e.g., a flat gravel river terrace for flood avoidance), while "situation" relates to its geographical connectivity to surrounding regions.
3. Differentiate the 4 phases of urban movement: Be prepared to explain why people suburbanize (larger homes, car ownership) versus why they counter-urbanize (escaping city pollution, retirement, teleworking) or re-urbanize (dockland luxury flats, cultural nightlife).
