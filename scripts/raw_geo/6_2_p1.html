6.2 Population Structures Change Over Time

Examine how age and gender compositions evolve across nations: mastering population pyramids, calculating Dependency Ratios, contrasting the challenges of Youthful populations with Ageing societies, and analyzing demographic disparities in India.

1. Population Structure, Cohorts & The Dependency Ratio

While total population size dictates a country's absolute resource consumption, the structure of the population—specifically its division by age and sex—governs its economic vitality, social demands, and infrastructural requirements.

Demographers divide any national population into three primary functional age cohorts:

1. Young Dependents (Aged 0–14)

Children who do not contribute to national tax revenues and require massive state investment in pediatric healthcare, immunizations, primary and secondary schooling, and family allowances.

2. Economically Active (Aged 15–64)

The national labor force. This cohort works, pays income taxes, produces goods and services, and economically sustains the dependent cohorts.

3. Elderly Dependents (Aged 65 and over)

Retired citizens who rely on accumulated state pensions, geriatric medical care, chronic illness treatment, and residential nursing facilities.

Calculating the Dependency Ratio

The Dependency Ratio is an indispensable demographic indicator that measures the financial and care burden placed upon the working-age population by non-working dependents:

Dependency Ratio = [(% Pop aged 0–14) + (% Pop aged 65+)] / (% Pop aged 15–64) × 100
Expressed as the number of dependents supported by every 100 economically active workers.

This ratio can be subdivided into the Youth Dependency Ratio: [(0–14) / (15–64)] × 100 and the Old-Age Dependency Ratio: [(65+) / (15–64)] × 100.

Reading Anomalies, Indents and Asymmetries

Experienced geographers look beyond the broad outline of a pyramid to detect historical disruptions:

  • Indentations / Notches: A localized narrowing across a specific age group. For instance, wars leave severe notches on the male side for cohorts who were military-age during conflict. Famines and epidemics (such as the 1918 influenza or Cambodian genocide) suppress births and elevate infant mortality, leaving a permanent waistline indent that climbs up the pyramid over decades.
  • Bulges (Baby Booms & Labor In-Migration): Post-crisis fertility rebounds create wide cohorts that advance over time (e.g., the 1946–1964 baby boom now entering the 65+ category). In Gulf oil states (UAE, Qatar), pyramids exhibit extreme lateral asymmetry, with male cohorts aged 20–49 protruding 3 to 4 times wider than corresponding female bars due to hundreds of thousands of foreign construction and engineering guest workers.
  • The Gender Longevity Divergence: In virtually every country on Earth, the female side of the pyramid is visibly wider and taller above age 75. Biologically and behaviorally, women enjoy greater life expectancy than men (averaging 4 to 6 years longer in HICs).

3. The Youthful Population Challenge & Case Study: The Gambia

A youthful population occurs when a high proportion of a country's total population is under the age of 15 (frequently 40% to 50%). It is the demographic hallmark of Stage 2 nations experiencing rapid natural increase.

Detailed Case Study: The Republic of The Gambia

The Gambia is a small West African nation of roughly 2.7 million people. While its Total Fertility Rate has declined from 6.1 in 1970 to approximately 5.1 in recent years, its rate of natural increase remains exceptionally high at nearly 3.0% per year, meaning its population doubles in roughly 24 years.

Key Socio-Economic Impacts in The Gambia:
  • Severe Pressure on Education: With ~44% of the population under 15, the state cannot build schools fast enough. Class sizes routinely exceed 50–60 pupils, leading to a mandatory two-shift school system. Shortages of textbooks, desks, and qualified teachers depress literacy rates.
  • Healthcare Infrastructure Strain: High maternal mortality rates and inadequate pediatric immunization clinics. Basic childhood illnesses like diarrheal dehydration and malaria cause preventable infant deaths.
  • Deforestation & Environmental Crisis: Wood is the principal cooking fuel for 85%+ of households. Rapid population growth has decimated surrounding forest reserves around the capital Banjul, leading to severe soil erosion and advancing Sahel desertification.
  • Youth Unemployment & Rural-Urban Migration: Thousands of youth migrate from rural farming regions to the urbanized coastal strip (Serrekunda/Banjul), creating sprawling informal settlements, sanitation deficits, and high youth underemployment.

4. The Ageing Population Crisis & Case Study: The European Union & Japan

Demographic ageing is defined as a persistent rise in the median age of a population. It occurs when fertility rates fall below replacement level (2.1) while medical advances extend average life expectancy well beyond 80 years.

Figure 6.18: European Union projections (2000–2070). The working-age population contracts continuously after 2010, while the old-age dependency ratio surges from 25% to nearly 60% by 2070.

Detailed Case Study: Japan's Hyper-Aged Society

Japan is the world's most demographically aged nation. By 2023, nearly 30% of its population was aged 65 or older, and its total population has been shrinking by roughly 500,000 to 800,000 people each year since peaking in 2008 at 128 million.

Structural Ramifications for Japan:
  • Fiscal Pension Crisis: Japan operates a "pay-as-you-go" pension model. In 1960, there were roughly 11 workers per retiree; today there are barely 2 workers per retiree, and by 2050 this will drop to 1.3 workers. The national debt has ballooned to over 260% of GDP.
  • Labor Shortages & Economic Stagnation: Chronic shortages of bus drivers, agricultural workers, construction crew, and elderly care nurses. Tens of thousands of schools have closed permanently across rural prefectures. Over 9 million rural homes ("Akiya") sit abandoned.
  • Surging Geriatric Healthcare Demands: Soaring prevalence of dementia, Alzheimer's, and chronic cardiovascular conditions requiring dedicated nursing home beds and high government subsidization.
  • Technological Adaptation: Japan is pioneering robotic elder care (carebots like "Paro", robotic lift exoskeletons for nurses, automated delivery drones) and raising the retirement age to 70.

5. Regional Demographic Disparities: Case Study of India

National demographic averages often mask dramatic internal variations within large countries. India—the world's most populous nation with over 1.43 billion people—provides a vivid geographical illustration of regional demographic divergence.

Figure 6.19: Total fertility rate by state in India (2019–2020), showing stark divergence between northern high-fertility states (Bihar: 3.0, Uttar Pradesh: 2.4) and southern below-replacement states (Kerala: 1.8, Tamil Nadu: 1.8).
Northern Hindi Heartland (Bihar, UP)
  • TFR remains significantly above replacement level (Bihar ~3.0, UP ~2.4).
  • Lower female literacy rates (~50–60%), early age of marriage, and strong agrarian dependence.
  • Rapid population growth creates intense pressure on arable land, resulting in mass labor migration toward southern and western industrial hubs.
Southern States (Kerala, Tamil Nadu)
  • TFR has plunged well below replacement level to 1.7–1.8 births per woman.
  • Kerala achieved near-100% female literacy decades ago; women possess high social status, autonomy, and enter white-collar professions.
  • Comprehensive primary healthcare and family planning clinics have reduced infant mortality to single digits. These states are now entering Stage 4/5 aging issues.

When answering questions comparing youthful vs. ageing population pyramids:
1. Use precise cohort brackets: Refer explicitly to Young Dependents (0–14), Economically Active (15–64), and Elderly Dependents (65+).
2. Calculate and compare Dependency Ratios: Showing your work using the formula earns direct analytical credit.
3. Link pyramid shape to the DTM: State clearly whether the pyramid is expansive (wide base, Stage 2), stationary (columnar, Stage 4), or constrictive/inverted (narrow base, Stage 5).
