10.2 The Global Patterns of Food Supply and Demand

A global geographical analysis of food distribution: spatial inequalities in daily calorie supply; the dietary transition toward animal protein; agro-industrial vertical integration; the spatial geography of food waste; and the dominant power of multinational food corporations in international trade.

1

Global Calorie Supply Patterns & The Nutrition Transition

Nghe cả phần

Although global agriculture produces more than enough total calories to feed all 8 billion people on Earth, food supply is distributed with profound geographical inequality. While high-income nations suffer from chronic caloric surplus and food waste, millions across low-income regions experience caloric deficits and acute food insecurity.

Figure 10.9: Global variation in the daily supply of calories per person in 2021 (Hodder Education).

Spatial Pattern Analysis: North America and Europe register average consumption exceeding 3,400–3,800 kcal/person/day (well above the WHO recommended 2,000–2,500 kcal baseline). Conversely, widespread swathes of Sub-Saharan Africa (such as Chad, CAR, and Madagascar) and parts of South Asia average below 2,100–2,200 kcal/day, failing to meet basic metabolic requirements for active labor.

The "Nutrition Transition" (Sự Chuyển Dịch Dinh Dưỡng)

As developing countries urbanise and experience income growth (e.g. China, India, Brazil), national diets undergo a rapid shift known as the Nutrition Transition: populations consume fewer traditional starchy root vegetables and coarse grains, and dramatically increase their intake of meat, dairy, refined sugar, fats, and ultra-processed foods. This sparks a dramatic rise in global feed crop demand (soy and maize) and non-communicable diseases (diabetes, heart disease).

Food Waste Geography & Multinational Agribusiness Corporations

Nghe cả phần

According to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), approximately one-third of all food produced globally (~1.3 billion tonnes per year) is wasted or lost. However, the geographic point of loss differs radically between economic worlds:

Food Loss in Developing Nations (LEDCs)

Losses occur predominantly at the upstream production and post-harvest stages: lack of paved roads, absence of refrigerated cold-storage trucks, inadequate grain silos vulnerable to rats and moisture, and inability to process perishable surpluses.

Food Waste in Developed Nations (MEDCs)

Losses occur at the downstream retail and consumer end: aesthetic cosmetic standards by supermarkets (rejecting misshapen fruit), confusion over "best before" vs "use by" dates, promotional multi-buys ("buy-one-get-one-free"), and consumer over-purchasing.
