TASK 1
Source A: Population pyramid for the UK, projected 2030
Source B: Population pyramid for Somalia, projected 2030

Source A shows the population pyramid for the UK, a typical HIC. Source B shows that of Somalia, a typical LIC.

  1. Estimate the percentage of children 0-14
    1. in the UK and
    2. in Somalia.
  2. Estimate the percentage of elderly over the age of 65
    1. in the UK and
    2. in Somalia.
  3. What does the difference between these numbers in the two countries show?
  4. Describe three differences in the shape and structure of the two population pyramids.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Estimated percentage of children aged 0–14:

  • i. UK: Approximately 17–18% of the population. The UK's pyramid has a relatively narrow base, reflecting decades of low birth rates (below replacement level of 2.1 TFR).
  • ii. Somalia: Approximately 45–50% of the population. Somalia's wide, rapidly tapering pyramid base reflects one of the world's highest total fertility rates (~6 children per woman).

b. Estimated percentage of elderly aged 65+:

  • i. UK: Approximately 18–20%. The UK has a large ageing population due to low birth rates and high life expectancy (~82 years).
  • ii. Somalia: Approximately 2–3%. Very few Somalians survive to old age due to lower life expectancy (~57 years), reflecting limited healthcare and ongoing conflict.

c. What the differences show:

  • The contrast reveals the two extremes of the Demographic Transition Model. Somalia has a youthful population with high fertility and low life expectancy — typical of a Stage 2 LIC. The UK has an ageing population with low fertility and high life expectancy — typical of a Stage 4/5 HIC.
  • This has major implications: Somalia faces a "youth bulge" with pressure on education, food, and future employment; the UK faces an "ageing crisis" with pressure on pensions, healthcare, and a shrinking working-age workforce.

d. Three differences in the shape and structure of the two pyramids:

  • 1. Base width (birth rate): Somalia's pyramid has an extremely wide base (many children per family; high birth rate), while the UK's base is narrow (few children per family; low birth rate near replacement level).
  • 2. Shape of sides (death rate through ages): Somalia's pyramid tapers steeply and concavely — large numbers of people die in childhood and young adulthood, leaving fewer survivors at older ages. The UK's pyramid has nearly vertical sides up to age ~60, showing that most people survive well into older age before dying.
  • 3. Width at the top (life expectancy / ageing population): Somalia's pyramid has a very narrow apex — few elderly people. The UK's pyramid is wide at the top, showing a substantial elderly population that has grown larger with each generation due to rising life expectancy. The UK's pyramid is sometimes described as "barrel-shaped" or "inverted-triangle" rather than a true pyramid.
TASK 2
Source C: Population pyramids showing Japan's ageing population (1990, 2020, 2050)
  1. Estimate the predicted number of elderly dependents in Japan in 2050.
  2. Use the three population pyramids to describe the changes in Japan's ageing population over time.
  3. Japan is a HIC. Suggest why death rates are low but birth rates are lower.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Estimated number of elderly dependents in Japan in 2050:

  • Based on Japan's projected 2050 population pyramid, the elderly population (65+) is expected to represent approximately 35–38% of Japan's total population (~125 million projected, so roughly 44–47 million elderly people).
  • This makes Japan's ageing population one of the most extreme in the world — by 2050, there will be approximately 1 working-age person for every 1 elderly dependent, an unsustainable ratio for the pension and healthcare system.

b. Changes in Japan's ageing population shown by the three pyramids:

  • Trend 1 — Narrowing base: Across all three pyramids (e.g., 1990, 2020, 2050), the base of Japan's pyramid becomes progressively narrower, showing a continuing fall in birth rates. Japan's total fertility rate (TFR) has fallen to around 1.2 — one of the world's lowest — far below the 2.1 replacement rate.
  • Trend 2 — Widening top: The upper sections of the pyramid (elderly age groups, 65+) become progressively wider across the three pyramids, reflecting rapidly increasing life expectancy and a growing proportion of elderly citizens. Japan has among the world's highest life expectancy (~84 years).
  • Trend 3 — Shrinking working-age population: The middle sections (ages 15–64) of the pyramid decrease over time, meaning fewer workers must support a rapidly growing retired population — creating severe economic challenges for pension and healthcare funding.
  • Overall: The pyramid transitions from a roughly barrel/beehive shape to an increasingly "top-heavy" or inverted structure by 2050, indicating population decline and extreme demographic ageing.

c. Why death rates are low but birth rates are even lower in Japan (an HIC):

  • Low death rates result from: world-class healthcare system, high nutritional standards (traditional Japanese diet), universal health insurance, low rates of obesity and cardiovascular disease, and strong public health infrastructure.
  • Very low birth rates are caused by:
    • Extremely high cost of living in urban Japan, especially child-rearing costs (private school, housing, childcare).
    • Long working hours culture (karoshi) — Japanese workers, especially men, face intense pressure to prioritise career over family life, leaving little time or energy for parenting.
    • Female career aspirations: Highly educated Japanese women increasingly prioritise careers and delay or forego marriage and childbearing. Japan's work culture has historically made it difficult for women to balance career and motherhood.
    • Cultural pressure and cost of raising children to high academic standards — the juku (cram school) culture means parents invest enormous time and money per child, incentivising smaller families.
TASK 3
Source D: Active elderly people in an HIC
Source E: Elderly people receiving care in an HIC

Discuss the following questions in pairs:

  1. Identify one economic challenge and one social challenge for a country with an ageing population.
  2. Explain why they are a challenge for the government of a country.
  3. Study Sources D and E. Give two reasons why an ageing population can bring benefits to a country.
  4. What strategies can a government use to try to cut the economic costs of an ageing population?
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. One economic challenge and one social challenge for a country with an ageing population:

  • Economic challenge — Rising pension costs and shrinking tax base: As the proportion of retired people increases, governments must pay out more in state pensions and healthcare while simultaneously receiving less income tax (from a shrinking working-age population). This creates a growing funding gap that may require tax rises, benefit cuts, or borrowing.
  • Social challenge — Increasing strain on healthcare and social care services: Elderly people require significantly more medical care, residential care, and community support than younger people. An ageing population dramatically increases demand for hospitals, care homes, and home nursing services at a time when the working-age population (who provide and fund these services) is shrinking.

b. Why these are a challenge for government:

  • The state pension and NHS (or equivalent healthcare system) are funded primarily by taxes paid by working-age people. An increasing "old-age dependency ratio" means fewer workers must support more retirees — the financial burden per working person rises steeply.
  • Governments face politically difficult choices: raise taxes (unpopular with workers), cut pension/healthcare benefits (unpopular with the elderly, who are a large voting bloc), raise the retirement age (unpopular with workers), or encourage immigration of young workers (politically controversial).
  • The challenge is compounded by demographic momentum — the ageing population will grow regardless of policies adopted today, since the large baby boom generation has already been born and is approaching retirement.

c. Two benefits of an ageing population (from Sources D and E):

  • 1. Grandparents provide informal childcare: Many grandparents provide regular, unpaid childcare for their grandchildren, enabling parents (especially mothers) to return to work. This supports female workforce participation, boosts economic output, and saves families significant childcare costs.
  • 2. The "silver economy" and volunteering contributions: Healthy, active elderly people contribute significantly to the economy through their spending (on leisure, travel, healthcare, goods), their savings (which banks lend for investment), and their voluntary work in schools, hospitals, and community organisations. Many retired people have decades of skills and experience that they contribute through mentoring, consulting, and volunteering.

d. Government strategies to cut economic costs of an ageing population:

  • Raising the retirement age: Increasing the state pension age (e.g., the UK raising it from 65 to 67, and planning to raise it further to 68) means people contribute taxes for longer and receive pensions for fewer years, reducing the government's pension bill.
  • Encouraging private pensions and savings: Governments can mandate workplace pension contributions (auto-enrolment in the UK) to shift some retirement income responsibility from the state to individuals and employers, reducing long-term state pension costs.
  • Encouraging immigration of working-age people: Skilled migrants of working age fill labour shortages, pay taxes, and support the pension system. Countries like Canada and Germany have adopted selective immigration programmes to address ageing demographics.
  • Pro-natalist policies to increase the birth rate: Long-term, increasing the number of children born today means more workers and taxpayers in 20–30 years. France's generous family support policies have helped maintain a relatively higher birth rate than most European neighbours.
  • Increasing productivity through technology: If the remaining working population becomes more productive through automation, AI, and better technology, total economic output can be maintained even as the workforce shrinks.
TASK 4
  1. Create a table listing the economic, social, and environmental challenges and opportunities of a country with a youthful population. You can carry out research to supplement this table.
  2. Uganda has the youngest population in the world. Carry out research to answer the following questions:
    1. What percentage of the Ugandan population are below the age of 30?
    2. Why does Uganda have such a youthful population?
    3. What are some of the problems and some of the benefits of Uganda's youthful population?
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Table of challenges and opportunities of a youthful population:

Category Challenges Opportunities
Economic High youth dependency ratio — many children supported by few working adults; low government tax revenue; high cost of schools and infrastructure; youth unemployment if jobs unavailable. "Demographic dividend" — when the large youth cohort enters the workforce, a surge of productive workers can drive rapid economic growth (as experienced in East Asia 1960s–1990s).
Social Pressure on schools, hospitals, housing; maternal health strain from high fertility; risk of social instability from large numbers of unemployed young people ("youth bulge" effect). Young, energetic, adaptable population can embrace new technologies; potential for large domestic consumer market as incomes rise.
Environmental Rapid population growth increases pressure on land, water, forests; increased food production demand leads to deforestation and soil degradation; rapid urbanisation strains infrastructure and creates pollution. Young populations in LICs have lower per-capita carbon footprints than HIC elderly populations; potential to "leapfrog" to green energy without legacy fossil fuel infrastructure.

b. Uganda — Research answers:

  • i. Percentage of Ugandan population below age 30: Approximately 75–78% of Uganda's population is under 30 years old. Uganda has one of the youngest populations and highest youth proportions in the world.
  • ii. Why Uganda has such a youthful population:
    • Very high total fertility rate (~4.6 children per woman) — one of the highest in the world, though declining slowly from ~7 in the 1980s.
    • Limited access to contraception and family planning services, particularly in rural areas.
    • Cultural and religious norms that value large families — children are seen as a sign of wealth, status, and security in old age.
    • Economic dependency on children — subsistence farming remains dominant; children provide valuable farm labour.
    • Improving healthcare has reduced child mortality, but birth rates remain high, producing rapid natural increase.
  • iii. Problems and benefits of Uganda's youthful population:
    • Problems: The government must build and staff thousands of new schools and hospitals every year just to keep up with population growth. Youth unemployment is severe — Uganda's economy cannot yet create enough jobs for the rapidly growing number of young people entering the workforce. Continued rapid population growth strains food production, water resources, and urban infrastructure.
    • Benefits: Uganda's large, young labour force has enormous potential. If education quality improves and investment increases, Uganda could experience a "demographic dividend" — a period of rapid economic growth driven by a large productive workforce. Uganda also has a growing, youthful consumer market attractive to businesses. The high proportion of working-age people (as the youth cohort ages) could support a small elderly population very easily, avoiding the pension crisis facing ageing countries like Japan.
