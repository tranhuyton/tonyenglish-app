TASK 1: Study Source A

Study Source A: Daily supply of calories per person (2018)

Source A: Daily supply of calories per person (2018)

Globally, calorie intake varies significantly both between countries and within countries. In pairs:

  1. a. Compare the distribution of countries with a high-caloric intake to those with a low-caloric intake.
  2. b. Explain the pattern of calorie intake that you identified in part a.
  3. c. In pairs, carry out research about the calorie intake for your country and then your town. Are there areas that have a lower or higher calorie intake per person?
  4. d. Draw a sketch map of your country and look for patterns of low or high calorie intake per person.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Comparing the global distribution of calorie intake:

  • High-caloric intake (>3,200 to 3,800+ kcal/day): Strongly concentrated in the Global North and High-Income Countries (HICs). Key regions include North America (USA >3,800 kcal, Canada), Western and Northern Europe (Germany, France, UK, Scandinavia), Russia, Australia, New Zealand, and wealthy Middle Eastern nations (e.g. UAE, Saudi Arabia).
  • Moderate-caloric intake (2,600 to 3,200 kcal/day): Found across emerging and middle-income economies in South America (Brazil, Argentina), North Africa (Egypt, Algeria), and parts of East/Southeast Asia (China, Vietnam).
  • Low-caloric intake (<2,100 to 2,300 kcal/day): Heavily clustered in Sub-Saharan Africa (e.g. Chad, Central African Republic, Madagascar, Zimbabwe, Ethiopia) and isolated low-income areas in South/Central Asia (e.g. Afghanistan, Yemen, North Korea). Sub-Saharan Africa forms the largest contiguous belt of calorie deprivation globally.

b. Explaining the pattern of caloric intake:

  • Economic wealth & purchasing power: High GDP per capita in HICs allows consumers to purchase surplus food, dine out, and consume energy-dense processed meats, dairy, and sugars. Low disposable incomes in LICs restrict families to basic, monotonous staple grains with minimal protein or fats.
  • Agricultural technology & yields: HICs employ modern intensive agriculture (high mechanisation, chemical fertilisers, automated irrigation, HYVs, and refrigerated cold-chains), ensuring consistent year-round bumper harvests. LICs rely heavily on rain-fed subsistence farming vulnerable to droughts, floods, and pest infestations.
  • Global trade networks: Wealthy nations have strong currencies to import fresh food globally throughout all seasons. LICs face balance-of-payment constraints, high tariffs, and poor transport infrastructure, making emergency food imports costly.
  • Conflict & political instability: Civil conflict and governance failures in parts of Africa and the Middle East disrupt farming, displace rural populations, and block humanitarian food relief.

c & d. Guidelines for local/national research and sketch map:

  • National overview (e.g. Vietnam / Developing nations): Average calorie intake is around 2,800–3,000 kcal/person/day. Major urban centers (Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Da Nang) exhibit significantly higher calorie consumption with Westernised diets, meat, dairy, and fast foods. Conversely, remote mountainous provinces (Northwest, Central Highlands) report lower calorie intake and less dietary diversity, relying heavily on rice, maize, and root crops.
  • Sketch map requirements: Draw an outline of your country, include a title and north arrow, label major urban conurbations as higher-calorie zones, shade remote/rural areas as lower-calorie zones, and provide an annotated key explaining the regional economic disparity.
TASK 2: Study Source B

Study Source B: Change in fast-food portion sizes and calorie counts (1986–2016)

Source B: The change in portion sizes and calorie value of fast food between 1986 and 2016.
(The average adult human requires 2,000 calories for females and 2,500 calories for males per day).
  1. a. Calculate the increase in calories for desserts between 1986 and 2016.
  2. b. Explain why an increase in the size of fast-food portions would be considered unhealthy.
  3. c. In pairs, discuss the marketing of fast food and how it impacts fast-food sales and the economy of a country, region, or town.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Calculation of dessert calorie increase:

  • Calories in 1986 = 234 kcal
  • Calories in 2016 = 420 kcal
  • Increase = 420 − 234 = 186 calories (an increase of +79.5%).
  • Additional observation: Portion size also grew substantially from 100g to 171g (+71g or +71%).

b. Why larger fast-food portion sizes are considered unhealthy:

  • Caloric surplus & obesity: Fast food is energy-dense and high in refined sugars, saturated/trans fats, and sodium. Oversized single portions easily exceed meal limits, causing prolonged energy surpluses and rapid weight gain.
  • Portion distortion: Consistently serving larger portion sizes distorts consumers' perception of normal serving sizes, conditioning individuals to overeat past natural fullness.
  • Chronic non-communicable diseases: Diets rich in oversized fast-food meals are directly linked to soaring rates of Type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease, and liver complications.
  • Poor nutrient density: Despite excessive calories, fast food lacks dietary fiber, essential vitamins, and micronutrients, contributing to hidden nutritional deficiencies alongside obesity.

c. Fast-food marketing, sales, and economic impacts:

  • Marketing tactics: Value bundling and "upsizing" for negligible marginal prices (e.g. "supersize for $0.50 more"), targeted advertising toward children (happy meals, toy promotions, cartoon mascots), digital app promotions, and partnerships with celebrities/sports events.
  • Positive economic impacts: Generates entry-level employment for youth (cashiers, cooks, delivery riders), creates local franchise business opportunities, pays commercial municipal taxes, and creates reliable demand for industrial food suppliers (meat, poultry, potato farming).
  • Negative economic impacts: Places massive long-term financial strains on national healthcare systems (treating obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular surgeries); causes lost economic productivity due to sick leave and premature mortality; outcompetes and displaces traditional healthier local food vendors.
TASK 3: Study Source C

Study Source C: Strategies to increase crop yields

Source C: Strategies to increase crop yields and food supplies

Negative impacts introduced by these intensive strategies:

  • Mechanisation: Resulted in the loss of agricultural jobs for many farm workers, forcing rural-to-urban migration to look for employment.
  • Agrochemicals: Herbicides, pesticides, and fertilisers cause decreased biodiversity and contaminate surrounding soils and waterways if not carefully managed.
  • High-yield varieties (HYVs & GM crops): Grow faster but require substantial fertiliser and water. Resource-poor farmers who cannot afford expensive inputs are outcompeted by wealthy agribusinesses.
  • Resource pressure: The increased consumption of freshwater and conversion of natural habitats for farmland puts severe strain on ecosystems.
  1. a. Write a 200 to 250-word essay on how the strategies outlined in Source C have increased food output from farms.
  2. b. In pairs, research the main problems that the techniques used to increase food output have caused. Create a spider diagram to show your findings. Consider the following factors in your research:
    • the impact of the increased use of herbicides and pesticides
    • the impact of the increased use of fertilisers
    • the clearing of natural areas for new farmland
    • the overuse or misuse of water resources
    • the mismanagement of soils (soil exhaustion/erosion).

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Model Essay (230 words): How Modern Farming Strategies Have Increased Food Output

Over the past half-century, modern agricultural innovations outlined in Source C have transformed global food production, allowing farm yields to keep pace with rapid population growth.

Foremost among these breakthroughs are High-Yielding Varieties (HYVs) and Genetically Modified (GM) crops. Bred specifically for accelerated maturation, enhanced drought and pest resistance, and higher grain-to-stalk ratios, these crops maximize output per hectare of cultivated land.

Chemical inputs play an equally pivotal role in boosting harvest volumes. Synthetic fertilisers replenish vital soil nutrients—chiefly nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium—accelerating plant development. Simultaneously, chemical herbicides eradicate competing weeds that deprive crops of sunlight and moisture, while targeted pesticides protect crops against destructive insect infestations and fungal blights, dramatically curbing pre-harvest losses.

Furthermore, mechanisation has revolutionized operational efficiency. Modern tractors, automated seed drills, and combine harvesters enable vast land areas to be prepared, planted, and harvested in fractions of the time required by manual labour, making it feasible to cultivate previously unworkable or heavy soils. Complementing this, mechanised irrigation systems—including center-pivot sprinklers and drip irrigation—deliver precise water supplies regardless of seasonal rainfall variations, enabling multi-cropping cycles throughout the year.

Together, these integrated techniques have converted low-intensity subsistence farming into high-yielding commercial production, substantially enhancing food security worldwide.

b. Spider Diagram Outline: Problems Caused by Intensive Agricultural Techniques

Central Hub: Environmental & Economic Problems of Intensive Agriculture

  • Branch 1: Herbicides & Pesticides
    • Bioaccumulation and biomagnification up trophic levels, harming predatory birds and wildlife.
    • Eradication of non-target pollinating insects, notably honeybees and butterflies.
    • Runoff contaminating local aquifers, rivers, and drinking water sources with toxic chemical residues.
  • Branch 2: Fertilisers
    • Nutrient leaching (nitrates and phosphates) causing eutrophication in lakes and rivers.
    • Toxic algal blooms that block sunlight and deplete dissolved oxygen, creating aquatic "dead zones".
    • Emissions of nitrous oxide (N₂O), a greenhouse gas nearly 300 times more potent than CO₂.
  • Branch 3: Clearing Natural Areas (Deforestation)
    • Destruction of tropical rainforests and grasslands, causing severe habitat fragmentation and extinction.
    • Loss of vital carbon sinks, accelerating global warming.
    • Removal of deep tree roots exposes bare topsoil to immediate wind and torrential rain erosion.
  • Branch 4: Overuse/Misuse of Water Resources
    • Severe depletion of non-renewable subterranean aquifers (e.g. Ogallala Aquifer in the USA).
    • Drastic desiccation of rivers and lakes (e.g. the near-total shrinkage of the Aral Sea).
    • Soil salinisation in arid regions due to surface evaporation leaving toxic mineral crusts.
  • Branch 5: Mismanagement of Soils
    • Intensive monoculture exhausts organic nutrients, requiring ever-greater chemical supplements.
    • Heavy tractor machinery compacts soil structure, reducing aeration and rainwater infiltration.
    • Wind and sheet erosion strip fertile topsoil away, ultimately causing irreversible desertification.
