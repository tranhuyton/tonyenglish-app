Remote workers are mainly made up of the quaternary sectors. In small groups:

  1. a. Discuss possible reasons for the lower uptake in remote work for LICs (labelled as 'emerging economies' in the Source).
  2. b. Discuss how you think remote working options will change in LICs over the next ten years. Consider some of the challenges companies could face with remote work.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Reasons for lower uptake in remote work in emerging economies / LICs:

  • Dominance of physical/manual sectors: Emerging economies and LICs have a much higher percentage of their labor force employed in the primary sector (farming, mining) and secondary manufacturing (textiles, assembly). These jobs require physical presence and on-site operation.
  • Small quaternary sector: Knowledge-based information and data services represent only a minor proportion of total employment in LICs compared to advanced economies.
  • Technological and infrastructural deficits: Inconsistent electrical grids (frequent blackouts/load-shedding), limited access to high-speed broadband internet, and lower household computer and laptop ownership.
  • Housing conditions & informal economy: Crowded, multi-generational household living environments lack dedicated quiet office space; widespread informal economic activities (street selling, trade) cannot be performed remotely.

b. Future of remote work over the next ten years & challenges:

  • Anticipated growth: Remote work options will expand significantly due to global satellite internet (e.g. Starlink), undersea fiber cables, and smartphone ubiquity. Global TNCs will increasingly outsource digital services (software development, customer service, accounting, graphic design) to educated young professionals in LICs/MICs (e.g. India, Philippines, Kenya) to leverage lower labor costs.
  • Challenges for companies:
    • Cybersecurity & data privacy: Protecting proprietary corporate and client data across unencrypted home Wi-Fi networks.
    • Infrastructural disruptions: Managing lost productivity caused by sudden power outages or internet disruptions in developing countries.
    • Collaboration across time zones: Communication delays, language barriers, and difficulty maintaining team cohesion and oversight.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Identification and Justification:

  • Country A is a High-Income Country (HIC): It has a dominant tertiary service sector (~55–60%), a prominent quaternary knowledge sector (~10%), a moderate secondary sector (~25%), and a very small primary sector (<5%). This is characteristic of advanced, post-industrial consumer economies.
  • Country B is a Middle-Income Country (MIC): It has a substantial secondary manufacturing sector (~40%) alongside a growing tertiary sector (~40%), with a declining primary sector (~15%) and an emerging quaternary sector. This represents a rapidly industrializing emerging economy.
  • Country C is a Low-Income Country (LIC): It is overwhelmingly dominated by the primary sector (~70–75%), with a small tertiary sector (~20%), a tiny secondary sector (~5–10%), and no visible quaternary sector. Most of the labor force works in subsistence agriculture.

b. Country examples:

  • Country A (HIC): United Kingdom, Germany, or Japan. High technology, advanced healthcare, finance, and specialized services dominate employment; agriculture is highly mechanised and requires very few workers.
  • Country B (MIC): China, Vietnam, or Mexico. Major global manufacturing hubs where rapid industrialisation has shifted millions of workers from rural agriculture to factory assembly and city services.
  • Country C (LIC): Chad, Ethiopia, or Mali. Developing nations where the majority of citizens are subsistence farmers or pastoralists with limited domestic manufacturing.

c. Reasons why employment structures differ across development stages:

  • Agricultural mechanisation: As economies develop, modern machinery (tractors, combine harvesters) replaces manual farm labour, reducing primary sector employment and encouraging rural-to-urban migration.
  • Industrialisation and globalisation: Developing nations transition into secondary manufacturing by offering lower wages and land costs to TNCs. Over time, as domestic wages rise, heavy manufacturing moves abroad (deindustrialisation) and the country shifts towards high-value services.
  • Education and wealth: Higher national income enables mass university education, training workers for tertiary (finance, retail, tourism) and quaternary (scientific research, software, consulting) roles.

Table 9.2: Push and pull factors for moving manufacturing industry to LICs

Factor Push and pull factors for moving manufacturing industry to LICs
LandPull: Access to low-cost land to set up factories and offices.

Push: A reliable energy supply is crucial. Unreliable energy infrastructure and frequent blackouts make some LICs unsuitable.
TransportationPush: Ease of access to ports and transport networks is crucial for international trade. Underdeveloped road/rail/port infrastructure in LICs increases costs and delays.
MarketsPull: Ease of access to new markets with an emerging consumer base and growing population.
Push: TNCs need to consider cultural differences and local variations in consumer habits.
Political policiesPull: LIC governments encourage investment through tax exemptions, subsidies, or free trade zones. Favorable market entry policies encourage TNC expansion.
TechnologyPush: Access to reliable communication networks and specialised manufacturing technology can pose challenges in LICs where tech is harder to access.
CommunicationsPush: TNCs face challenges working remotely across language barriers and time zones; need translated equipment manuals and bilingual managers.
ContainerisationPush: Transport and distribution using containerisation is closely linked to port infrastructure, which can be underdeveloped in LICs.

In small groups, referring to Table 9.2, rank the top three factors you would consider when selecting a country for potential expansion. Justify your ranking.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

Top 3 Ranked Factors for an Expanding Clothing Brand:

  • Rank 1: Labour (Pull factor - low-cost, abundant workforce)

    Justification: Garment manufacturing is highly labor-intensive (cutting, sewing, tailoring). Wages constitute the highest recurring operational cost in clothing production. Finding a country with competitive wage rates and trainable textile workers directly determines whether the brand can price its clothes competitively in international retail markets.

  • Rank 2: Transportation & Containerisation (Infrastructure & Port access)

    Justification: The fashion industry relies on strict seasonal deadlines (spring/summer, autumn/winter collections). Reliable access to deepwater container ports and highway corridors ensures raw cotton/fabrics can be imported quickly and finished garments exported to global retail markets on schedule without costly warehouse delays.

  • Rank 3: Political Policies (Tax exemptions, Free Trade Zones, & Stability)

    Justification: Establishing operations inside an Export Processing Zone (EPZ) with tax holidays, tariff exemptions on imported fabrics, and zero export duties substantially reduces startup and running costs. Political stability guarantees that long-term factory capital investments are secure from civil unrest or arbitrary expropriation.

TASK 4: Study Source E

Study Source E: Newspaper article about the Suez Canal

Massive container ships blocks Egypt's Suez Canal

In what can only be described as a nightmare scenario for world trade, the Suez Canal has been blocked by a stranded giant container ship called 'Ever Given'. The blockage could create serious problems with deliveries of goods and fuel; it is used as the main route for the shipment of liquified natural gas and oil from the Middle East to Europe. Connecting the Red Sea to the Mediterranean, the Suez Canal is a passage of vital importance, enabling vessels to move between the Middle East, Asia, and Europe. On an average day around 50 vessels, which accounts for approximately 12% of world trade, pass along the canal. So, the big question now is how long the canal will remain impassable? The alternative route for vessels is the much longer journey around the southern tip of Africa.

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Factor discussed:

  • Transportation and Containerisation (international maritime shipping networks and supply chain logistics).

b. Major global markets linked:

  • The canal connects manufacturing export hubs in Asia (East, Southeast, and South Asia) and hydrocarbon producers in the Middle East directly to major consumer markets in Europe via the Red Sea and Mediterranean Sea.

c. Types of cargo transported:

  • Energy and fuel: Crude oil, refined petroleum products, and Liquified Natural Gas (LNG) tankers.
  • Containerised manufactured goods: Consumer electronics, cars and automotive components, clothing/textiles, footwear, toys, and household goods.
  • Bulk commodities: Grain, agricultural produce, chemical fertilizers, and industrial ores.

d. Impacts of the blockage on TNCs operating in LICs:

  • Supply chain bottlenecks & cancelled orders: Factories in LICs/MICs (e.g. Garment factories in Bangladesh or electronics assembly in Vietnam) could not deliver finished orders to European retailers, resulting in missed delivery penalties and cancelled contracts.
  • Factory shutdowns due to parts shortages: TNC factories in developing nations that rely on imported specialist components, dyes, or machinery parts from Europe were halted due to stockouts, leading to temporary worker layoffs.
  • Surging freight rates: Ships rerouted around the Cape of Good Hope added 10–14 days and enormous fuel expenses, causing shipping lines to impose hefty surcharges that eroded profit margins for producers.
  • Port congestion: Finished export containers overwhelmed warehouses and storage docks at export ports in developing nations while awaiting delayed container ships.
