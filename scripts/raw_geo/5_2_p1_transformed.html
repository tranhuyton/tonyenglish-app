5.2 The Impacts of Climate Change at Multiple Scales

Evaluating the multi-tiered geographical consequences of global warming: from planetary-scale thermal anomalies and sea level rise to regional cryospheric breakdown, delta inundation, desertification, and localized agricultural disruption.

1

Global Temperature Trends & IPCC Emissions Scenarios

Nghe cả phần

Climate change is no longer a theoretical projection; its signatures are definitively observable across global instruments. In its landmark Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) issued a severe "Code Red for Humanity", confirming that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land at an unprecedented rate.

Figure 5.9: Change in global surface temperatures between 1880 and 2020 relative to the long-term average (Hodder Education).

Key Trend Analysis: Between 1880 and 1940, global surface temperatures consistently dipped below the long-term baseline, with the coldest decade recorded around 1900–1910 (-0.4°C anomaly). From 1940 to 1980, temperatures fluctuated near the mean. Crucially, every year since 1980 has remained above average, surpassing +1.0°C by 2015 and projected to exceed +1.1°C to +1.2°C in the mid-2020s.

Sea Level Rise: Physical Drivers & Exposure Vulnerability

Nghe cả phần

Global Mean Sea Level (GMSL) has risen by 21–24 cm since 1880. Crucially, the rate of rise is accelerating dramatically: from 1.4 mm/year during most of the twentieth century to 3.6 mm/year between 2006 and 2015 (a 2.5× acceleration rate).

Figure 5.11: Global mean sea level rise, 1800–2100 (projected under low, intermediate, and extreme ice-sheet collapse scenarios) (Hodder Education).

Explanation of Projections: Sea levels are projected to rise by at least 0.3 m by 2100 under aggressive mitigation, but could exceed 1.0 m to 2.0 m if high greenhouse gas emissions trigger unstable Marine Ice Sheet Instability (MISI) in Antarctica.

A

Thermal Expansion (Steric Effect)

Water expands as it warms. As the upper layers of the ocean absorb over 90% of excess planetary heat, water molecules increase their kinetic energy and volume. This accounts for ~40% of observed sea level rise.

B

Melting Land Ice (Glaciers & Ice Sheets)

Meltwater from mountain glaciers (Alps, Andes, Himalayas) and polar continental ice sheets (Greenland and West Antarctica) flows into the oceans, adding immense physical mass to the global water budget (~55% of SLR).

C

Terrestrial Water Storage Depletion

Over-extraction of deep groundwater aquifers for agriculture and industry transfers liquid water from land reservoirs into rivers and ultimately into the ocean, contributing ~5% of annual sea level increases.

Human Exposure & Demographic Vulnerability

Currently, over 260 million people live on land threatened by sea level rise and coastal storm surges; by 2100, this figure will escalate past 400 million people. Over 60% of these vulnerable populations reside in tropical regions. Low-lying mega-deltas (e.g. Ganges-Brahmaputra, Mekong, Nile) and coastal megacities under 10 meters elevation face devastating combination hazards: high tides, storm surge inundation, and severe salinisation of drinking water aquifers and agricultural soils.

Systemic Human & Biospheric Cascades

Nghe cả phần

Climate impacts do not occur in isolation. Rising temperatures and altered precipitation regimes trigger complex multi-sector cascading disruptions across agriculture, hydrology, human health, and biodiversity:

🌾 Food Production & Agriculture

  • Southern Africa: Up to 30% of maize-growing areas and 50% of bean-growing lands are projected to become climatically unviable by 2050 due to persistent drought and heat stress.
  • Middle East & North Africa: Extreme heatwaves exceeding 50°C and rapid desertification are forcing rural abandonment across Egypt, Iraq, and Saudi Arabia.
  • Agro-climatic Poleward Shift: Temperate cereal belts in Canada and Russia may see extended growing seasons, but tropical breadbaskets face devastating net yield contractions.

🌊 Ocean Acidification & Marine Collapse

  • Chemical Mechanism: Oceans absorb ~25% of anthropogenic CO₂, reacting to form carbonic acid: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
  • pH Decline: Surface ocean pH has fallen from 8.2 to 8.1 (a 30% increase in hydrogen ion concentration).
  • Calcifier Failure: Dissolves calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) shells of pteropods, shellfish, and coral skeletons, disrupting entire marine trophic webs.

🩺 Human Health & Disease Vectors

  • Vector Expansion: Warmer temperatures and changing rainfall expand the geographic range and seasonal window for vector-borne diseases like malaria and dengue fever to higher altitudes and latitudes.
  • Heat Stress: Wet-bulb temperatures exceeding 35°C (human physiological survivability limit) are projected in South Asia and the Persian Gulf.
  • Urban Heat Island (UHI): Dense concrete and asphalt amplify extreme heat mortality among elderly and vulnerable urban populations.

📉 Economic Losses & Climate Migration

  • Global GDP Contraction: IPCC models estimate an annual 10%–23% loss in global economic output by 2100 under high-emissions warming pathways.
  • Infrastructure Destruction: Increased cyclone intensity, storm surges, and permafrost subsidence cause billions in structural damages.
  • Environmental Refugees: The UN estimates between 150 and 200 million people could become permanently displaced environmental migrants by 2050.
