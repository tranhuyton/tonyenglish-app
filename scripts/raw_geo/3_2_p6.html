TASK 1: Study Source A

Source A: Antarctic marine food web

Source A: Antarctic marine food web

Primary producers (photosynthesising phytoplankton) form the base of the food web in Source A. Primary consumers feed on primary producers (krill, zooplankton). In turn, secondary consumers and apex predators feed on those organisms.

  1. a. Explain why the smallest organism in the food web, phytoplankton, is so important to the food web.
  2. b. Describe how the introduction of a non-native species that eats penguin eggs would impact the food web.
  3. c. Identify the organism that consumes the most diverse diet in the food web.
  4. d. With a partner, discuss the food web. Are there any organisms that have a diet that surprises you?
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Importance of phytoplankton to the food web:

  • Primary Producers: Phytoplankton are microscopic single-celled organisms that photosynthesise, converting sunlight and upwelled oceanic nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, iron) into organic chemical energy (glucose and biomass).
  • Base of Trophic Pyramid: They are the fundamental foundation upon which all other Antarctic trophic levels depend. Antarctic krill and other herbivorous zooplankton feed directly on phytoplankton. Because almost all secondary and apex predators (whales, seals, penguins, squid, fish) feed on krill, any collapse in phytoplankton would cause the entire Southern Ocean food web to starve and collapse.

b. Impact of an invasive predator eating penguin eggs:

  • Penguin Decline: Heavy predation on eggs prevents successful reproductive recruitment, leading to a rapid collapse in penguin populations over subsequent generations.
  • Impact on Natural Predators: Apex predators that rely on penguins as a major food source—such as leopard seals, killer whales (orcas), and skuas—would face food shortages and would be forced to increase predation pressure on alternate prey (such as fish, crabeater seals, and squid).
  • Trophic Cascade on Krill: Fewer penguins would mean lower consumption of krill and small fish by penguins, temporarily leaving more krill available for competing filter feeders (like baleen whales), disrupting natural ecosystem equilibrium.

c. Organism consuming the most diverse diet:

  • Killer Whale (Orca) or Leopard Seal: Both apex predators feed across multiple trophic levels. Leopard seals consume krill, fish, squid, various penguin species, and young seals. Killer whales hunt fish, squid, penguins, multiple seal species, and even large baleen whales.

d. Surprising dietary features in the food web:

  • Baleen Whales (e.g. Blue Whales) surviving on tiny Krill: The largest animals on Earth (weighing up to 150–200 tonnes) feed almost exclusively on a tiny crustacean measuring only 5–6 cm. By feeding directly on primary consumers (krill), large whales bypass intermediate trophic levels, harnessing vast amounts of energy with minimal 90% trophic transfer loss.
  • Squid as a critical ecological hinge: Squid consume small zooplankton and fish, while simultaneously sustaining massive populations of wandering albatrosses, sperm whales, and elephant seals.
TASK 2: Study Source B

Source B: The Antarctic Treaty System and International Governance

Source B: Key pillars of the Antarctic Treaty System
  1. a. The Antarctic Treaty of 1959 put in place a rule that no single country can own or rule Antarctica. In small groups, discuss this. Explain in a short, written summary why you think this is important.
  2. b. Explain why countries would want to test military equipment in Antarctica.
  3. c. Explain why you think countries should have cooperative scientific research and share data collected in Antarctica.
  4. d. Draw a spider diagram with sustainability at the centre and add how each of the international treaties (IWC, MPAs, Polar Code, IAATO and Montreal Protocol) all contribute towards sustainability.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Importance of the rule that no country owns Antarctica (Article IV):

  • Prevention of Geopolitical Conflict: Before 1959, seven nations (UK, New Zealand, France, Norway, Australia, Chile, Argentina) had competing, overlapping territorial claims. By "freezing" all territorial claims, the treaty prevented armed conflict, border militarisation, and Cold War proxy tensions.
  • Preservation as a Global Commons: Designating Antarctica as a shared global commons dedicated solely to peace and science ensures equal scientific access and stops any single superpower from monopolising vast mineral, oil, or freshwater reserves.

b. Why countries would want to test military equipment in Antarctica:

  • Extreme Environmental Stress-Testing: Sub-zero temperatures (-40°C to -80°C), blizzards, sea ice, and metal-embrittling conditions provide an unmatched testing environment for cold-weather military vehicles, aircraft engines, satellite radar, missile guidance, and survival gear.
  • Secrecy & Isolation: Its remoteness historically offered covert isolation away from civilian scrutiny. Note: Article I of the Antarctic Treaty strictly bans all military bases, weapons testing, and troop maneuvers.

c. Why cooperative scientific research and data sharing is essential:

  • Global Scale of Climate Research: Antarctica is the Earth's primary climate archive (ice cores preserve 800,000-year records of atmospheric CO₂) and regulates global ocean thermohaline circulation. Issues like rising sea levels, ocean acidification, and ozone recovery impact every nation on Earth and cannot be solved by one country alone.
  • High Operational Logistics & Cost Pooling: Operating polar icebreakers, ski-planes, and year-round stations is enormously expensive. Pooling research vessels, scientific equipment, and sharing peer-reviewed data prevents costly duplication and accelerates scientific breakthroughs.

d. Spider Diagram: How international agreements contribute to sustainability:

Centre: Sustainability of Antarctica and the Southern Ocean
  • IWC (International Whaling Commission): Enforces a global moratorium on commercial whaling and designated the Southern Ocean Whale Sanctuary in 1994 to allow depleted whale populations to recover.
  • MPAs (Marine Protected Areas, e.g. Ross Sea MPA): Establishes legally enforceable no-fishing and limited-catch zones to conserve fragile benthic marine habitats, fish breeding stocks, and krill populations.
  • Polar Code (IMO): Mandates rigorous safety, hull-strengthening, and environmental regulations for ships navigating polar waters, completely banning the carriage and use of toxic heavy fuel oil (HFO) and untreated sewage discharge.
  • IAATO (International Association of Antarctica Tour Operators): Promotes sustainable eco-tourism by limiting shore landings to 100 passengers at a time, mandating biosecurity boot-washing to prevent invasive seeds, and maintaining strict minimum approach distances from wildlife.
  • Montreal Protocol (1987): Globally phased out ozone-depleting chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and halons, successfully curbing the expansion of the Antarctic ozone hole and protecting marine phytoplankton from harmful ultraviolet-B (UV-B) radiation.
TASK 3: Study Source C

Source C: Cartoon showing difference of opinions and priorities in the Antarctic ecosystem

Source C: Cartoon showing human vs wildlife perspective in Antarctica

In small groups, discuss the message that the cartoon in Source C is trying to deliver. Then write a 100 to 150-word summary of your interpretation of the cartoon's message. Use knowledge that you have gained throughout this topic to support your viewpoint.

💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

Deconstructing the Cartoon:

The cartoon depicts human visitors/scientists in red cold-weather parkas observing penguins and thinking: "Why do they come to such a random place to molt?" In contrast, the penguins look at a large industrial shipping container dropped onto the ice and think: "Why did they put a container in such a random place?"

Model Interpretation Summary (approx. 135 words):

The cartoon uses satire to highlight human anthropocentrism and the clash between natural ecosystems and human activity in Antarctica. The human observers view Antarctica through a self-centered lens, considering the penguins' presence in their own native habitat as "random." In reality, molting is an essential, ancestral survival cycle for penguins that requires weeks on land or fast ice.

Conversely, the penguins' viewpoint exposes the absurdity of human encroachment: a manufactured metal shipping container is what is truly foreign and intrusive in this wilderness. As Antarctic scientific research stations expand and tourism numbers climb past 100,000 visitors annually, human infrastructure, waste, and logistics increasingly encroach on pristine wildlife habitats. The cartoon reminds us that Antarctica belongs to its native fauna, and human activities must respect, rather than disrupt, natural ecological processes.
TASK 4: Study Source D

Source D: Data on the decrease in Antarctic ice sheet mass since 2002

Source D: Antarctic ice sheet mass loss (Gigatonnes) from 2002 to 2017
  1. a. Describe the trend in ice sheet mass between 2002 and 2017.
  2. b. Explain why the ice sheets are thinning in Antarctica.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Trend in ice sheet mass between 2002 and 2017:

  • Continuous Overall Decline: There is a steep, continuous, and significant net decrease in Antarctic ice sheet mass from 2002 to 2017.
  • Quantified Data: The baseline mass starts around 0 Gt in 2002 and plummets to approximately -1,900 to -2,000 Gigatonnes (Gt) by 2016/2017, representing an immense net loss of land-based ice.
  • Accelerating Rate: The decline accelerates over time. The slope between 2002 and 2008 shows a loss of ~500 Gt, whereas between 2008 and 2016 the rate steepens sharply, losing roughly 1,400 Gt in just 8 years.
  • Seasonal Fluctuations: The graph shows short-term sawtooth seasonal oscillations (winter snowfall mass gains vs summer melt/calving losses) superimposed on a strong long-term downward trajectory.

b. Why ice sheets are thinning in Antarctica:

  • Ocean Warming and Basal Melting: Warmer Circumpolar Deep Water (CDW) currents are circulating closer to the continental shelf, flowing beneath floating coastal ice shelves (such as Pine Island and Thwaites glaciers in West Antarctica). Melting ice from below thins the shelves, reducing the mechanical friction/back-pressure that buttresses land ice and causing inland ice sheets to flow rapidly into the sea.
  • Atmospheric Warming & Surface Melt: Anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions have driven rising atmospheric temperatures, particularly along the Antarctic Peninsula. Summer meltwater pools on ice surfaces (melt ponds), percolating into crevasses and causing rapid mechanical fracturing (hydrofracturing) and calving.
  • Albedo Feedback Loop: As reflective white ice melts, darker ocean water and exposed rock absorb more solar radiation, accelerating local warming and further thinning ice sheets.
