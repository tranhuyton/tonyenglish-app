1. The Internal Structure of the Earth

Nghe cả phần

The Earth is not a uniform solid sphere; instead, it consists of concentric spherical layers differentiated by chemical composition, density, temperature, and physical state (solid, plastic, or liquid). Understanding this layered architecture is essential to explaining how subterranean heat drives catastrophic surface hazards.

🌍 Interactive Diagram: Earth's Internal Layers & Convection Dynamics

Core Characteristics of Earth's Concentric Layers

  • The Crust: The outermost, rigid rock shell. Divided into two fundamental varieties:
    • Oceanic crust (5–10 km thick): Young (< 200 million years), dense (approx. 3.0 g/cm³), and composed predominantly of dark basaltic rocks. Continuously formed at constructive mid-ocean ridges and recycled into the mantle at subduction zones.
    • Continental crust (30–50 km, up to 70 km under mountains): Ancient (up to 4 billion years old), relatively buoyant/light (approx. 2.7 g/cm³), composed mainly of granitic silica-aluminum rich rock. Because of its lower density, continental crust cannot sink into the mantle and is permanent.
  • The Lithosphere & Asthenosphere:
    • Lithosphere (0–100 km): Comprises the crust and the rigid uppermost layer of the mantle. It is fractured into distinct puzzle-like slabs called tectonic plates.
    • Asthenosphere (100–300 km): The semi-molten, ductile layer directly beneath the lithosphere. Under extreme temperatures and pressures, solid silicate rocks behave plastically ("rheid"), flowing sluggishly like heated tar over millions of years.
  • The Mantle (depth to 2,900 km): Makes up 84% of Earth's volume. Rich in iron and magnesium silicates (peridotite). Temperatures range from 1,000°C near the crust to over 3,700°C near the core boundary. Convective circulations within this layer transfer heat from Earth's deep interior.
  • The Outer Core (2,900–5,100 km): Composed of liquid iron and nickel. High convective churning of this liquid metal, influenced by Earth's rotation (Coriolis effect), acts as a planetary dynamo generating Earth's geomagnetic field—which shields life from lethal solar cosmic rays.
  • The Inner Core (5,100–6,371 km): A solid sphere of nickel-iron alloy with a radius of approximately 1,220 km. Despite scorching temperatures reaching 5,500°C–6,000°C (hotter than the surface of the Sun), the incomprehensible pressure (over 3.6 million atmospheres) forces the atoms into an immoveable solid crystalline lattice ("pressure freezing").

2. Plate Tectonics and Driving Mechanisms

Nghe cả phần

The theory of plate tectonics states that Earth's rigid outer shell (lithosphere) is fractured into seven colossal major plates and several minor micro-plates that float and slide across the ductile asthenosphere beneath. These plates move at average rates between 2 to 10 cm per year (roughly equivalent to human fingernail growth).

The Triad of Plate Movement Drivers

  1. Mantle Convection Currents: Heat released by primordial accretion and the ongoing radioactive decay of unstable isotopes (uranium-235, thorium-232, potassium-40) deep in the mantle causes hot plumes to become less dense and rise toward the lithosphere. At the base of the crust, they spread laterally, cool, increase in density, and sink back down, acting like a slow planetary conveyor belt.
  2. Slab Pull (Primary Driving Force): As newly created oceanic crust moves away from mid-ocean ridges, it cools, accumulates sediment, and becomes significantly denser than the underlying asthenosphere. When it descends into a deep subduction trench, gravitational force pulls the rest of the trailing plate downward into the mantle behind it. Geophysicists consider slab pull the most powerful force driving plate motions today.
  3. Ridge Push (Gravitational Sliding): Magma rising at mid-ocean ridges produces hot, elevated submarine mountain chains standing 2 to 3 km above the abyssal ocean plain. Gravity causes the elevated oceanic lithosphere to slide downslope away from the ridge crest, exerting a lateral pushing force upon the plate.

Virtually all global seismicity and volcanism is focused along plate margins where plates collide, pull apart, or grind past one another. The Cambridge IGCSE syllabus identifies four fundamental plate boundaries:

Major Seismic and Volcanic Belts

  • The Pacific "Ring of Fire": A massive 40,000-km horseshoe-shaped belt circling the basin of the Pacific Ocean. Driven by subduction zones beneath South America (Andes), Central America, North America (Cascades/Alaska), the Aleutians, Japan, the Philippines, Indonesia, and New Zealand. It contains 75% of the world's active volcanoes and generates over 90% of all global earthquakes.
  • Mid-Ocean Ridges (e.g., Mid-Atlantic Ridge): A submarine chain stretching over 65,000 km across the world's ocean floors where divergence generates constant gentle volcanic eruptions and shallow seismic activity.
  • The Alpine-Himalayan Belt: A vast collision zone running from the Mediterranean through the Middle East, Iran, northern India, and Myanmar, characterized by immense tectonic compression and catastrophic earthquakes, but few volcanoes except in the Mediterranean.
  • Mantle Plumes and Volcanic Hotspots:
    • An isolated, abnormally hot column of upwelling mantle rock located far from plate boundaries.
    • As an oceanic plate glides slowly over a stationary hotspot, molten basalt punches through the crust, generating a linear chain of progressively older volcanic islands.
    • Classic Example: The Hawaiian Island chain. The youngest active volcano (Kilauea/Mauna Loa) sits directly over the hotspot at the southeast end, while older eroded islands (Maui, Oahu, Kauai) trail northwestwards along the Pacific Plate's drift vector.
