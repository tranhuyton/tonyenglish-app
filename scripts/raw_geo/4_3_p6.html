TASK 1: Study Sources A, B, and C
Source A: The Moment Magnitude Scale
Source B: The Richter scale
Source C: The Modified Mercalli intensity scale
  1. At what strength on the Modified Mercalli intensity scale is the earthquake felt by all, and may result in the movement of some heavy furniture or cause plaster to fall off the wall?
  2. With reference to Source A, describe the relationship of the number of earthquakes per year (worldwide) with the magnitude of the earthquake.
  3. Compare the Modified Mercalli intensity scale with the Moment Magnitude Scale and Richter scale.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Strength on Modified Mercalli scale: felt by all, may move heavy furniture or cause plaster to fall:

  • This description corresponds to intensity level VI (6) on the Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale.
  • At this level: the earthquake is felt by everyone, causes alarm, people run outdoors. Furniture moves, heavy objects shift, plaster may crack and fall, slight damage to poorly built structures.

b. Relationship between number of earthquakes per year and magnitude (with reference to Source A — Moment Magnitude Scale):

  • There is a strong inverse (negative) relationship between magnitude and frequency.
  • Low-magnitude earthquakes (e.g., M1–2) occur millions of times per year worldwide and are rarely felt.
  • Moderate earthquakes (M5–6) occur thousands of times per year and can cause local damage.
  • High-magnitude "great" earthquakes (M8+) occur very rarely — only once every few years globally — and are capable of catastrophic destruction over a wide area.
  • In general, for every increase of 1 on the Moment Magnitude Scale, the number of earthquakes decreases by approximately 10 times (a logarithmic relationship).

c. Comparison of Modified Mercalli scale vs. Moment Magnitude Scale (MMS) and Richter Scale:

  • What is being measured: The MMS and Richter scale measure the energy released at the source (focus). The Modified Mercalli scale measures the intensity of shaking experienced at a specific location on the surface.
  • Objectivity: The MMS and Richter scale produce an objective number calculated from seismograph readings. The Mercalli scale is more subjective — it relies on reports of how people felt the shaking and the extent of observed damage.
  • Spatial variation: An earthquake has one MMS/Richter magnitude value, but Mercalli intensity varies by location — places closer to the epicentre experience higher Mercalli values than distant places.
  • Scale: The Richter/MMS use a logarithmic scale (M1–M9+). The Mercalli scale uses Roman numerals I–XII describing effects ranging from imperceptible (I) to total destruction (XII).
  • Usefulness: The Mercalli scale is more useful for assessing damage and comparing impacts on communities. The MMS/Richter scale is more useful for scientific comparison of earthquake energy between different events.
TASK 2
  1. Explain how the speed and frequency of tectonic hazards can result in an increase or decrease in potential impacts.
  2. Consider the positive and negative impacts of ash fall from a volcano. List a positive and a negative impact for the three categories: 'economic', 'social', and 'environmental'.
  3. In pairs, discuss why two earthquakes of the same size in different locations can cause death in one location, but not in the other.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. How speed and frequency of tectonic hazards affect potential impacts:

  • Speed of onset: Earthquakes strike with virtually no warning (seconds), allowing little time for evacuation, so impacts tend to be high. Volcanic eruptions often develop over days or weeks (rising seismic activity, ground deformation), giving authorities more time to evacuate, potentially reducing deaths.
  • Frequency: In areas where tectonic hazards occur frequently (e.g., Japan, Philippines), populations, governments, and infrastructure become better prepared through experience — strict building codes, well-practised evacuation drills, effective early warning systems. This can significantly reduce impacts.
  • In areas of infrequent hazards: Preparedness is usually lower. When a rare major event does occur, the social and economic impacts tend to be far greater because communities are caught off guard and infrastructure is not designed to cope.

b. Positive and negative impacts of ash fall from a volcano:

Category Positive Impact Negative Impact
Economic Long-term, volcanic ash enriches soil with minerals (phosphorus, potassium), boosting agricultural yields and supporting profitable farming. Short-term crop destruction; damage to buildings, vehicles, machinery; closure of airports and roads disrupts trade and tourism.
Social Community solidarity during clean-up and recovery; some volcanic regions attract geotourism. Respiratory health problems (silicosis), eye irritation; collapse of heavy ash-laden roofs causing deaths and injuries; mass evacuation disrupts communities.
Environmental Ash deposits create nutrient-rich soils over decades; fine particles in the stratosphere can temporarily cool global temperatures (volcanic winter effect). Smothers vegetation and ecosystems; acidic ash contaminates rivers, lakes, and drinking water; kills livestock and wildlife.

c. Why two earthquakes of the same magnitude can cause death in one location but not the other:

  • Level of development (MEDCs vs. LEDCs): Wealthier countries (MEDCs) invest heavily in earthquake-resistant building codes, infrastructure, emergency services, and community preparedness. A M6.5 earthquake in Japan may cause very few deaths, while the same magnitude in a poorer country could kill thousands.
  • Population density: A high-magnitude earthquake in a remote, sparsely populated area may cause no deaths; the same quake directly beneath a dense urban area can be catastrophic.
  • Local geology: Soft sedimentary soils and alluvial plains amplify seismic shaking (soil amplification). Bedrock areas experience less severe shaking.
  • Focal depth: A shallow-focus earthquake causes more intense surface shaking than a deep-focus one of the same magnitude.
  • Time of day: An earthquake at night may catch people asleep in buildings; daytime may mean fewer people at home in residential areas.
  • Secondary hazards: In one location, the quake may trigger a tsunami, landslides, or fires, multiplying the death toll; in another location, these secondary hazards may be absent.
TASK 3: Study Source D
During a visit to Mount Agung, Indonesian photographer Putu Sayoga said: 'I saw people live so close to the volcano. I was afraid at the time, but the people I met really did not seem to care'. Locals laughed and said, 'Oh yeah, there is a fire over there'. Putu suggests that because the villagers live so close to the volcano, they have a different understanding of it. The Indonesian government has installed early warning systems to evacuate the population if necessary. When asked about the possibility of evacuation, Putu describes locals saying 'that they do not want to live in other villages, because they still have livestock and farms in the villages that they come from'.
Source D: Living in the shadow of a volcano
  1. Why do you think the people who live on the slopes of the volcano are less afraid than visitors?
  2. The Indonesian government has put steps in place to evacuate an area if it is identified as becoming dangerous. Do you think that all the villagers are happy to leave their homes? Explain your answer.
  3. List two volcanic hazards that the villagers may face.
  4. Explain why living on or close to a volcano is beneficial to the farmers.
💡 View Suggested Answers

Teacher's Suggested Answer & Guidelines

a. Why people living on the slopes of a volcano are less afraid than visitors:

  • Familiarity and normalisation: Residents have lived alongside the volcano for generations. What appears dramatic and terrifying to an outsider is simply the familiar environment they have grown up in. They have learned to interpret volcanic activity as part of daily life.
  • Cultural and spiritual connections: In many communities (as in Bali/Indonesia), volcanoes hold deep spiritual and religious significance. This cultural relationship can reduce fear and increase acceptance of risk.
  • Economic dependency: As Putu explains, locals depend on the volcano's fertile soils for livestock and farming. Leaving would mean losing their entire livelihood, which creates a strong incentive to remain and rationalise the risk.

b. Are all villagers happy to evacuate their homes?

  • No — not all villagers are happy to leave. The source makes this clear: when asked about evacuation, locals said they do not want to live in other villages because they still have livestock and farms in their home villages.
  • For subsistence farmers, leaving means their animals may die, their crops fail, and their economic survival is threatened.
  • Long-term evacuations can break up close-knit communities, cause psychological distress, and result in loss of cultural identity tied to place.
  • However, some villagers — especially those with children or elderly relatives — may welcome evacuation as a lifesaving measure.

c. Two volcanic hazards the villagers may face:

  • 1. Pyroclastic flows: Fast-moving (up to 700 km/h), extremely hot (up to 1,000°C) clouds of gas, ash, and rock fragments that descend volcano slopes — instantly fatal to anyone in their path and able to travel several kilometres from the summit.
  • 2. Ash fall: Volcanic ash falls over a wide area, damaging crops and roofs, contaminating water supplies, causing respiratory health problems, and making roads impassable. Heavy ash can collapse roofs and cause structural damage.
  • Other acceptable answers: lava flows, lahars (volcanic mudflows), volcanic gases (SO₂), volcanic bombs.

d. Why living on or close to a volcano is beneficial to farmers:

  • Volcanic eruptions deposit layers of nutrient-rich ash and minerals (including phosphorus, potassium, and silica) onto the surrounding land over time.
  • This creates exceptionally fertile volcanic soils (Andosols) that support high agricultural productivity — ideal for growing crops such as rice, coffee, and vegetables.
  • The fertile soil allows farmers to grow abundant harvests with relatively little fertiliser input, supporting both subsistence and commercial agriculture.
  • The availability of geothermal heat in some volcanic regions can also extend growing seasons or provide energy for farming operations.
